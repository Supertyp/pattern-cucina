  - pattern: buying-hard-cheeses
    note: Buy a wedge of Pecorino Romano DOP cut from the wheel, not a tub of grated cheese. Some cooks soften it with a little Parmigiano Reggiano, but all-pecorino is the Roman standard.
  - pattern: grating-hard-cheese
    note: Grate the pecorino on your finest rasp straight from the fridge, just before you start cooking, so it's light and fluffy and melts the moment it meets warm water.
  - pattern: measuring-by-weight
    note: Weigh 200 g of pasta and 80 to 100 g of grated pecorino. Rasp-grated cheese is so fluffy that a cup measure would give you far too little.
  - pattern: salting-pasta-water
    note: Drop to about 6 to 7 g of salt per litre. The pecorino is very salty and the cooking water ends up in the sauce, so the full 10 g would make the dish too salty.
  - pattern: cooking-pasta-al-dente
    note: Spaghetti or the Roman tonnarelli both work. Lift it out a good 2 minutes before it's al dente, because it finishes cooking in the pepper and water before the cheese goes in.
  - pattern: saving-pasta-water
    note: This is the dish where cooking in less water pays off. Use about 1.5 litres in a wide pan and stir often, so the water turns cloudy and starchy, and keep it all until the dish is done.
  - pattern: emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces
    note: Toast 1 to 2 teaspoons of coarsely cracked black pepper in a dry pan, then add a ladle of pasta water. Mix the pecorino with a few spoonfuls of warm, not boiling, water into a thick cream before it meets the pasta.
  - pattern: finishing-pasta-in-the-pan
    note: Toss the pasta in the peppery water over the heat until it's nearly al dente and the liquid has thickened, then take the pan off and wait 20 to 30 seconds before adding the cheese cream and tossing hard.
  - pattern: setting-the-italian-table
    note: Warm the bowls well and have everyone seated before you start tossing. A cheese sauce thickens within a minute or two, so it can't wait for anyone, and there's no need for a cheese bowl on the table.
  - pattern: eating-pasta
    note: Eat it at once, twirling a few strands at a time. If the last forkfuls turn thick and sticky, that's the cheese cooling, a reminder to serve smaller portions straight from the pan.
  - pattern: pairing-by-region
    note: Look to Lazio. A crisp white such as Frascati suits the salty cheese and pepper well, and a light red from the region such as Cesanese works if you prefer red.

## About the dish

Cacio e pepe (*cacio* is an old word for cheese) is one of Rome's classic pastas, often said to come from shepherds who carried dried pasta, pecorino and pepper because they kept well. When it's right, the pasta is coated in a pale, creamy sauce that flows slightly when you tilt the bowl, with no strings or lumps of cheese, and the pepper is warm and fragrant. Cooks argue about a spoonful of butter or oil, mixing in Parmigiano, and whether to make a cheese paste first or work the cheese straight into the pan. Cream has no place in the Roman version.

## What you need

- 200 g dried spaghetti or tonnarelli
- 80 to 100 g Pecorino Romano, from a wedge
- 1 to 2 teaspoons whole black peppercorns
- About 10 g salt for the pasta water
- Equipment: a fine rasp grater, a mortar or heavy pan for cracking the pepper, a wide frying pan, tongs and a small mixing bowl

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Stringy clumps of cheese.** The pan was too hot when the cheese went in. Take it off the heat and wait (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../../patterns/emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)).
- **A grainy sauce that never comes together.** The cheese was pre-grated or coarse (see [Grating hard cheese](../../patterns/grating-hard-cheese/)).
- **Too salty.** The water was salted as usual on top of the pecorino (see [Salting the pasta water](../../patterns/salting-pasta-water/)).
