  - pattern: cold-fermenting-dough
    note: Give the dough an hour at room temperature, then refrigerate it for about 24 hours, which gives most of the flavour benefit. Take it out 2 to 3 hours before it goes into the tray.
  - pattern: storing-olive-oil
    note: You'll use about 150 ml of olive oil, much of it barely cooked, so a stale bottle tastes of crayon all through the bread. A mild, fresh Ligurian oil (Riviera Ligure DOP) is traditional; whatever you use, keep it dark and cool.

    note: Press the dough into a 30 by 40 cm tray oiled with 50 ml oil, in stages. After an hour's rise, dimple it to the tray and pour over a brine of 75 ml water, 50 ml oil and 6 g salt. Rest 30 to 45 minutes, then bake at 230 °C (210 °C fan-forced) for 18 to 22 minutes.
  - pattern: using-day-old-bread
    note: Focaccia is best within hours; in Genoa it's even dipped in morning coffee. Revive leftovers in a hot oven for 5 minutes. Freeze what you won't eat that day, and toast stale pieces gently into crumbs, as they're oily and burn faster than plain bread.

## About the dish

Focaccia genovese, *fugassa* in Genoese dialect, is eaten across Liguria at any time of day. It's thin, usually 1.5 to 2 cm, with a golden, almost fried underside, a soft and slightly chewy crumb, and a top that stays pale and moist in the deep dimples where oil and brine pool, with crisper ridges between. It should taste of good oil and salt, not of yeast. Bakers argue about the details: how much oil goes into the dough, whether to add a little malt or sugar, and how thin to press it. Today many people know the taller, airier focaccia made from a very wet dough, which is delicious but a different bread from the Genoese one.

## What you need

- 500 g medium-strong flour (11 to 12.5 % protein)
- 315 g water
- 12 g fine salt
- 2 g instant dried yeast (or 6 g fresh yeast)
- 150 ml extra virgin olive oil in all (25 ml in the dough, 50 ml for the tray, 50 ml for the brine, a little for your fingers)
- For the brine, 75 ml lukewarm water and 6 g fine salt
- A pinch of coarse salt for the top (optional)
- Equipment: a 30 by 40 cm metal baking tray (not glass), a scale reading to 0.1 g, a lidded container and a wire rack

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Dense and bready.** The dough was baked before it had relaxed and risen, or the brine was skimped. Press it into the tray in stages and give it its full rise, as in [Baking focaccia in a tray](../../patterns/baking-focaccia/).
- **Shallow dimples that disappear in the oven.** Fingertips didn't go right down to the tray, or the dough was too wet to hold them. Keep the firmer hydration from [Choosing dough hydration](../../patterns/dough-hydration/) and press firmly.
- **A pale, soft or soggy base.** Too little oil in the tray, too high a shelf, or it was left to steam in the tin. See [Baking focaccia in a tray](../../patterns/baking-focaccia/) and cool it on a rack.
- **A stale, waxy taste.** The oil was old. See [Storing olive oil](../../patterns/storing-olive-oil/).
