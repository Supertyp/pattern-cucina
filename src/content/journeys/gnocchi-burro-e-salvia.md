  - pattern: measuring-by-weight
    note: Weigh the riced potato before adding any flour, because potatoes vary. Start with 200 g flour per kilo of riced potato and keep the rest of the flour to hand, adding it only if the test gnocco falls apart.
  - pattern: potato-gnocchi
    note: Bake about 1.1 kg of floury potatoes whole, which gives roughly 1 kg of riced flesh. Make ropes about 2 cm thick, cut 2 cm pillows, and roll them over a fork so the ridges catch the butter. Leave out the egg if your potatoes are dry.
  - pattern: keeping-fresh-herbs
    note: You need 16 to 20 sage leaves, so buy a fresh, firm bunch and keep it wrapped in barely damp paper in the fridge. Pick small to medium leaves, which crisp better, and make sure they're completely dry before they go into the hot butter or they'll spit.
  - pattern: portioning-pasta
    note: Gnocchi aren't covered by the dried pasta weights. A kilo of riced potato with its flour makes about 1.2 kg of gnocchi, enough for four generous first courses. Butter sauce is rich, so 80 to 100 g of butter is plenty for four.
  - pattern: salting-pasta-water
    note: Use the full 10 g of salt per litre, because the butter sauce is plain. Bring 4 to 5 litres to the boil in a wide pot, then lower it to a gentle boil so the gnocchi aren't battered as they cook.
  - pattern: finishing-pasta-in-the-pan
    note: This is the exception to tossing hard. Melt 90 g butter with the sage until it foams and smells nutty, add 2 or 3 tablespoons of the cooking water, then lift the gnocchi in with a slotted spoon about 30 seconds after they float. Swirl the pan gently to coat them, rather than stirring.
  - pattern: grating-hard-cheese
    note: Grate about 50 g of Parmigiano Reggiano on a fine rasp, so it melts into the butter on contact. Scatter half over in the pan off the heat and bring the rest to the table.

## About the dish

Gnocchi di patate are eaten all over northern Italy, and butter and sage is their simplest and most loved dressing. They became common once the potato spread through northern Italian kitchens, and Verona has a famous Carnival tradition built around them. Well-made gnocchi are light and tender, almost pillowy, but they hold their shape on the fork and taste of potato, not flour. The butter should be golden and nutty, never black, and the sage crisp and fragrant. Whether egg belongs in the dough is a genuine argument: many cooks leave it out for the lightest result, while others add a yolk for safety. Both are traditional somewhere.

## What you need

- About 1.1 kg floury potatoes (such as Russet, King Edward or Désirée), of similar size
- 200 to 250 g 00 or plain flour, plus extra for the bench
- 1 egg yolk (optional)
- Salt
- 90 g butter
- 16 to 20 sage leaves
- 50 g Parmigiano Reggiano, in a piece
- Equipment: a potato ricer, a bench scraper, a fork or gnocchi board, a wide pot, a slotted spoon and a large frying pan

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Heavy, gummy or chewy gnocchi.** Too much flour, waxy potatoes or kneading like pasta dough. Fold gently and test one first, as in [Potato gnocchi](../../patterns/potato-gnocchi/).
- **Gnocchi that dissolve in the water.** Wet, boiled potatoes or a hard rolling boil. Bake the potatoes, let the steam escape, and keep the water at a gentle boil, as in [Salting the pasta water](../../patterns/salting-pasta-water/).
- **Burnt, bitter butter.** The pan was too hot or the sage was wet. Take it off the heat as soon as it smells nutty and add the cooking water, as in [Finishing pasta in the pan](../../patterns/finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/).
- **Sticky dough that won't shape.** It sat too long. Shape and cook within an hour or two, or freeze them raw on a tray.
