## About the dish

Panna cotta means "cooked cream" and is usually credited to Piedmont, where cream, milk and sugar are set with gelatine and flavoured with vanilla. When it's right, it's pure white, smells of vanilla and milk, and trembles as a whole when you nudge the plate, then melts in the mouth with no rubbery bounce. Serving it in glasses gives the softest set; turning it out looks smarter but needs more gelatine. Toppings are a matter of taste: caramel is common in Piedmont, fruit sauces elsewhere. Some cooks use only cream, others add milk for a lighter result.

## What you need

- 400 ml pouring cream (35 % fat)
- 100 ml full-cream milk
- 60 to 70 g caster sugar
- 1 vanilla bean, split, or 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
- 5 g gelatine for glasses, or 7.5 g to turn out (leaves or powder)
- 200 to 300 g seasonal fruit, to serve
- Neutral oil, for the moulds if turning out
- About 20 g ground coffee for a 4-cup moka pot
- Equipment: digital scale, saucepan, fine sieve, jug, four 125 ml glasses or moulds, a 4-cup moka pot

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Rubbery and bouncy.** Too much gelatine, often from counting sheets instead of weighing (see [Setting a cream with gelatine](../../patterns/setting-panna-cotta/)).
- **It never set.** The gelatine was boiled, or raw pineapple or kiwi fruit went into the cream (see [Shopping by season](../../patterns/shopping-by-season/) for what's ripe, and the setting pattern for the enzyme warning).
- **Bitter, burnt coffee.** The moka was left on after it started to gurgle, or the heat was too high (see [Making coffee in a moka pot](../../patterns/making-moka-pot-coffee/)).
