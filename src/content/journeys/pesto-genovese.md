    note: Buy about 50 g of young, small-leaved basil on the day, keep it in a glass of water on the bench, and wash and dry it completely just before you start. Any water left on the leaves thins and darkens the pesto.
  - pattern: preparing-garlic
    note: Use one small clove, or half a large one, and remove any green germ. The garlic stays raw, so it's at its strongest here, and Ligurian cooks use very little.
  - pattern: grating-hard-cheese
    note: Grate 30 g of Parmigiano Reggiano and 15 to 20 g of aged pecorino on the small holes of a box grater. The denser grains blend into the paste evenly and weigh more consistently than fluffy rasp-grated cheese.
  - pattern: making-pesto
    note: In a mortar, grind the garlic with a few grains of coarse salt, then 1 to 2 tablespoons of pine nuts, then the basil, then the cheeses, and finally 80 to 100 ml of mild extra virgin olive oil. Allow about 20 minutes.
  - pattern: matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce
    note: Trofie, short twisted Ligurian pasta, hold pesto in their curls. Trenette, flat strands like linguine, are the other classic. Avoid big tubes, which leave pesto stranded on the plate.
  - pattern: salting-pasta-water
    note: Use 4 litres of water and about 8 g of salt per litre, a little under the usual amount. The pesto brings cheese and salt, but the potatoes and beans cook in this water and need seasoning too.
  - pattern: cooking-pasta-al-dente
    note: Add 200 g of potato in 1.5 cm cubes a few minutes before the pasta, and 150 g of green beans for the last 6 to 8 minutes, so the beans are tender and the potato is soft enough to break slightly.
  - pattern: saving-pasta-water
    note: Scoop out a mug before draining. It's extra starchy from the potato, which helps the pesto turn creamy rather than oily, and you'll need 2 or 3 tablespoons, plus a little more if it looks thick.
  - pattern: finishing-pasta-in-the-pan
    note: The exception to the rule is that pesto never goes on the heat. Loosen it with the hot water in a warm serving bowl, then tip in the drained pasta, potato and beans and toss until glossy.
  - pattern: pairing-by-region
    note: Stay in Liguria with a young, crisp Vermentino or Pigato served well chilled. Its herbal, slightly salty character suits basil and pine nuts.

## About the dish

Pesto alla genovese comes from Genoa and the Ligurian coast, where it's traditionally ground in a marble mortar with a wooden pestle. Serving it with trofie or trenette, potato and green beans is a classic Genoese way. When it's right, the pesto is bright green and smells sweet rather than grassy, the garlic is a background note, and the potato melts a little into the sauce so it coats the pasta creamily without any slick of oil. Cooks argue about mortar or blender, how much garlic, and whether the potatoes and beans belong in every version.

## What you need

- 400 g dried trofie or trenette
- 200 g waxy potato, peeled and cut into 1.5 cm cubes
- 150 g green beans, topped and cut into 3 cm lengths
- About 50 g young basil leaves
- 1 small garlic clove
- 1 to 2 tablespoons pine nuts
- 30 g Parmigiano Reggiano and 15 to 20 g aged pecorino
- 80 to 100 ml mild extra virgin olive oil, such as a Ligurian one
- Coarse salt (a few grains for the pesto, about 32 g for the water)
- Equipment: a marble or stone mortar and wooden pestle (or a blender with a chilled jug), a box grater, a large pot and a warmed serving bowl

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Dark, bitter pesto.** The basil was pounded or blended hot. Grind gently, or pulse briefly in a cold jug (see [Making pesto](../../patterns/making-pesto/)).
- **Oily, separated sauce.** The pesto went into a hot pan, or there was too little starchy water (see [Saving the pasta water](../../patterns/saving-pasta-water/)).
- **Hard potato or grey beans.** They went into the pot at the wrong time, so allow for each one (see [Cooking pasta al dente](../../patterns/cooking-pasta-al-dente/)).
