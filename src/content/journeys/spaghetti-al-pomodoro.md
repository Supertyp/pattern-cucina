  - pattern: choosing-tomatoes
    note: The tomato is the whole dish, so this is the place for your best tin. In summer use about 1 kg of ripe plum tomatoes; the rest of the year, two 400 g tins of whole peeled tomatoes, San Marzano DOP if you want to treat yourself.
  - pattern: peeling-and-seeding-tomatoes
    note: Only for fresh tomatoes. Blanch and peel them for a silky sauce, but keep the seeds and jelly, which add savoury flavour and cook down. Skip this step entirely with tinned pelati.
  - pattern: keeping-fresh-herbs
    note: Buy a small bunch of basil and keep it in a glass of water on the bench, not in the fridge. Tear the leaves only as they go into the sauce, so they stay green and fragrant.
  - pattern: portioning-pasta
    note: Use 400 g of spaghetti as a main for four, or 320 g as a primo. You want about 400 to 500 ml of finished sauce, which is what two tins reduce to.
  - pattern: quick-tomato-sauce
    note: Warm 2 lightly crushed garlic cloves in about 80 ml of oil, lift them out when pale gold, then add the hand-crushed tomatoes. Simmer uncovered for 20 to 30 minutes, until thick and the oil beads orange at the edges.
  - pattern: salting-pasta-water
    note: Use 4 litres of water and the full 40 g of salt. A plain tomato sauce brings little salt of its own, so the pasta needs seasoning from the water.
  - pattern: cooking-pasta-al-dente
    note: Drop the pasta when the sauce has about 10 minutes to go, and lift it out 1 to 2 minutes before it's al dente, since it finishes in the sauce.
  - pattern: matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce
    note: A smooth tomato sauce is exactly what long strands carry well. Linguine or vermicelli are fine swaps from the same family; avoid fine capellini, which turn soft and clump under a sauce this thick.
  - pattern: finishing-pasta-in-the-pan
    note: A tomato sauce is already wet, so you need only 1 or 2 tablespoons of pasta water per portion. Toss for a minute over high heat until every strand is red and glossy, then add the basil and a thread of raw oil off the heat.
  - pattern: seasoning-and-tasting
    note: Taste the sauce once it has reduced and again with the pasta in it. If it's flat, add salt; if it's still sharp, it needed a few more minutes of simmering, which is worth remembering for next time.
  - pattern: grating-hard-cheese
    note: Grate about 30 to 40 g of Parmigiano Reggiano, or pecorino if you like it sharper, on a fine rasp just before serving and pass it at the table, so everyone adds their own.
  - pattern: wine-with-tomato-dishes
    note: A quick, bright sauce wants a light, high-acid red served slightly cool, such as a Barbera or a Campanian Piedirosso, or a crisp white such as Falanghina on a hot day.

## About the dish

Spaghetti al pomodoro is the simplest and most Neapolitan of pasta dishes, built on the tomatoes grown around Vesuvius. When it's right, the sauce is thick enough to coat each strand without pooling, deep red and glossy with oil, sweet but still bright, and scented with basil rather than tasting of it. Cooks disagree about garlic or onion as the base, butter or oil, and whether a pinch of sugar is allowed. This version uses garlic and oil, as is common in Naples.

## What you need

- 400 g dried spaghetti
- Two 400 g tins whole peeled tomatoes, or about 1 kg ripe plum tomatoes in summer
- About 80 ml extra virgin olive oil, plus a little to finish
- 2 garlic cloves
- A small bunch of basil
- 30 to 40 g Parmigiano Reggiano or pecorino, to serve
- Salt (40 g for the pasta water)
- Equipment: a large pot, a wide frying pan or sauté pan (28 to 30 cm) and tongs

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **Thin, watery sauce that runs off the pasta.** It wasn't reduced long enough. Wait for the oil to bead on the surface (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../../patterns/quick-tomato-sauce/)).
- **Sour, sharp taste.** Pale out-of-season tomatoes or a poor tin (see [Choosing fresh and tinned tomatoes](../../patterns/choosing-tomatoes/)).
- **Sauce sitting on top of naked pasta.** The pasta was drained and sauced in the bowl instead of finished together (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../../patterns/finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)).
