steps:
  - pattern: mise-en-place
    note: The ragù is slow, so prepare only what starts it and make the pasta while it simmers. Save full *mise en place* for the final ten minutes, when the pasta cooks in two.
  - pattern: dicing-soffritto-vegetables
    note: Dice the onion, carrot and celery fine, about 2 to 3 mm, so they melt into the sauce. The Bologna recipe uses roughly equal weights of the three rather than double the onion, about 50 g each here.
  - pattern: building-a-soffritto
    note: Start by cooking the finely chopped pancetta in a little butter or oil until its fat runs, then add the vegetables and cook them gently for 15 to 20 minutes until soft and sweet, with no more than a hint of gold.
  - pattern: browning-meat
    note: Add the mince in two batches, pressed flat and left to colour before breaking it up. Some Bolognese families only cook it until it loses its raw colour; either way, it should sizzle and fry, not sit grey in its juices.
  - pattern: deglazing-with-wine
    note: Use about 125 ml dry white wine, as the Bologna tradition does, and scrape up the fond as it bubbles. Wait until the boozy smell has gone and only a glaze is left before the tomato goes in.
  - pattern: slow-cooked-ragu
    note: Stir in about 200 g passata (or 2 tablespoons tomato paste loosened with stock), cover partly and keep it at a lazy plop for 2 to 3 hours, adding stock when it looks dry. Add about 125 ml milk, at the start or in the last half hour as your family prefers.
  - pattern: cooking-meat-by-temperature
    note: Mince must reach 75 °C, which hours of simmering easily does. Where the probe matters is with ragù made the day before. Reheat it until it bubbles all through and the centre of the pot reads well above 75 °C.
  - pattern: egg-pasta-dough
    note: Use 400 g 00 flour and 4 large eggs, with no salt or oil, as most Emilian cooks do. Make the well on a wooden board while the ragù simmers.
  - pattern: kneading-and-resting-dough
    note: Knead for the full 8 to 10 minutes and rest it wrapped for 30 minutes to an hour, which fits neatly into the ragù's simmering time.
  - pattern: rolling-pasta-sheets
    note: Roll to about 1 mm, usually the second-thinnest setting, so the ribbons have enough body to carry a heavy meat sauce. If you hand-roll a *sfoglia*, its rougher surface holds the ragù even better.
  - pattern: cutting-pasta-ribbons
    note: Cut by hand to about 7 mm, which swells close to Bologna's registered 8 mm once cooked. Shake the ribbons open and coil them into nests of about 100 g.
  - pattern: drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta
    note: Hold the nests on a semolina-dusted tray for up to a couple of hours while the ragù finishes. To cook another day, freeze them on the tray and cook from frozen.
  - pattern: cooking-pasta-al-dente
    note: Fresh tagliatelle need only 1 to 3 minutes. Lift them out while they're still springy and slightly firm, because they finish in the sauce.
  - pattern: finishing-pasta-in-the-pan
    note: Warm about 100 ml of ragù per 100 g nest in a wide pan, add the tagliatelle and a splash of pasta water, and toss for 30 seconds until every ribbon is coated, not drowned.
  - pattern: grating-hard-cheese
    note: Grate Parmigiano Reggiano fresh on a rasp and pass it at the table, about 10 g a person. It's the cheese of the same region.
  - pattern: pairing-by-region
    note: A dry Lambrusco, the sparkling red of Emilia, cuts through the richness and is the local habit; a Sangiovese di Romagna is a good still alternative. Sparkling mineral water works too.

## About the dish

In Bologna, ragù traditionally goes with fresh egg tagliatelle, not the spaghetti most of the world pairs it with. A good ragù is a meat sauce first: thick, glossy and deep brown-red, with soft, fine-textured meat and just enough tomato to tie it together. The pasta is thin, golden and slightly rough, so the sauce clings to it. In 1982 the Accademia Italiana della Cucina registered a reference recipe with beef, pancetta, white wine, milk and a little tomato but families still disagree about milk, the cut of meat and how much tomato.

## What you need

For the ragù:

- 500 g coarsely minced beef (or half beef, half pork)
- 150 g pancetta, finely chopped
- 50 g each onion, carrot and celery
- 2 tablespoons butter or olive oil
- 125 ml dry white wine
- 200 g passata, or 2 tablespoons tomato paste
- 125 ml full-cream milk
- About 500 ml light meat stock, salt and pepper

For the pasta:

- 400 g 00 flour, plus fine semolina for dusting
- 4 large eggs (about 230 g shelled)
- Parmigiano Reggiano, to serve
- Equipment: heavy pot, large wooden board, pasta machine or long rolling pin, probe thermometer

## Where it usually goes wrong

- **The ragù tastes of tomato, not meat.** Too much passata, or not enough browning. Keep the tomato small and get colour on the mince: see [Slow-cooking a ragù](../../patterns/slow-cooked-ragu/) and [Browning meat](../../patterns/browning-meat/).
- **The tagliatelle stick together in clumps.** They were cut while the sheet was still soft, or left pressed in the roll. Wait for the leathery stage and open them at once: see [Cutting tagliatelle and pappardelle](../../patterns/cutting-pasta-ribbons/).
- **Soft, flabby pasta.** Fresh pasta was boiled like dried. Taste after a minute and finish it in the sauce: see [Cooking pasta al dente](../../patterns/cooking-pasta-al-dente/).
