## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking risotto on the stove. It describes how to add the stock so the rice cooks evenly, releases its starch into a creamy sauce and finishes with a little bite in about 16 to 18 minutes.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep the stock at a bare simmer in a pot next to the risotto, about 1 litre for every 300 g of rice, with a little extra hot water in reserve. | Hot stock keeps the risotto at a steady simmer. Cold stock stops the cooking each time, so the outside of the grain softens while the centre stays hard. |
| Add one ladle at a time, just enough to cover the rice, and add the next when the pan is nearly dry again. | A small amount of liquid lets the grains rub against each other and release starch, and it stops you overshooting into a soupy risotto. |
| Stir often but not constantly, every 20 to 30 seconds, scraping the base and sides. | Regular stirring helps release the starch and stops the rice catching on the base. Non-stop stirring isn't needed and cools the pan. |
| Start tasting at about 14 minutes and stop when the grain is tender with a tiny firm centre, usually 16 to 18 minutes for Carnaroli. | Packet times are only a guide, and the rice keeps softening during the finish and on the plate. |
| Stop adding stock while the risotto is still slightly loose, flowing gently when you shake the pan (*all'onda*, like a wave). | The butter and cheese at the end thicken it further, so a risotto that looks right at this stage ends up stiff. |

## Context

This pattern suits classic stovetop risotto with any flavouring.

It does not suit oven-baked "no-stir" risotto, rice cooker or pressure-cooker versions, or rice soups where the rice cooks in a big pot of broth.

There's a trade-off between attention and convenience. The ladle-by-ladle method takes 20 minutes of standing at the stove, but gives you control over texture and seasoning. Adding most of the stock at once, or baking the risotto, frees you up but gives a less creamy, less even result. This pattern takes the traditional route, and the shortcut is fine for a busy weeknight when nobody minds.

## Usage

**Choosing and seasoning the stock:** homemade [brodo](../making-brodo/) gives the best flavour. Bought stock and stock cubes are often very salty and strong, so dilute them about half and half with water. Salt the stock lightly, or not at all, because it reduces as the rice cooks and the cheese at the end adds more salt. Adjust near the end (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)).

**Keeping the rhythm:** after the wine is absorbed, add the first ladle. The risotto should bubble steadily, not boil hard (the liquid evaporates before the rice absorbs it) or barely move (the rice stews). Each ladle takes 1 to 2 minutes to be absorbed. When you drag a spoon through the rice and the line holds for a moment before closing, it's time for the next.

**Judging doneness:** bite a grain. Raw rice is chalky and gritty. Al dente rice is soft all over with a small, firm point in the middle, and no white dot when you squash a grain. If it's cooked but too thick, loosen it with a splash of stock.

**Common mistakes:** cold stock, several ladles at once, and cooking it until it looks finished in the pan. Take it off the heat while it still flows, then go straight to [finishing the risotto](../finishing-risotto/).
