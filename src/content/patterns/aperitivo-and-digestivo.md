## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants to open or close a meal the Italian way. It describes the *aperitivo* (a light, often bitter drink with small bites before a meal) and the *digestivo* (a small after-dinner drink such as amaro or grappa), and how to host both with moderation and plenty of non-alcoholic choices.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Serve a light, lower-alcohol aperitivo, often bitter or sparkling, about 30 to 60 minutes before dinner. | The word comes from the Latin for "to open": bitterness and bubbles are meant to wake the appetite, not fill you up. |
| Offer a few salty bites such as olives, crisps, nuts or a little focaccia, never a full meal. | Small salty snacks soften the effect of the drink and keep guests comfortable without spoiling the dinner you've cooked. |
| Always have a good non-alcoholic option, such as a bitter Italian soda, a tonic with citrus or sparkling water with orange. | Not everyone drinks, and an appealing alternative lets everyone join in without feeling singled out. |
| After the meal, offer a digestivo as a small pour of amaro, grappa or limoncello, poured only for those who want it. | These are strong drinks (often 30 to 40 % alcohol), so a little is enough; coffee or an herbal tea works just as well. |

## Context

This pattern suits relaxed dinners at home and drinks with friends, and it explains what to expect at a bar in Italy, where aperitivo usually runs in the early evening and often comes with snacks.

It does not suit anyone who is pregnant, under 18, driving, taking medication that interacts with alcohol or avoiding alcohol for any reason. Australian guidelines recommend healthy adults drink no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day, and that pregnant women and under-18s don't drink at all.

There's a trade-off between ritual and restraint. The aperitivo and digestivo are pleasant traditions that frame a meal, but they add drinks to an evening that may already include wine. This pattern keeps both small and optional: one aperitivo, one small digestivo at most, water on the table throughout, and no pressure on anyone to drink. Skipping one or both is entirely normal.

## Usage

**Setting up an aperitivo:** classic drinks include a spritz (prosecco, a bitter aperitivo such as Aperol or Campari, and a splash of soda), an Americano (Campari, red vermouth and soda) or simply a glass of chilled prosecco or dry white. Serve sparkling drinks cold (see [Serving wine at the right temperature](../serving-wine-at-the-right-temperature/)). Italian non-alcoholic bitters such as Crodino or Sanbittèr, or a mocktail of bitter tonic, orange and ice, look and taste just as festive.

**Keeping the bites small:** a bowl of olives, some salted nuts, crisps, a few cubes of hard cheese or a small piece of focaccia is enough for four to six people. If you'd like something more generous, serve a proper antipasto at the table instead (see [Building an antipasto board](../building-an-antipasto-board/)). Food safety still applies: don't leave cheese, cured meat or dips out for more than 2 hours in warm weather, and refrigerate or discard them by the 4-hour mark.

**Offering a digestivo:** after dessert and coffee (see [Drinking coffee the Italian way](../ordering-coffee-in-italy/)), you might put out a bottle of amaro, grappa or chilled limoncello with small glasses. Pour about 30 ml, served neat or over ice. Ask rather than pour, and offer tea or water just as warmly.

**Being a good host:** make sure anyone driving has a lift or alcohol-free drinks, keep water glasses full, and never top up a glass that someone has covered or left untouched. The pleasure of aperitivo is the conversation, not the alcohol.
