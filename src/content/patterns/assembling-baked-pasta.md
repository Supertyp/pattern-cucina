## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making lasagne or other baked pasta. It describes an order of work that keeps the pasta tender, the layers moist and the dish neat to serve, and how to make it ahead safely.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Make the sauces a little looser than you'd serve them on pasta. | Pasta keeps absorbing liquid in the oven, so a sauce that's already thick leaves the dish dry and stiff. |
| Par-cook dried pasta shapes for about half the packet time, and blanch fresh lasagne sheets for 30 seconds or use them raw. | The pasta finishes cooking in the sauce. Fully cooked pasta turns soft and mushy after 30 to 40 minutes in the oven. |
| Start with a thin layer of sauce, then alternate pasta and sauces, and finish with sauce and grated cheese, with no pasta left bare. | Sauce underneath stops sticking and burning, and exposed pasta edges dry out and go hard. |
| Bake at about 180 to 200 °C (160 to 180 °C fan-forced), covered for the first half if it browns fast, until bubbling at the edges and golden on top. | Covering lets the middle heat through before the top burns, and uncovering at the end gives a crisp, golden crust. |
| Rest it for 15 to 20 minutes before cutting. | As it cools slightly the sauces set, so it cuts into neat portions instead of sliding apart. |

## Context

This pattern suits lasagne (with [ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/) and [besciamella](../making-besciamella/), or with vegetables), cannelloni, and baked short pasta such as pasta al forno or baked ziti. It's a dish for planning ahead, which helps when you're cooking a big meal (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)).

It does not suit pasta that's simply sauced and served (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)) or filled pasta such as ravioli, which is boiled.

There's a trade-off between fresh and dried sheets. Thin fresh egg pasta, as in Emilia-Romagna, gives many delicate layers and a lighter result, but it takes time to make (see [Rolling pasta sheets](../rolling-pasta-sheets/)). Dried and no-boil sheets are convenient and hold their shape, but need wetter sauce and give a firmer, chunkier lasagne. This pattern works with both. Choose fresh for a special meal and dried for an everyday bake.

## Usage

**Building the layers:** use a ceramic or glass dish about 6 to 8 cm deep. Spread a thin layer of sauce over the base, then a single layer of pasta, overlapping sheets by a centimetre or so. Spread sauce thinly to the edges each time, more like a coating than a filling. Four to six layers is typical. For no-boil sheets, check the packet and make sure every sheet is fully covered, or the corners stay hard.

**Getting the moisture right:** the assembled dish should look generous with sauce, with besciamella that pours slowly and ragù that's spoonable rather than stiff. If you're using dried tubes, the sauce should coat them and pool a little in the dish. A skewer pushed into the centre near the end should slide through the pasta easily.

**Making ahead and leftovers:** if you assemble ahead, let the hot sauces cool first, then cover the dish and put it in the fridge within 2 hours, and bake within a day. Add 15 to 20 minutes to the baking time from the fridge, and check that the centre is steaming hot. Cool leftovers quickly in shallow portions, refrigerate within 2 hours, and reheat until piping hot all the way through. Use oven gloves, and take care with steam when you lift the foil.
