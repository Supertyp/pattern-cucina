## Summary

This pattern is for anyone baking focaccia at home. It describes how to proof a soft dough in an oiled tray, dimple it, season it with a salty brine, and bake it so the inside is light and the outside golden and crisp.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Make a soft dough, about 70 to 80 % hydration for a thick, airy tray focaccia (see [Choosing dough hydration](../dough-hydration/)). | The extra water turns to steam in the oven and opens the crumb, and the tray holds the slack dough in shape. |
| Oil the tray generously, about 3 to 4 tablespoons for a 23 by 33 cm tin, and let the dough rise in it. | The oil fries the base to a crisp, golden crust and stops sticking. Proofing in the tray means you never knock the gas out by moving the dough. |
| Press deep dimples all over with oiled fingertips, right down to the tray, then pour over a *salamoia* (brine of water, oil and salt). | The dimples stop the top puffing into one dome and catch pools of brine, which keeps the surface moist, salty and golden in the oven. |
| Bake on a low shelf at 220 to 230 °C (200 to 210 °C fan-forced) for 20 to 25 minutes, and cool it on a rack. | Strong bottom heat crisps the base before the top over-browns, and a rack lets steam escape so the base stays crisp. |

## Context

This pattern suits Ligurian-style focaccia, thick tray focaccia with rosemary, olives, tomatoes or onions, and Roman-style tray pizza.

It does not suit round pizza baked on a stone (see [Baking pizza in a home oven](../baking-pizza-at-home/)), or stuffed and cheese-filled focaccia such as *focaccia di Recco*, which uses a different, unleavened dough.

There's a trade-off between tradition and ease. Genoese focaccia (*fugassa*) is traditionally made from a firmer dough of about 60 to 65 % water, rolled or pressed thin and soaked with plenty of brine, giving a flatter bread with a soft, oily surface. Modern home focaccia uses a much wetter, often no-knead dough, which is easier and gives a taller, airier crumb. This pattern takes the wetter home style because it's forgiving. Choose the firmer Genoese dough when you want a thin, salty focaccia like those of Liguria.

## Usage

**Getting the dough into the tray:** after its first rise (or a night in the fridge, see [Cold-fermenting dough](../cold-fermenting-dough/)), tip the dough into the oiled tray and turn it once to coat. Gently press it towards the corners. It will resist and shrink back at first. Leave it 15 to 20 minutes and press again, repeating until it fills the tray. Don't force it, as tearing knocks out gas.

**Proofing and dimpling:** cover the tray and let the dough rise until it's puffy, wobbly and about doubled, usually 1 to 2 hours at room temperature. Dip your fingertips in oil and press them firmly straight down, all the way to the tray, in rows across the surface. Whisk the brine, for example 50 ml water, 25 ml olive oil and 5 g salt, and pour it over so it pools in the dimples. Add rosemary, olives or sliced onion now, and let it rest 20 to 30 minutes more.

**Baking and checking:** bake until the top is deep golden and the edges are crisp and coming away from the tin. Lift a corner with a spatula: the base should be golden brown and firm. If the top browns before the base, move the tray lower. Turn it out onto a rack within a few minutes, or it will steam soggy in the tin. Focaccia is best the day it's made. Leftovers revive in a hot oven for a few minutes, and stale pieces make excellent breadcrumbs (see [Using day-old bread](../using-day-old-bread/)).
