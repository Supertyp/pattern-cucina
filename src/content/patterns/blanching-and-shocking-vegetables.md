## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants vegetables bright, evenly cooked and ready to finish later. It describes how to cook them briefly in plenty of fast-boiling salted water, then plunge them into iced water to stop the cooking.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a big pot of water (about 4 to 5 litres for 500 g of vegetables) at a full rolling boil, and salt it about as well as pasta water. | Lots of water returns to the boil quickly, so the vegetables cook fast and evenly. The salt seasons them from the inside. |
| Cut the vegetables into even pieces and cook in batches, one kind at a time. | Even pieces cook at the same rate, and small batches don't stop the water boiling. |
| Cook only until just tender with a slight bite: about 30 seconds for spinach, 1 to 3 minutes for asparagus and peas, 2 to 4 minutes for beans and broccoli. | The green colour is brightest after a short cook. Longer cooking turns chlorophyll olive-grey and the texture soft. |
| Lift them straight into a bowl of iced water, and take them out as soon as they're cold. | The cold stops the cooking and sets the colour. Left soaking, they turn waterlogged and lose flavour. |
| Drain well and dry them on a clean tea towel before finishing them. | Water left on the vegetables dilutes a dressing and makes hot oil spit. |

## Context

This pattern suits green beans, broccoli, asparagus, peas, broad beans, spinach and bitter greens such as *cime di rapa* (turnip tops), and also vegetables you want to freeze. Briefly dipping tomatoes and peaches to loosen the skin is a related trick (see [Peeling and seeding tomatoes](../peeling-and-seeding-tomatoes/)).

It does not suit vegetables you'll roast, grill or braise straight from raw, which develop more flavour that way (see [Roasting vegetables](../roasting-vegetables/)), or potatoes and root vegetables, which need to start in cold water to cook evenly.

There's a trade-off between colour and convenience. The iced-water shock is a restaurant habit that keeps vegetables vivid and lets you prepare them hours ahead. Many Italian home cooks skip it, draining the greens and tossing them straight into a pan of garlic and oil, which is quicker and keeps more flavour in the vegetable. This pattern shocks when you're cooking ahead or serving the vegetables cold. Skip it when they go straight into the pan (see [Sautéing greens with garlic and chilli](../sauteing-greens-with-garlic/)).

## Usage

**Setting up:** before the water boils, fill a large bowl with ice and cold water, about one part ice to two parts water, and keep a slotted spoon or spider and a tray lined with a tea towel next to it. Use about a heaped teaspoon of salt per litre (see [Salting the pasta water](../salting-pasta-water/)). Leave the lid off once the vegetables go in, so you can see the water come back to the boil.

**Timing:** start tasting early. Take out a piece, cool it in the ice water and bite it. It should be tender at the edge with a little resistance in the centre, and bright green. Thick stalks of broccoli or asparagus take longer than the tips, so cut them separately or put them in a minute earlier. Cook pale vegetables first and strongly flavoured or coloured ones (cime di rapa, beetroot greens) last, so the same water can be reused.

**Finishing:** blanched vegetables keep, covered, in the fridge for a day. Reheat them in a pan with butter or olive oil for a minute or two, dress them for a salad, or add them to a pasta in its last minute. If they've gone grey, they were cooked too long or left too long in a covered pot or a hot pan. If they're squeaky, give them another minute next time. Warm vegetables from the water should be eaten soon or cooled fast and refrigerated within 2 hours.
