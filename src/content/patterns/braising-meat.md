## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking tough cuts slowly in liquid. It describes braising: browning the meat, adding aromatics and a little liquid, then cooking it covered, low and long, until it's tender enough to cut with a spoon.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose cuts with plenty of connective tissue, such as beef shin, chuck or cheek, veal shank or lamb shoulder. | Their collagen slowly melts into gelatine, which makes the meat tender and gives the sauce body. Lean cuts just dry out. |
| [Brown the meat](../browning-meat/) well, soften a [soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/) in the same pot, then deglaze with wine. | Browning and the softened vegetables build the flavour, and the wine lifts the browned bits into the sauce. |
| Add liquid to come one-third to halfway up the meat, not to cover it. | Less liquid gives a concentrated sauce, and the exposed top cooks gently in the steam under the lid. Cubed stews can take a little more. |
| Cover and cook at a bare simmer, or in the oven at 150 to 160 °C (130 to 140 °C fan-forced), for 2 to 3 hours. | Collagen breaks down best just below boiling over a long time. A hard boil squeezes the muscle fibres dry before the collagen has melted. |
| Cook until a fork slides in and out with no resistance, then reduce the sauce if it's thin. | Tough cuts go through a firm, chewy stage before they turn tender, so time alone isn't a reliable test. |

## Context

This pattern suits classic Italian braises: ossobuco (veal shank), brasato (a whole piece of beef braised in red wine), spezzatino (a stew of cubed meat), lamb or kid shoulder, and beef cheeks. Serve them with [polenta](../cooking-polenta/) or bread.

It does not suit tender, lean cuts such as fillet, sirloin or chicken breast, which become dry and grainy when braised. Cook those quickly to temperature (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)).

There's a trade-off between the stove and the oven. Braising on the stovetop is traditional and easy to check, but the base can catch and you need to watch the heat. The oven gives even, all-round heat and you can leave it alone. This pattern suggests the oven for large pieces and long braises, and the stove for smaller stews you'll stir anyway.

## Usage

**Building the braise:** use a heavy pot just big enough to hold the meat in one layer. For ossobuco, dust the shanks lightly in flour before browning. Add the soffritto, deglaze with a glass of wine (see [Deglazing with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/)). Then add stock, tomatoes or a mix, and bring to a simmer before covering.

**Keeping it low:** the liquid should just tremble, with a bubble or two now and then. Turn the meat every 30 to 45 minutes so every side spends time in the liquid (baste delicate pieces such as veal shanks instead, so the marrow stays in the bone), and add a splash of hot stock or water if it's drying out. Cubes for spezzatino, about 4 cm, take around 1½ to 2 hours. Veal shanks are often tender after 1½ to 2 hours; beef shin and whole pieces for brasato take 2½ to 3 hours. Start testing early.

**Finishing the sauce:** lift out the meat and keep it warm. Skim off fat, then boil the sauce until it coats the back of a spoon, or blend it smooth for brasato. Slice a whole piece across the grain and pour the sauce over. Many braises taste better the next day: cool them quickly, refrigerate within 2 hours, lift off the set fat and reheat until piping hot.

**Common mistakes:** a rolling boil (the meat goes stringy), too much liquid (a watery sauce), and stopping during the firm stage because "the time is up". Tough meat usually needs longer, not less.
