## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who serves or eats bread with an Italian meal. It describes the role bread plays at the Italian table, what not to add to it, and when *fare la scarpetta* (wiping up sauce with bread; the phrase means "making the little shoe") is welcome.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Put plain, sliced bread on the table and leave out butter and bowls of dipping oil. | In Italy bread accompanies the food, and butter or oil on the side is uncommon. Bread dipped in oil as a starter is mainly a restaurant habit from outside Italy. |
| Eat bread with the antipasto, secondo and contorno, not as a starter on its own. | Bread is there to carry and balance the other food. Filling up on it first leaves less room for the meal you've cooked. |
| Go easy on bread with pasta and risotto. | Both are already starchy, so most Italians don't eat bread alongside them, though using a piece at the end for the sauce is common. |
| Wipe up sauce with a small piece of bread at casual meals, and skip it at formal ones. | *Fare la scarpetta* is a compliment to the cook at home and in a trattoria, but traditional etiquette still frowns on it at formal dinners. |

## Context

This pattern suits home meals and everyday restaurants, served with plain crusty bread such as a rustic loaf or Tuscan unsalted bread, or with grissini.

It does not suit bread-based dishes in their own right, such as bruschetta, crostini or focaccia, where oil is part of the dish. Bruschetta toasted and drizzled with oil in the kitchen is a perfectly Italian antipasto. For using up leftover bread, see [Using day-old bread](../using-day-old-bread/).

There's a trade-off between custom and what your guests expect. Many people outside Italy are used to bread with butter or oil while waiting for food, and may feel something is missing. This pattern follows the Italian custom because it keeps the focus on the courses. If guests will wait a while, it's better to serve a small antipasto (see [Building an antipasto board](../building-an-antipasto-board/)) than a basket of bread, and if your household loves dipping bread in good oil, enjoy it as a snack of its own.

## Usage

**Setting out the bread:** slice it shortly before serving so it doesn't dry out, and put it in a basket or on a board within reach of everyone. Italians often place bread directly on the tablecloth beside the plate rather than on a side plate (see [Setting the table](../setting-the-italian-table/)). Tear pieces from your slice with your fingers rather than biting into a whole slice.

**Timing it with the courses:** if you serve an antipasto of cured meats and cheese, bread belongs there. With the primo, leave the basket on the table but don't expect much to go. With the secondo and contorno, bread comes into its own, helping to pick up juices and vegetables. Plan about one or two slices per person for a multi-course meal; see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/) for how the courses run.

**Doing the scarpetta well:** take a small piece, hold it in your fingers (or on your fork if the setting is a little more formal) and sweep it through the last of the sauce in one or two passes. Do it at the end, not as you go, and don't scrub the plate clean. At home, a cook will usually take it as the best compliment you can give.

**What about the oil cruet?** Oil and vinegar on an Italian table are generally for dressing your own salad, not for dipping. In restaurants in Italy the bread may come with a small cover charge, the *coperto*, which covers bread and the table setting.
