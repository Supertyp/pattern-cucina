## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who browns meat before cooking it further. It describes how to get a deep brown crust on pieces, whole cuts and mince, and why each step matters.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pat the meat dry with paper towel, and salt it just before it goes in the pan. | Surface water has to boil off before browning can start, and it keeps the surface at about 100 °C, too cool for the browning (Maillard) reactions. |
| Heat a heavy pan over medium-high heat with a thin film of oil until the oil shimmers. | A hot, heavy pan holds its heat when the meat goes in, so the surface browns instead of slowly warming up. |
| Brown in batches, leaving a finger's width between pieces. | Crowded meat drops the pan's temperature and releases juices that can't evaporate, so it steams and goes grey. |
| Leave each side alone for 2 to 4 minutes, until it releases easily from the pan. | Meat sticks at first and lets go once a crust has formed. Moving it early tears the surface and stops the crust building. |
| Keep the browned bits on the base (*fondo*, the fond) and dissolve them with wine or stock afterwards. | Those bits are concentrated flavour from the same browning reactions, and they become the backbone of the sauce. |

## Context

This pattern suits pieces for spezzatino or other stews, shanks and whole cuts for [braising](../braising-meat/), sausages, and mince for a [ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/) or meatballs.

It does not suit dishes deliberately cooked pale, such as boiled meats (*bollito*), white stews (*in bianco*) or poached chicken, or thin cutlets, which cook through in the time it takes to brown them.

There's a trade-off between flavour and time. Browning in batches adds 15 to 20 minutes, splatters the stove and needs your attention, but gives a much deeper flavour. Some cooks skip it for weeknight stews or ragù, and some traditional ragù recipes only cook the mince until it loses its raw colour. This pattern favours proper browning, and skipping it is reasonable when the dish has lots of other flavour or you're short of time.

## Usage

**Getting the heat right:** use stainless steel, cast iron or enamelled cast iron, not a thin non-stick pan. Heat it for a couple of minutes before adding oil, then add the meat when the oil ripples. It should sizzle loudly the moment it touches the pan. If it hisses weakly, take it out and wait. If the oil smokes heavily, turn the heat down a little. Brown each side to a deep chestnut colour, not black.

**Mince:** spread it out in a thick layer, or press it in as one large patty, and leave it to brown underneath before breaking it up. Stirring it from the start just steams it into grey crumbs. For a large amount, brown in two or three batches and pour off excess liquid fat if it pools.

**Batches and the fond:** put browned pieces on a plate (keep any juices to add back later). If the fond starts to look too dark between batches, add a splash of water and scrape it up, then wipe the pan and start again with fresh oil. A dark brown fond is good, while black, burnt bits taste bitter. Then go on to [deglazing with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/).

**Safety:** hot oil spits, especially on wet meat, so dry it well, lay pieces in the pan away from you, and keep handles turned inwards. Never put water on burning oil: turn off the heat and cover the pan with a lid. Browning doesn't cook meat through or "seal in" the juices, so pieces still need their full cooking time (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)).
