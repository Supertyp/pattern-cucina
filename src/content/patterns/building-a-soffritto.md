## Summary

This pattern is for anyone starting an Italian sauce, soup or braise. It describes how to cook a soffritto, the slowly softened base of onion, carrot and celery, so it gives the dish a deep, sweet, rounded flavour.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use roughly 2 parts onion to 1 part carrot and 1 part celery, cut to an even size. | This is the commonly agreed balance of sweetness and savouriness, and even pieces cook at the same rate. |
| Start the vegetables in olive oil or butter over low to medium heat, with a pinch of salt. | Gentle heat softens them and draws out their sugars, and salt pulls out moisture so they soften faster without browning. |
| Cook for 10 to 15 minutes for a quick sauce and up to 20 to 30 minutes for a ragù, until soft, translucent and at most lightly golden. | Long, slow cooking turns sharp raw onion sweet and mellow. Dark browning adds bitterness and changes the character of the dish. |
| Add garlic, herbs or tomato paste only in the last minute or two. | Garlic burns in moments and turns bitter, and concentrate scorches if it sits too long on the base of the pan. |

## Context

This pattern suits ragù, tomato sauces with onion, bean and vegetable soups such as minestrone, risotto bases, and braises such as ossobuco or spezzatino. The same idea works with other aromatics: some regions add garlic, parsley, rosemary or pancetta.

It does not suit sauces built on garlic alone (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)) or very quick dishes where onion would never have time to soften.

There's a trade-off between time and depth. A soffritto cooked slowly for half an hour tastes noticeably richer than one cooked for 5 minutes, but it ties up the stove. This pattern takes the slow route for long-cooked dishes. For a weeknight, chop finer and cook a shorter time, or make a big batch ahead: cooked soffritto keeps in the fridge for a few days and freezes well in small portions.

## Usage

**Battuto and soffritto:** the raw chopped mix is called *battuto* (beaten, from the chopping), and once it's cooked in fat it becomes the soffritto. Chop fine for ragù and smooth sauces, and coarser for chunky soups (see [Dicing onion, carrot and celery](../dicing-soffritto-vegetables/)). A food processor works, but pulse briefly and stop before it turns to a wet pulp.

**The cooking:** use a heavy pan wide enough for the vegetables to sit in a shallow layer, and enough fat to coat them, about 3 tablespoons of olive oil per 300 g of vegetables. You should hear a gentle sizzle, never loud spitting. Stir every few minutes. It's ready when the onion is translucent, the carrot is soft enough to crush with a spoon, and the kitchen smells sweet rather than sharp.

**Common mistakes:** heat too high is the usual problem. If the edges brown before the middle softens, turn it down and add a spoonful of water to stop it catching. If anything goes black, start again, because burnt bits make the whole dish bitter. Too little fat makes the vegetables stew and stick. Once the soffritto is ready, you can brown meat on top or [deglaze with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/) before the main liquid goes in.
