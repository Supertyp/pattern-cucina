## Summary

This pattern is for anyone putting together a shared antipasto (the course "before the meal") from cured meats (*salumi*), cheeses (*formaggi*), preserved vegetables and bread. It describes how much to buy, how to choose a balanced mix and how to serve it so it tastes its best and stays safe.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Allow about 40 to 60 g of cured meat and 30 to 50 g of cheese per person before a full meal, and roughly double that if the board is the meal. | An antipasto is meant to open the appetite, not end it, and it's easy to overbuy cured meat. |
| Pick 2 or 3 cured meats, 2 or 3 cheeses and 2 or 3 vegetables that contrast. | A sweet raw ham, a peppery salami, a hard aged cheese and a soft fresh one, plus something sharp, keep each bite interesting. |
| Take meats and hard cheeses out of the fridge 20 to 30 minutes before serving, but keep fresh cheeses cold until the last minute. | Fat that's fridge-cold tastes waxy and mute, while fresh cheeses like mozzarella spoil fastest. |
| Have prosciutto sliced paper-thin and lay it in loose folds rather than flat stacks. | Thin slices melt on the tongue, and stacked slices stick together and tear. |
| Bring the board in when guests sit down and put it back in the fridge within 2 hours. | Under the FSANZ 2-hour/4-hour rule, cut meats and soft cheese left out for up to 2 hours can still be refrigerated and used later. |

## Context

This pattern suits a cold antipasto before a meal, an aperitivo with a few friends, or a casual summer lunch built around good bought ingredients.

It does not suit hot antipasti such as fried or baked dishes (see [Deep-frying at home](../deep-frying-at-home/)), or a plated seafood starter, which is a different course altogether.

There's a trade-off between a lavish board and a modest one. A big spread looks generous and works as a meal in itself, but before a primo and secondo it fills people up and leaves you with expensive leftovers. This pattern takes the modest side when a meal follows, and the lavish side only when the board is the meal (see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/)).

## Usage

**Choosing the mix:** for cured meats, combine something sweet and raw such as prosciutto, something spiced such as salami or finocchiona, and something rich such as mortadella or coppa (see [Buying guanciale, pancetta and prosciutto](../buying-cured-meats/)). For cheese, a chunk of [Parmigiano Reggiano or Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/), a semi-soft cheese and a fresh one like burrata or bocconcini (see [Buying mozzarella, burrata and ricotta](../buying-fresh-cheeses/)). Add vegetables preserved in oil (*sott'olio*) or vinegar (*sott'aceto*), such as artichokes, peppers and pickled vegetables (*giardiniera*), olives and [grilled vegetables](../grilling-vegetables/). Add bread, grissini or focaccia, and perhaps fruit in season, such as figs or melon with the prosciutto.

**Slicing and arranging:** buy meat sliced to order and use it within a day or two; if you have to slice ahead, lay the slices on baking paper so they don't weld together. Salami is sliced thin, slightly on the diagonal. Break hard cheese into rough chunks with a short, pointed knife rather than slicing it, so it shows its grainy texture. Group each item in its own cluster, with the vegetables on a small plate or in bowls so their oil doesn't soak the bread. A cutting board, a large platter or a slate all work.

**Keeping it right:** meat that looks sweaty and translucent has been out too long or got too warm. If the room is hot, put out half and refill from the fridge. Leftovers that have been out under 2 hours can go back in the fridge; anything out for over 4 hours goes in the bin.
