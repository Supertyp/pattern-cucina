## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks Italian dishes with cured pork or serves it on a board. It describes what guanciale, pancetta and prosciutto are, which ones to cook and which to eat raw, how to buy and store them, and how bacon compares.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use guanciale (cured pork cheek) for Roman pastas such as carbonara, gricia and amatriciana. | It has more fat than lean, and the fat renders into a sweet, porky base that carries the sauce. |
| Use pancetta (cured pork belly, usually unsmoked) as the all-round cooking choice for soffritto, ragù, beans and pasta. | It's leaner than guanciale but cured the same way, so it gives a clean pork flavour without smoke. |
| Eat prosciutto crudo raw, sliced paper-thin to order, and add it to hot food only at the end. | Long curing makes it silky and sweet raw, while long cooking turns it tough and salty. The known exception is a brief sear, as in saltimbocca, where it crisps on the meat in a minute or two. |
| Buy guanciale and pancetta in a piece and cut them yourself; buy prosciutto sliced just before you eat it. | A piece lets you cut thick strips that stay juicy, and prosciutto slices dry out and lose aroma within hours. |
| Use bacon only as a substitute, and expect a smokier, wetter result. | Bacon is cured quickly, often with added water, and smoked, so it releases liquid and its smoke changes the dish's flavour. |

## Context

This pattern suits cured pork for pasta sauces, soups, braises and antipasto boards.

It does not suit cooked hams (*prosciutto cotto*) or sausages, which are fresh meat with their own safe cooking temperatures (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)).

There's a trade-off between tradition and availability. Guanciale gives the classic flavour in Roman pastas, but it's not always easy to find outside Italy. This pattern prefers guanciale, then pancetta, then a thick-cut, mildly smoked streaky bacon. Pancetta is a fine and common substitute; bacon works but makes a different dish, which is worth knowing rather than hiding.

## Usage

**Recognising them:** guanciale is a rough triangle of mostly white fat with a thin seam of pink meat, often rubbed with black pepper on the outside. Pancetta comes flat (*tesa*) for cubing or rolled (*arrotolata*), which can also be sliced thinly and eaten raw on a board. Prosciutto di Parma and Prosciutto di San Daniele are the best-known DOP hams (see [Reading DOP, IGP and STG labels](../reading-dop-igp-labels/)); good ones have rosy meat, white (not yellow) fat and a sweet smell.

**At the counter:** ask for prosciutto sliced so thin you can almost see through it, and ask the slicer to lay the slices flat on paper rather than piled up. For cooking meats, ask for a single thick piece. For how to render guanciale and pancetta, see [Rendering guanciale and pancetta](../rendering-guanciale/); you rarely need extra oil.

**Storing:** wrap pieces of guanciale and pancetta in baking paper, then loosely in a bag, and keep them in the fridge; they last for weeks, and you can trim off any dried surface. Keep sliced prosciutto in its paper in a sealed container in the fridge, take it out 15 minutes before serving and eat it within a day or two. Rinds and offcuts are good in soups and beans.

**Food safety:** cured meats eaten cold are a higher-risk food for *Listeria*. Pregnant women, and others at higher risk, should avoid cold cured meats such as prosciutto and salami, unless they're cooked to at least 75 °C and eaten straight away. Keep everything at 5 °C or below.
