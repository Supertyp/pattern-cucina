## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys Italian fresh cheeses. It describes the main kinds, how to judge freshness, which one to use raw and which to cook, and how to deal with their water.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Buy fresh cheeses close to their making date and eat them within a day or two of buying, well before the use-by date. | Fresh cheeses aren't aged, so their sweet, milky flavour fades quickly and their texture turns sour and slack. |
| Use *fior di latte* (cow's milk mozzarella) for cooking, and buffalo mozzarella and burrata mostly raw. | Fior di latte is firmer and milder and holds up in the oven; buffalo milk makes a richer, tangier, wetter cheese whose flavour is best uncooked. |
| For pizza in a home oven, drain fresh mozzarella well or use low-moisture mozzarella. | A home oven bakes more slowly than a pizza oven, so a wet cheese releases water before the base is cooked. |
| Drain ricotta in a lined sieve in the fridge before using it in fillings and cakes. | Excess whey makes fillings runny, splits pasta seals and gives cakes a wet, heavy crumb. |
| Serve fresh cheese cool but not fridge-cold, taking it out about 30 minutes before. | Cold mutes flavour and tightens the texture; the fat softens again near room temperature. |

## Context

This pattern suits mozzarella, burrata, stracciatella and ricotta for salads, antipasti, pizza, baked pasta, filled pasta and desserts.

It does not suit hard grating cheeses (see [Buying Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana and Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/)) or mascarpone (see [Working with mascarpone](../working-with-mascarpone/)).

There's a trade-off between freshness and convenience. A mozzarella made that morning at a local cheesemaker or deli is far better than a long-life ball, but it doesn't keep. This pattern buys fresh when the cheese is the star, and uses a good supermarket ball or low-moisture block for pizza and baked dishes, where the difference is smaller.

## Usage

**Knowing the cheeses:** fior di latte is the everyday mozzarella, made from cow's milk. Mozzarella di Bufala Campana DOP is made from buffalo milk in southern Italy and carries the DOP seal; buffalo mozzarella from elsewhere can be good but isn't the protected cheese. Burrata is a pouch of mozzarella filled with stracciatella (shreds of curd in cream). Ricotta is made by reheating whey, so it's light, moist and mild; it's best bought fresh from a deli in its basket rather than as a smooth, long-life tub.

**Judging freshness:** good mozzarella is bright white (buffalo is porcelain white), with a thin, shiny skin, and it should feel springy and release milky liquid when cut. Burrata should be soft and heavy, with no sour smell. Yellowing, slime or a sour smell mean it's old. Keep mozzarella and burrata in their liquid in the fridge (see [Storing cheese](../storing-cheese/)).

**Managing the water:** for pizza or baked pasta, tear or slice mozzarella, put it in a sieve in the fridge for an hour or more (see [Topping pizza with restraint](../topping-pizza-with-restraint/)). For ricotta, line a sieve with a clean cloth or paper towel, set it over a bowl and leave it in the fridge for a few hours or overnight, until it's thick enough to hold a shape for [filled pasta](../shaping-filled-pasta/).

**Food safety:** fresh cheeses are soft, moist and ready to eat, which makes them a higher-risk food for *Listeria*. Keep them in the fridge at 5 °C or below and respect the use-by date. Australian guidance is that pregnant women, and others at higher risk, should avoid soft cheeses such as ricotta unless they're cooked to at least 75 °C and eaten straight away, which baked dishes usually achieve.
