## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys seafood for Italian dishes, from a whole fish for the oven to clams for a pasta. It describes the signs of freshness, how to handle live shellfish, how to use the fishmonger, and how to choose sustainably.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose whole fish with clear, bulging eyes, bright red gills, shiny skin and firm flesh that springs back. | As fish ages, the eyes cloud and sink, gills turn brown and the flesh softens as its proteins break down. |
| Smell everything: fresh seafood smells of the sea, never of ammonia or "fishy". | The fishy smell comes from compounds produced as fish spoils, so your nose is the quickest test. |
| Buy mussels and clams alive: tightly closed, or closing when tapped, with unbroken shells. | Shellfish spoil fast once dead, and you can't tell how long a dead one has been dead. |
| Ask the fishmonger what came in today, and let them scale, gut or fillet for you. | They know what's freshest and best value, and they have the tools and the bin for the mess. |
| Check a sustainability guide or certification, and be flexible about species. | Many stocks are overfished, and a similar local fish usually works in the same recipe. |

## Context

This pattern suits buying fish and shellfish at a fishmonger, market or supermarket counter for grilling, baking, frying, stews and pasta.

It does not suit eating seafood raw, such as crudo or oysters, which needs very fresh, sashimi-grade fish from a trusted supplier and isn't recommended for pregnant women, young children, older people or anyone with weakened immunity.

There's a trade-off between fresh and frozen. Truly fresh fish is wonderful, but "fresh" fish on a counter may have been thawed or be several days old, while seafood frozen soon after catch can be better. This pattern buys fresh when the signs are clear, and chooses good frozen prawns, squid or fillets rather than tired fresh ones.

## Usage

**Judging fish:** look at the eyes first, then lift the gill cover: the gills should be red and moist, not brown or grey. Press the flesh gently; it should spring back. Fillets should look moist and glossy, with no browning or drying at the edges and no liquid pooling in the tray. Whole fish keep better than fillets, and the bones and head are good for stock.

**Prawns, squid and shellfish:** prawns should have firm shells and heads attached, with no black discolouration at the joints or a soft, mushy feel. Squid should be glossy and smell sweet. Mussels and clams should feel heavy for their size and be closed; discard any with broken shells or that stay open when tapped. For cleaning and purging, see [Cleaning mussels and clams](../cleaning-mussels-and-clams/).

**Getting it home:** buy seafood last, and bring an insulated bag with an ice pack. Put fish in the coldest part of the fridge (5 °C or below) and cook it within a day. Keep live shellfish in a bowl covered with a damp cloth in the fridge, never in a sealed bag or fresh water, which kills them, and cook them that day or the next.

**Choosing sustainably:** look for certification labels such as the MSC blue fish, check a local sustainable seafood guide, and ask where and how the fish was caught. If a recipe calls for a species that's overfished or unavailable, ask for a local fish of similar texture: firm white fish for firm white fish, oily for oily.
