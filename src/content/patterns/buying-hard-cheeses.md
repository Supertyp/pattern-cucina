## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys hard Italian cheese for cooking and the table. It describes how to tell the three main grating cheeses apart, how to pick a good piece, and which one suits which dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Buy a wedge cut from the wheel, with rind on one side, and grate it just before use. | Once grated, cheese dries out and loses aroma within days, and some pre-grated products outside the EU add anti-caking starch or cellulose, which stops them melting smoothly. |
| Choose Parmigiano Reggiano aged about 24 months for all-round cooking, younger for eating with fruit, older for grating over rich dishes. | At 12 to 18 months it's mild and milky; at 24 it's crumbly and balanced; at 30 months and beyond it's drier, nuttier and more intense. |
| Use Grana Padano as the everyday, milder and cheaper alternative. | It's a similar cow's milk cheese, made over a larger area with a shorter minimum ageing (9 months against 12), so it's softer in flavour and price. |
| Use Pecorino Romano for Roman dishes and anything that wants a sharp, salty bite. | It's made from sheep's milk and heavily salted, so a little gives a strong, tangy flavour; it can overwhelm delicate sauces. |
| Check for the name on the rind (or the DOP seal on a pre-cut pack) and a moist, grainy, pale-straw interior. | The rind markings prove the origin, and a dry, cracked or sweating cut face means the piece was cut long ago or kept badly. |

## Context

This pattern suits grating cheese for pasta, risotto, soups, gratins, fillings and pesto, and serving chunks on an [antipasto board](../building-an-antipasto-board/).

It does not suit fresh and soft cheeses (see [Buying mozzarella, burrata and ricotta](../buying-fresh-cheeses/)) or the many regional pecorinos sold young for eating, which are softer and milder than Romano.

There's a trade-off between cost and character. Parmigiano Reggiano is the most expensive, and its flavour is wasted when melted into a long-cooked sauce. This pattern uses Grana Padano or a good local hard cheese for cooking in, and saves Parmigiano and Pecorino for finishing, where you taste them. Mixing Parmigiano and Pecorino is common and a good compromise, for example in a carbonara.

## Usage

**Reading the rind:** Parmigiano Reggiano has its name stamped in pin-dots all over the rind, and Grana Padano has a pattern of diamonds with its name. A pre-cut wedge should show a piece of that marking, or the pack should carry the DOP seal (see [Reading DOP, IGP and STG labels](../reading-dop-igp-labels/)).

**Choosing the piece:** buy from a counter that cuts from a big wheel and sells a lot. The paste should be pale straw to golden, grainy, with tiny white crystals (amino acid clusters, a sign of age, not mould). Pecorino Romano is whiter and smells sharper.

**Matching to the dish:** Pecorino goes into cacio e pepe, gricia and amatriciana; Parmigiano into risotto, brodo, ragù and Emilian filled pasta; either or both onto most tomato and vegetable sauces. Use less salt in the pasta water and sauce when Pecorino is involved. For how much and when, see [When to add cheese](../cheese-on-pasta/).

**Keeping it and using every bit:** wrap the wedge in baking or wax paper and keep it in the fridge (see [Storing cheese](../storing-cheese/)), and grate as you need it (see [Grating hard cheese](../grating-hard-cheese/)). Scrape the rind clean and simmer it in soups and bean dishes, then remove it before serving. These hard, aged cheeses are considered low risk for listeria, so they're generally fine for pregnant women.
