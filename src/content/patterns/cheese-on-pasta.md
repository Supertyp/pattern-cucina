## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who serves or eats pasta and wonders whether to add grated cheese. It describes the general Italian custom, the usual exception for seafood, how to pick between Parmigiano and Pecorino, and how to handle the question gracefully at someone else's table.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pass freshly grated hard cheese with most meat, tomato, vegetable and butter-based pasta, risotto and soups. | Aged cheese adds salt and savoury depth (umami) that rounds out these dishes, and grating it fresh keeps its aroma (see [Grating hard cheese](../grating-hard-cheese/)). |
| Generally leave cheese off pasta with fish or shellfish. | The concentrated flavour of long-aged cheese tends to cover the delicate, briny taste of seafood. Toasted breadcrumbs (*pangrattato*) are the traditional crunchy topping instead. |
| Use Parmigiano Reggiano or Grana Padano for northern and mild dishes, and Pecorino Romano for Roman dishes and sharp, peppery sauces. | Parmigiano is nutty and mellow, while sheep's milk Pecorino is saltier and tangier, matching the guanciale and pepper dishes it grew up with. |
| Let the cook decide: taste first, and if cheese isn't offered, ask politely rather than reaching for it. | The cook has already balanced the salt and flavour, and in Italy a dish served without cheese is usually meant to be eaten that way. |

## Context

This pattern suits family meals, dinner parties and restaurants, especially when you're cooking Italian food for others or eating in Italy.

It does not suit dishes where cheese is part of the recipe itself, such as cacio e pepe or carbonara (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)), or the few traditional seafood dishes that do use cheese, such as some baked, crumbed mussels from southern Italy.

There's a trade-off between tradition and personal taste. The no-cheese-with-seafood custom is real and widely observed, but it's a custom rather than a law, and it's eroding: some Italian cooks and many outside Italy enjoy a little Parmigiano on prawn pasta. This pattern follows the custom when you're a guest or cooking a classic dish, because it respects the cook's balance. At your own table, if you prefer a dusting of cheese, go ahead and use a light hand.

## Usage

**Serving the cheese:** grate it just before the meal into a small bowl with a spoon, or pass a wedge and a rasp. Allow about 5 to 10 g per person. Put it on the table rather than piling it on each plate, so everyone can season to taste. Tip it on in a small heap at the side and let it melt into the hot pasta as you eat.

**Choosing the cheese:** for ragù, butter and sage, pumpkin ravioli and risotto, reach for Parmigiano Reggiano or the slightly milder, cheaper Grana Padano. For amatriciana, gricia and other Roman dishes, Pecorino Romano is traditional; a half-and-half mix softens its saltiness. Remember that both are salty, so season the sauce a little less if cheese will go on (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)). More on buying them is in [Buying Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana and Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/).

**When seafood is involved:** for clams, mussels, prawns, squid or fish in the sauce, skip the cheese bowl. If you want a finishing touch, scatter crisp breadcrumbs fried in olive oil, a few drops of good oil, chopped parsley or lemon zest. When a dish combines seafood and vegetables, follow the seafood.

**As a guest:** taste before you season. If no cheese comes and you'd like some, ask simply and without fuss. In a restaurant in Italy a waiter may gently say it isn't served with that dish; take that as advice about the dish rather than a rebuke.
