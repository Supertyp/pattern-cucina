## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys dried pasta and wants it to cook up firm, with a surface that holds sauce. It describes what the pack should say, what good pasta looks like through the window, and when the cheaper packet is fine.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Check that the only ingredients are durum wheat semolina (*semola di grano duro*) and water. | Durum wheat has strong, plentiful protein that keeps pasta firm and al dente. Italian law requires it for dried pasta sold in Italy, but elsewhere soft wheat may be used. |
| Prefer pasta labelled bronze-die (*trafilata al bronzo*). | The rough bronze die leaves a matte, slightly porous surface that releases starch and grips sauce; smooth Teflon dies make glossy pasta the sauce slides off. |
| Look for slow or low-temperature drying (*essiccazione lenta*). | Drying gently over many hours rather than fast at high heat keeps the pasta's wheat flavour and structure, so it's less brittle and holds its shape in the pot. |
| Judge by eye: pale, dull and slightly dusty is good; bright yellow and shiny suggests fast, hot production. | The look follows directly from the die and the drying, so you can check even when the pack doesn't say. |
| Look at the protein figure: around 13 to 15 g per 100 g suggests a firmer bite. | More protein forms a stronger gluten network that holds starch in and keeps the pasta from going soft. |

## Context

This pattern suits dried durum-wheat pasta in all shapes, for everyday meals as well as special dishes.

It does not suit fresh egg pasta (see [Mixing egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/)), dried egg pasta such as nests of tagliatelle, which follow their own rules, or gluten-free pasta, which uses different grains entirely.

There's a trade-off between price and texture. Bronze-die, slow-dried pasta costs more and is made in smaller quantities, while large-brand smooth pasta is cheap and consistent. This pattern chooses bronze-die pasta when the sauce is light and has to cling, such as oil, cheese or a quick tomato sauce, and is happy with a good everyday brand for baked pasta, soups and chunky sauces where the difference matters less. Some cooks find the difference in taste small, and blind tastings don't always agree.

## Usage

**Reading the pack:** Italian packs list *pasta di semola di grano duro* as the product name. "Trafilata al bronzo" and "essiccata lentamente" or "a bassa temperatura" are claims producers make proudly, so if they're missing, assume the opposite. A named origin for the wheat is a bonus, not essential: Italian makers have long blended in durum wheat from Australia, Canada and the US.

**Looking at the pasta:** hold the pack to the light. Good pasta is creamy-pale, matte and feels slightly rough between your fingers, like fine sandpaper. In the pot it releases more starch, so the cooking water becomes cloudy and useful for finishing the sauce (see [Saving the pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/)). It also takes a minute or two longer to cook than the same shape from a fast-dried brand, so taste rather than trusting the clock (see [Cooking pasta al dente](../cooking-pasta-al-dente/)).

**Buying and keeping:** buy the shapes you actually cook (see [Matching pasta shape to sauce](../matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce/)) and keep them in their pack or an airtight jar in a cool, dry cupboard. Dried pasta keeps for a year or more, but broken pieces and dust at the bottom of the bag are a sign of fast drying or rough handling.
