## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys olive oil and wants it to taste the way Italian cooks expect it to. It describes how to read the label, which bottle to choose, and why it's worth keeping two oils: a sound everyday one and a better one for finishing.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Buy oil labelled extra virgin, not "pure", "light" or plain "olive oil". | Extra virgin is mechanically pressed and has passed chemical and tasting tests. The others are partly or fully refined, which strips out both faults and flavour. |
| Look for a harvest date and buy the most recent one, ideally within about 12 months of harvest. | Olive oil doesn't improve with age. Its fruity aromas and peppery antioxidants fade from the day it's pressed, and a best-before date says little about when that was. |
| Choose dark glass, a tin or a box, and skip clear bottles that have stood under shop lights. | Light speeds up oxidation, which is what turns oil stale and then rancid. |
| Check where the olives were grown, not just where the oil was bottled. | "Bottled in Italy" can mean a blend from several countries. A named country, region or a DOP tells you the oil's real origin. |
| Keep a reasonably priced, fresh extra virgin for cooking and a small bottle of a characterful one for raw use. | Heat dulls the finer aromas, so a premium oil is wasted in a long braise but shines drizzled on soup, bread or grilled vegetables. |

## Context

This pattern suits all the everyday uses of olive oil in Italian cooking: softening a [soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/), dressing salads, finishing soups and grilled food, and making pesto.

It does not suit deep-frying in large quantities, where cost and smoke point come into play (see [Deep-frying at home](../deep-frying-at-home/)), or baking where a neutral fat is wanted. Many Italian cooks do fry in extra virgin olive oil, but it's an expensive way to fill a pot.

There's a trade-off between price and flavour. A cheap extra virgin can be fine for cooking if it's fresh, while an expensive one can be stale if it's old. This pattern puts freshness first and spends more only on the oil you taste raw.

## Usage

**Reading the bottle:** turn it around and look for three things: the words extra virgin, a harvest date (sometimes written as the harvest season, such as "2025/26"), and the country of origin of the olives. Oils are pressed in autumn in both hemispheres, so a Southern Hemisphere oil is about six months fresher than a Northern Hemisphere one bought at the same time, and the other way around later in the year. Local oils, such as Australian ones, are often the freshest choice on the shelf. Producer associations and DOP seals add a check on top (see [Reading DOP, IGP and STG labels](../reading-dop-igp-labels/)).

**Tasting it:** pour a spoonful into a small glass and smell it. Good oil smells green and fresh, of cut grass, tomato leaf, artichoke or green apple. Sip it: a bitter edge and a peppery catch at the back of the throat are good signs of fresh oil, not faults. Waxy, stale-nut or crayon smells mean it's old (see [Storing olive oil](../storing-olive-oil/)).

**Matching oil to use:** milder, buttery oils (often from Liguria or the north) suit fish and delicate dishes. Robust, grassy oils (often from Tuscany, Puglia or Sicily) suit bean soups, bitter greens and grilled meat. Buy the finishing oil in a size you'll use within a couple of months of opening.
