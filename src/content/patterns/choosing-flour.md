## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who bakes or makes pasta from Italian recipes. It describes what the Italian flour grades actually measure, how to read protein and strength, and which local flours stand in well.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Read 00, 0, 1 and 2 as how finely the wheat is milled and sifted, not how strong it is. | The grades are set by ash (mineral) content: 00 is the whitest and finest, 2 keeps more bran. A 00 flour can be soft enough for cake or strong enough for long-rise pizza. |
| Choose by protein and, if listed, W (strength): about 9 to 11 % protein for egg pasta and short-rise doughs, about 12 to 13 % (W 250 to 320) for pizza fermented overnight or longer. | More protein makes more gluten. W measures how much stretch and resistance the dough has, which decides how long it can ferment without collapsing. |
| Use *semola rimacinata* (twice-milled durum wheat semolina) for eggless pasta and southern breads. | Durum gluten is firm rather than stretchy, which gives shapes such as orecchiette their bite and a golden colour. |
| Substitute with local flour by protein: plain or all-purpose for egg pasta, bread or baker's flour for pizza and bread. | Matching the protein matters far more than the 00 label, and a good local flour beats an old imported one. |

## Context

This pattern suits fresh egg pasta, eggless semolina pasta, pizza, focaccia and Italian breads.

It does not suit gluten-free baking or cakes and pastry, where a low-protein cake or plain flour is the right choice and the strength numbers don't apply. Note that 00 is a wheat flour, not a gluten-free one.

There's a trade-off between authenticity and availability. Imported Italian pizza flours print their protein and often W, and behave predictably in a hot oven. Local flours are cheaper and fresher but rarely list strength. This pattern buys an Italian flour for long-fermented pizza, where strength really matters, and uses good local flour for pasta and everyday bread.

## Usage

**For fresh egg pasta:** 00 gives the silkiest sheets, but plain flour works well, and many cooks mix in some semola for bite. Too strong a flour makes a dough that springs back when you roll it. See [Mixing egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/).

**For pizza and bread:** for a dough you'll ferment for a day or more in the fridge (see [Cold-fermenting dough](../cold-fermenting-dough/)), use a flour of about 12 to 13 % protein. Weak flour gives a dough that tears and spreads; very strong flour (W 350 and up) is meant for very long fermentations and can feel tight and rubbery in a short one. Type 1 and 2 flours add a nutty, wholesome flavour and absorb more water, so increase the [hydration](../dough-hydration/) a little. Weigh your flour rather than using cups (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)).

**For eggless pasta:** semola rimacinata is fine and pale yellow, like sand. Coarse semolina (for porridge or dusting) gives a crumbly dough, so check the word rimacinata. See [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/).

**Storing and safety:** keep flour airtight in a cool cupboard and use it within a few months; wholemeal types go stale sooner. Raw flour isn't a ready-to-eat food and has been linked to *E. coli* outbreaks, so don't eat raw dough or let children lick the bowl.
