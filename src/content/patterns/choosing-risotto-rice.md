## Summary

This pattern is for anyone shopping for rice to make risotto. It describes the three main Italian risotto varieties, what each does in the pan, and how to look after the rice so it gives you a creamy risotto with grains that still have bite.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose Carnaroli as your everyday risotto rice, especially while you're learning. | Its long, plump grain holds its shape through 16 to 18 minutes of cooking and forgives a minute too long, while still releasing plenty of starch for creaminess. |
| Use Arborio when it's all you can find, but taste it earlier and don't let it go past al dente. | Arborio releases its surface starch quickly, so it gets creamy fast but softens and loses its bite sooner than Carnaroli. |
| Pick Vialone Nano for loose, flowing risottos, such as those with vegetables or seafood. | Its small, round grain absorbs liquid and flavour well and gives a soft, wave-like risotto, which is why it's the rice of the Veneto. |
| Never rinse risotto rice. | The fine starch on the outside of the grain is what thickens the stock into a creamy sauce. Washing it off leaves you with rice in thin broth. |
| Allow about 80 g per person as a first course and 100 g as a main, and store the rest airtight in a cool, dark cupboard. | Those amounts give a generous plate without leftovers going to waste, and dry, sealed rice keeps for many months without picking up smells or pests. |

## Context

This pattern suits any risotto, from a plain parmesan one to saffron, mushroom or seafood versions, and it's a good guide for [arancini](../deep-frying-at-home/) and thick rice soups too.

It does not suit long-grain rice (basmati, jasmine), which stays separate and never turns creamy, or sushi and other Asian short-grain rice, which goes sticky rather than creamy.

There's a trade-off between tradition and availability. In northern Italy cooks argue happily about Carnaroli versus Vialone Nano. Outside Italy, Arborio is often the only option. This pattern favours Carnaroli for its forgiving texture, but a well-cooked Arborio risotto is far better than none, so use what you can get and adjust your timing.

## Usage

**Reading the packet:** look for the variety name, not just "risotto rice", which can be a blend or an unnamed variety. In Italy, Arborio and Carnaroli are graded *superfino* (the largest grains), and Vialone Nano *semifino*. Some Carnaroli is sold as "Carnaroli classico", which signals the original variety rather than a similar modern one.

**What the grain tells you:** good risotto rice has a chalky white core (the *perla*, or pearl) and a more translucent outside. The outside softens and gives up its starch, while the core keeps the bite. Grains should be whole, with few broken pieces, because broken bits cook to mush.

**Matching rice to dish:** Carnaroli suits almost everything. Vialone Nano suits seafood and spring vegetables, where you want a loose, delicate texture. Arborio works well for arancini, where a stickier risotto helps the balls hold together. Whatever you choose, follow the same method: [toast it](../toasting-rice-for-risotto/), [add the stock gradually](../adding-stock-to-risotto/) and [finish it with butter and cheese](../finishing-risotto/).

**Common mistakes:** rinsing the rice "to stop it sticking" (you want that starch), and keeping an open bag for years. Rice slowly goes stale, so buy what you'll use within a year and keep it in a sealed jar.
