## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks with tomatoes, which in Italian cooking means most people. It describes when fresh tomatoes are worth it, which tinned product suits which job, and what the San Marzano label really means.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use fresh tomatoes in summer, when they're ripe, heavy and smell of tomato at the stem, and good tinned ones the rest of the year. | Tomatoes for tinning are picked ripe and packed within a day or so, so a good tin beats a pale winter tomato that was picked green and travelled far. |
| Make whole peeled tomatoes (*pelati*) your default tin and crush them yourself. | Only the best, firmest fruit survives whole, and crushing by hand gives you the texture you want, from chunky to smooth. |
| Use passata (smooth, sieved tomato) for quick, silky sauces and soups, and chopped tomatoes or *polpa* (pulp) when you want small pieces without crushing. | Passata has no skins or seeds and cooks down fast; polpa holds its shape a little better. |
| Treat concentrate (paste) as a seasoning: a spoonful fried briefly in oil. | It's tomato cooked down to a dense, sweet, savoury paste, too strong to be the base of a sauce on its own. |
| Pay for San Marzano DOP only when tomato is the star, and check for the full name, the seals and a numbered tin. | Many tins say "San Marzano" in large letters but aren't the protected product, and a good ordinary plum tomato is often just as good in a long-cooked sauce. |

## Context

This pattern suits tomato sauces, ragù, soups, braises and pizza, and fresh tomatoes for salads and bruschetta.

It does not suit preserving your own tomatoes, which needs proper bottling and acidity guidance beyond this page, or green and unripe tomatoes, which are a different ingredient.

There's a trade-off between fresh and tinned. Fresh tomatoes in season give a brighter, lighter sauce and are irreplaceable raw. Tinned give a deeper, more reliable result for most of the year. This pattern uses fresh only when they're truly ripe and local, and is happy with tins the rest of the time; many Italian cooks do exactly the same.

## Usage

**Choosing fresh:** pick tomatoes that feel heavy, give slightly when pressed and smell sweet and leafy at the stem. Plum types (such as Roma) have more flesh and fewer seeds, so they suit sauces; round and heirloom tomatoes are best raw. Keep them at room temperature, stem side down, out of the sun; the fridge dulls their flavour and makes the flesh mealy. Use soft ones for sauce first. For skins and seeds, see [Peeling and seeding tomatoes](../peeling-and-seeding-tomatoes/).

**Reading a tin:** the ingredient list should say tomatoes, tomato juice or purée, and perhaps salt, basil or citric acid. Thick juice, a deep red colour and firm, whole fruit are good signs when you open it; watery pink liquid and broken, pale tomatoes are not. Taste a few brands in a [quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/) and stick with the one you like.

**San Marzano DOP:** the protected product is "Pomodoro San Marzano dell'Agro Sarnese-Nocerino DOP", grown in a small area near Naples and sold peeled, either whole or cut lengthwise into fillets. A genuine tin carries the EU DOP seal, the consortium's logo and an individual number. "San Marzano style" or "San Marzano variety" is just a plant type grown anywhere (see [Reading DOP, IGP and STG labels](../reading-dop-igp-labels/)).

**Leftovers:** once opened, tip unused tomatoes into a covered glass or plastic container, refrigerate and use within a few days, or freeze them.
