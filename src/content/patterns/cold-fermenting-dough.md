## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants better-tasting dough on their own schedule. It describes how to slow fermentation in the fridge for 24 to 72 hours, plan it backwards from the bake, and bring the dough back to life before shaping.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Ferment the dough in the fridge at 3 to 7 °C for 24 to 72 hours, with a low yeast amount (see [Using yeast and preferments](../using-yeast-and-preferments/)). | Cold slows the yeast far more than the flour's enzymes, which keep breaking starch into sugars and proteins into smaller pieces, giving more flavour, better browning and a lighter crust. |
| Give the dough an hour at room temperature before it goes into the fridge. | A short warm start gets the yeast going, so the dough doesn't sit dormant and then struggle to rise after its cold rest. |
| Store it in a lightly oiled, airtight container with room for the dough to double. | A sealed container stops a dry skin forming, and enough room lets it rise without bursting the lid. |
| Take the dough out 2 to 4 hours before you bake, until its centre is at least 18 °C. | Cold dough is stiff and springs back when you stretch it. Warmed dough relaxes, finishes rising and bakes up airy. |

## Context

This pattern suits pizza and focaccia doughs made with strong flour, and many bread doughs, especially when you bake on a weekend or for guests.

It does not suit enriched sweet doughs that need a warm, fast rise, or doughs made with weak flour, whose gluten can break down over a long fermentation and turn slack and sticky.

There's a trade-off between flavour and flexibility. Longer cold fermentation tastes better, up to about three days, but the dough is committed: past its peak it turns sour, weak and hard to shape. This pattern aims for 48 hours as a reliable middle. If you need pizza tonight, a same-day dough with more yeast still works, while 24 hours already gives most of the benefit.

## Usage

**Planning backwards:** pick the time you want to eat, then count back. For pizza at 7 pm on Saturday, mix the dough on Thursday evening, leave it an hour at room temperature, and refrigerate it. Ball it on Friday or on Saturday morning (see [Balling and proofing dough](../balling-and-proofing-dough/)), and take it out at about 3 or 4 pm on Saturday. In a warm kitchen in summer it needs less time out of the fridge, in winter more.

**Bulk or balls:** you can ferment the dough in one piece (bulk) and divide it a few hours before baking, or divide it into balls first and ferment them separately. Bulk fermentation is more forgiving and develops strength. Balls take less work on the day. Either way, keep the fridge door shut as much as possible: frequent opening warms the dough and speeds it up.

**Reading the dough:** after a day or two the dough should have grown by about half, with small bubbles visible on the sides and bottom of the container, and it should smell pleasantly sour, like yoghurt or beer. A dough that has collapsed, smells strongly of alcohol, and tears like wet paper has gone too far. Bake it as flatbread or focaccia rather than throwing it away. A dough that has barely moved needs longer at room temperature before shaping.
