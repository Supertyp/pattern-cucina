## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks meat at home. It describes how to use a probe thermometer to make sure meat is safe, following Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ), and how to hit the doneness you want in whole cuts.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a digital probe thermometer and check it in iced water (0 °C) now and then. | Colour, juices and cooking times are unreliable guides. A quick-reading thermometer tells you what's actually happening inside, and a check keeps it honest. |
| Cook all poultry (whole, pieces or mince), minced or rolled meat, sausages, burgers, meatballs and offal to at least 75 °C in the centre. | Bacteria on the surface get mixed or rolled into the middle of these foods, so the whole of the inside must get hot enough to kill them. |
| For other cooked meat, 70 °C in the centre is the general safe guide, and for vulnerable people, cook whole cuts to at least that. | That gives a safe margin for people most at risk of serious food poisoning. |
| For healthy adults, whole cuts of beef, veal and lamb can be served pink: about 52 °C rare, 55 to 57 °C medium-rare and 60 to 63 °C medium. | Bacteria on solid cuts sit on the outside, which a hot pan or oven kills, while the uncut centre is protected. |
| Take whole cuts off the heat 3 to 5 °C below your target, and probe the thickest part, away from bone and fat. | Heat keeps travelling inwards while the meat rests, and bone or fat gives a false reading. |

## Context

This pattern suits roasts, steaks, chops, poultry, sausages, meatballs, burgers and rolled cuts.

It does not suit long braises and stews, which are cooked far past any safe temperature and judged by tenderness instead (see [Braising meat](../braising-meat/)), or very thin cutlets that you can't probe.

There's a trade-off between eating quality and margin of safety. Chefs and many Italian cooks serve beef and lamb rare, and a [tagliata](../resting-meat/) is meant to be pink. Some official meat-industry guides recommend higher temperatures, for example 60 °C even for rare. This pattern gives the lower doneness temperatures for healthy adults, because whole cuts are low risk. Choose the higher figures, or well done, when serving anyone vulnerable. The 75 °C rule for poultry, mince and sausages is never optional.

## Usage

**Using the thermometer:** push the probe in from the side for steaks and chops, so the tip sits in the very centre. For a whole chicken, probe the thickest part of the thigh without touching bone. Wash the probe in hot soapy water after each reading of raw or partly cooked meat, so you don't carry bacteria back into food that's ready.

**Hitting your target:** start checking well before the recipe time. For steaks and roasts, take them off a few degrees early and rest them (see [Resting meat](../resting-meat/)). Pork whole cuts are commonly cooked to 65 to 70 °C, so they stay juicy with a faint blush. For poultry, sausages and mince, don't rely on resting: make sure the reading is 75 °C before it leaves the heat.

**What it looks like:** rare beef is deep red and soft, medium-rare rosy pink and springy, and medium pale pink and firmer. But colour misleads: mince and poultry can stay pink when safe and turn brown when they're not, which is why the number matters.

**Keeping it safe afterwards:** refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and reheat them until piping hot all the way through. Cooked meat left out for 2 to 4 hours must be eaten straight away, and after 4 hours, thrown out.
