## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking live mussels or clams. It describes how to steam them open in a covered pan with a little wine and garlic, take them out as they open so they stay plump, and keep the briny liquor for the sauce.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with live, cleaned shellfish: purged clams, debearded mussels, and none with broken shells or open shells that won't close when tapped. | Live shellfish are safe and sweet. Dead ones spoil fast, and grit left inside ends up in the sauce. |
| Use a wide pan with a lid, warm garlic (and chilli) in olive oil, add the shellfish and a splash of dry white wine (about 100 ml per kilo), and cover over high heat. | The shellfish cook in the steam from the wine and their own juices, and a wide pan keeps them in a shallow layer so they open evenly. |
| Shake the pan and lift out shells as they open, usually after 2 to 5 minutes. | The meat cooks in the time it takes to open, and every extra minute shrinks it and makes it rubbery. |
| Strain the liquor through a fine sieve lined with a paper towel or cloth. | The juices are full of flavour but may hold sand, which settles or gets caught in the filter. |
| Don't add salt until you've tasted the liquor. | Shellfish release seawater as they open, and the liquor is often salty enough already. |

## Context

This pattern suits mussels (*cozze*) and clams (*vongole*) for *impepata di cozze* (mussels with black pepper), *sauté di cozze*, steamed clams, and pasta or soups built on the liquor. For cleaning and purging before cooking, see [Cleaning mussels and clams](../cleaning-mussels-and-clams/).

It does not suit oysters and scallops, which are opened raw, or frozen and pre-cooked shellfish, which only need warming through in the sauce.

There's a trade-off between tradition and caution over shells that stay closed. The long-standing rule is to throw away any that don't open, and many cooks still follow it. Some sources now say a closed shell may just be stuck and is fine if it opens easily with a knife. This pattern stays with the cautious rule: if a shell is still firmly shut after the rest have opened and a couple more minutes of cooking, or the meat smells off, throw it away.

## Usage

**Before cooking:** buy shellfish from a reputable fishmonger, keep them cold in the fridge (in a bowl covered with a damp cloth, never sealed in plastic or soaked in tap water), and cook them within a day (see [Buying fresh fish and shellfish](../buying-fresh-seafood/)). They should smell of the sea, not fishy.

**Cooking:** warm sliced or crushed garlic in olive oil until fragrant but not brown (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)). Add the shellfish, turn the heat to high, pour in the wine and put the lid on at once. Within a minute or two steam escapes from the lid. Shake the pan, then check. Use tongs to move open shells to a bowl. For impepata, skip the wine and season with plenty of black pepper and lemon.

**Using the liquor:** pour the liquor through the lined sieve and leave behind the last spoonful in the pan, where sand collects. For pasta, cook it until 2 minutes short of done, then finish it in the strained liquor with oil and parsley so it absorbs the flavour, and add the shellfish back at the end (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)).

**Safety:** shellfish filter seawater and can carry viruses and bacteria. Pregnant women, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity should only eat shellfish thoroughly cooked. Eat cooked shellfish straight away, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and eat them the next day.
