## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who boils pasta. It describes how to cook it *al dente* ("to the tooth": tender with a slight bite), and how timing differs for dried, fresh and filled pasta.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Bring the water to a full rolling boil before the pasta goes in, then keep it boiling briskly with the lid off. | The pasta drops the water temperature. A strong boil brings it back fast, so the surface sets instead of turning sticky and soft. |
| Stir well in the first minute or two, and leave the oil out of the water. | The starch on the surface is gluey at first, which is when pasta sticks together. Oil floats on top, does little to stop sticking, and coats the drained pasta so the sauce slides off. |
| Start tasting about 2 minutes before the packet time, and drain when there's still a thin, firm core. | Packet times vary with the pot, stove and altitude. The pasta keeps softening after draining and in the sauce. |
| If you'll finish it in the sauce, drain 1 to 2 minutes earlier still. | The pasta finishes cooking in the pan as it absorbs the sauce, so it reaches al dente there rather than overcooking (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). |
| Time fresh egg pasta in minutes, not by the packet: about 1 to 3 minutes for thin ribbons, 3 to 5 for filled pasta. | Fresh pasta is already hydrated, so it cooks very quickly and goes from right to overcooked in a minute. |

## Context

This pattern suits dried durum wheat pasta of every shape, fresh egg pasta, and filled pasta cooked in boiling salted water (see [Salting the pasta water](../salting-pasta-water/)).

It does not suit pasta baked in the oven, which should be cooked well under al dente beforehand (see [Assembling baked pasta](../assembling-baked-pasta/)), or pasta for soup, which is cooked in the broth itself.

There's a trade-off between Italian bite and softer tastes. Al dente is the Italian standard, and well-made dried pasta (see [Choosing dried pasta](../choosing-dried-pasta/)) holds that texture best. Some people, including young children and older people, find it too firm. Cooking a minute or two longer is fine, as long as the pasta isn't swollen and falling apart. This pattern aims for al dente and lets you choose the last minute.

## Usage

**Setting up:** use a big pot with plenty of water and have the sauce, a colander or tongs, and a mug for scooping out pasta water ready before the pasta goes in (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)). Put a lid on to bring the water to the boil faster, then take it off once the pasta is in, so it doesn't boil over. Lower long pasta gently and push it under as it softens. Breaking spaghetti is frowned on in Italy, but it does no harm.

**Tasting for doneness:** fish out a piece, let it cool for a few seconds and bite. Raw pasta is hard and chalky. Al dente pasta is tender all through but resists slightly in the centre, and if you bite or cut it you'll see a thin, slightly paler line in the middle, without a white, floury core. Overcooked pasta is soft, swollen and slippery. Throwing it at the wall tells you nothing.

**Fresh and filled pasta:** have everything ready before fresh pasta goes in, because it's done in a few minutes. Ribbons are ready when they're tender but still springy; filled pasta when the folded edges are no longer tough. Use a gentler boil for filled pasta so it doesn't burst.

**Draining safely:** carry the pot to the sink with oven gloves, and tip it away from you so the steam doesn't hit your face, or lift the pasta out with tongs or a spider straight into the sauce. Scoop out a mug of the cooking water first (see [Saving the pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/)) and don't rinse it, unless it's for a cold pasta salad.
