## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking polenta from cornmeal. It describes the ratio of water to meal, how to add it without lumps, how long to cook it, and how to serve it soft or set it for grilling.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use about 1 litre of water to 250 g of cornmeal for soft polenta, and 1 litre to 300 g for polenta you'll set and slice, with about 10 g salt per litre. | The ratio sets the final texture, and salting the water is the only way to season the cornmeal evenly through. |
| Bring the water to a simmer and pour the cornmeal in slowly, like rain (*a pioggia*), whisking all the time. | Each grain gets wet on its own before it can stick to its neighbours, which is what prevents lumps. |
| Turn the heat to low and cook for 40 to 50 minutes for traditional coarse meal, stirring every few minutes with a wooden spoon. | The starch needs time to swell and soften fully. Undercooked polenta tastes raw and gritty, even when it looks thick. |
| Keep a kettle of hot water nearby and loosen the polenta whenever it gets too stiff to stir. | Polenta keeps thickening as it cooks, and topping it up keeps it smooth and stops it catching on the base. |
| Partly cover the pot and stand back when you stir. | Thick polenta bubbles up and spits drops that stick to skin and burn badly. |

## Context

This pattern suits yellow or white cornmeal for polenta, coarse (*bramata*) or medium, served soft with stews, [braises](../braising-meat/), [ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/), sausages, mushrooms or melting cheese, or set and grilled.

It does not suit instant or pre-cooked polenta, which is ready in about 5 minutes (follow the packet), or fine cornmeal for baking.

There's a trade-off between tradition and convenience. Traditional coarse polenta takes the best part of an hour and gives a deeper corn flavour and a pleasing grain. Instant polenta is ready in minutes but tastes flatter and goes gluey sooner. This pattern takes the slow route, which is mostly hands-off once the cornmeal is in, and instant is a fair choice on a weeknight.

## Usage

**Getting it in without lumps:** use a heavy pot with plenty of room. Pour the cornmeal from a jug or your hand in a thin, steady stream while whisking in one direction. Once it thickens, after 2 to 3 minutes, swap the whisk for a long wooden spoon. If you get a few lumps, press them against the side of the pot.

**The long cook:** keep it at a lazy, occasional "plop". Stir every 5 minutes or so, scraping the base and sides. It's ready when it tastes sweet and soft, not gritty, and pulls away from the sides of the pot as you stir. For soft polenta, stir in butter and grated Parmigiano at the end, or a splash of milk for a creamier result, and serve straight away, because it firms up within minutes.

**Setting and grilling:** pour the firmer polenta onto a wet board or an oiled tray in a layer 1.5 to 2 cm thick and let it cool and set, a couple of hours or overnight in the fridge. Cut it into slices or squares, brush with oil and grill on a hot ridged pan (see [Grilling vegetables](../grilling-vegetables/)), or fry until crisp.

**Safety and mistakes:** never lean over bubbling polenta, use a long-handled spoon and an oven glove, and keep children away. The usual mistakes are dumping the cornmeal in at once, cooking it on high heat (it catches and spits), and stopping after 15 minutes because it looks thick.
