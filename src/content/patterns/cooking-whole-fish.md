## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking a fish whole, on the bone. It describes how to prepare it, bake it plainly or in a salt crust, tell when it's done at the spine and fillet it neatly to serve.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Have the fish scaled, gutted and the gills removed, then rinse the cavity and pat it dry. | Scales are unpleasant to eat, and gills and blood along the spine taste bitter. A dry skin crisps and browns. |
| Score the thickest part 2 or 3 times on each side, down to the bone. | Heat reaches the thick middle sooner, so it cooks at the same pace as the thin tail. |
| Bake at 200 to 220 °C (180 to 200 °C fan), allowing roughly 10 minutes per 2.5 cm of thickness at the thickest point. | A hot oven cooks the fish before it dries out. Thickness, not weight, decides the time. |
| Check doneness at the spine: the flesh should be opaque and lift cleanly off the bone. | The flesh next to the backbone is the last to cook, so if it's done there, it's done everywhere. |
| Fillet it by lifting the top fillets off the spine, then lifting out the whole skeleton from the tail. | The bones come out in one piece, so the bottom fillets reach the plate nearly bone-free. |

## Context

This pattern suits whole fish of about 500 g to 2 kg, such as snapper, bream, sea bass (*branzino*), barramundi, trout or mullet, baked *al forno* with lemon and herbs, or *al sale* (in a salt crust).

It does not suit very large fish better cut into steaks, fillets, which cook in minutes in a pan, or fish for raw or cured dishes. For choosing a fresh fish, see [Buying fresh fish and shellfish](../buying-fresh-seafood/).

There's a trade-off between convenience and flavour. Fillets are quicker and boneless, but cooking on the bone keeps the fish moister and tastier, and the skin protects it. This pattern takes the whole fish for a shared meal. Choose fillets for a quick weeknight dinner or for guests who don't want to deal with bones.

## Usage

**Preparing:** ask the fishmonger to scale and gut the fish. At home, scrape the scales off from tail to head with the back of a knife, inside a bag to catch them. Snip out the red gills with scissors and rinse out the blood line along the spine. For a salt crust, you can leave the scales on, since the skin is peeled off before serving. Use a sharp knife on a stable board (see [Sharp knife, stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/)).

**Baking:** rub the fish with olive oil and salt inside and out, and fill the cavity with lemon slices, parsley, garlic or fennel. Lay it on baking paper so it doesn't stick. For a salt crust, mix about 1 kg coarse salt with 2 egg whites and a little water per kilo of fish until it feels like damp sand. Pack it over the fish on a layer of salt, and bake for 25 to 35 minutes for a 1 kg fish. Crack the crust at the table and brush away the salt. The fish steams gently inside and isn't salty.

**Knowing when it's done:** push a knife or fork into the thickest part next to the spine. Done flesh is opaque white and flakes, and the eye turns white. A thermometer near the bone should read about 55 to 60 °C. For pregnant women, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity, cook it to 63 °C. If it's not there yet, give it 5 more minutes.

**Filleting at the table:** run a knife along the line down the middle of the side, then from head to tail along the back. Ease the top fillets off the bones in two pieces. Lift the tail and peel the skeleton and head away in one go, then serve the bottom fillets. Put out a plate for bones.
