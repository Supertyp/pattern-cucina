## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who adds wine to a savoury dish or dessert. It describes which wine to use, how to cook it so the harsh alcohol taste disappears, and how white, red and sweet wines each change a dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a dry, unoaked wine you'd be happy to drink, and avoid bottles sold as "cooking wine". | Cooking concentrates whatever is in the wine, so faults and sourness get stronger. Commercial cooking wine usually contains added salt and preservatives. |
| Add wine early and let it bubble until the sharp alcohol smell has gone, usually 1 to 3 minutes, before adding other liquid. | Simmering drives off the harsh edge and reduces the wine to its flavour. Adding stock too soon traps a raw, boozy taste. |
| Use white wine for fish, poultry, risotto and pale sauces, and red for red meat and dark ragù. | White adds acidity without colouring the dish. Red adds colour, tannin and depth that stand up to beef, lamb and game. |
| Use sweet wines such as marsala or vin santo only where you want sweetness, mostly in desserts and a few classic savoury dishes. | Their sugar and nutty, caramel flavours are part of dishes like zabaglione or veal marsala, but would unbalance an ordinary sauce. |

## Context

This pattern suits risotto, braised meat (see [Braising meat](../braising-meat/)), ragù, pan sauces, mussels and clams steamed in wine (see [Cooking mussels and clams](../cooking-mussels-and-clams/)) and desserts made with sweet wine.

It does not suit people who must avoid alcohol completely, such as anyone pregnant, recovering from alcohol dependence or avoiding it for religious reasons. Cooking does not remove all of it: research summaries suggest about 40 % remains after 15 minutes of simmering and around 5 % after two to two and a half hours. For them, use stock with a squeeze of lemon or a dash of vinegar instead.

There's a trade-off between quality and economy. Expensive wine is wasted in a braise, but very cheap, sour or oxidised wine spoils it. This pattern takes the middle road: an inexpensive, sound, dry wine that you'd drink, and if a recipe needs only half a glass, the rest can be served with the meal or kept, sealed in the fridge, for cooking within a few days. Dry vermouth keeps for months in the fridge and is a handy stand-in for white wine.

## Usage

**Adding it at the right moment:** wine usually goes in after browning meat or softening the soffritto, when the pan is hot. Pour it in and scrape up the browned bits; that technique is covered in [Deglazing with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/). For risotto, add it after toasting the rice and let it be absorbed before the first ladle of stock (see [Toasting rice for risotto](../toasting-rice-for-risotto/)). Step back as you pour into a hot pan, because the steam is fierce, and if you cook on gas, take the pan off the flame while you pour so alcohol vapour can't catch.

**Knowing when it's cooked off:** lean over (carefully) and smell. At first the steam smells sharp and stings the nose; once it smells of fruit and the bubbling has calmed, move on. The liquid should be reduced by roughly half for a pan sauce.

**How much to use:** a splash to half a glass (about 60 to 125 ml) flavours a risotto or sauce for four, and a braise for six may take a glass or two (150 to 300 ml) alongside stock or tomato. Too much makes the dish sour; you can balance it with a knob of butter, a longer simmer or more stock.

**Sweet wines:** dry marsala and vin santo can also be reduced into pan sauces for veal or pork, but most often they flavour desserts: zabaglione, soaked biscuits and fruit poached in wine. Use these only where the recipe asks for them.
