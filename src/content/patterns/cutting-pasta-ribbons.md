## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cutting fresh egg pasta into ribbons. It describes when to cut, how wide to go and how to keep the strands separate until they hit the water.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Let the sheet dry for 10 to 20 minutes, until it feels like leather: dry to the touch but still flexible. | A sheet that's still soft sticks to itself when rolled up, while one that's brittle cracks along the folds. |
| Dust the sheet with semolina, roll it up loosely from the short end into a flat log and cut straight down with a sharp knife. | The dusting keeps the layers apart, and a clean downward cut (no sawing) gives neat, even edges. |
| Cut to the width the dish needs: about 2 to 3 mm for tagliolini, 6 to 8 mm for tagliatelle and fettuccine, and 2 to 3 cm for pappardelle. | Width changes how much sauce each ribbon carries; wider ribbons suit heavier sauces (see [Matching pasta shape to sauce](../matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce/)). |
| Unroll the ribbons straight away, toss them with a little more semolina and coil them into loose nests. | Cut ribbons pressed together in the log fuse within minutes, and nests are easy to portion and cook. |

## Context

This pattern suits egg pasta ribbons cut by hand or with the cutting rollers of a pasta machine, from fine tagliolini to wide pappardelle.

It does not suit filled pasta, which is cut and sealed while the sheet is still soft (see [Shaping and sealing filled pasta](../shaping-filled-pasta/)), or lasagne sheets, which are simply cut into rectangles.

There's a trade-off between knife and machine. Hand-cut ribbons are slightly irregular and have a rustic look and texture that many cooks prize, and in Bologna tagliatelle are traditionally cut by hand (a width of about 8 mm once cooked was registered with the city's chamber of commerce in 1972). A machine cutter is faster and perfectly even, but only offers fixed widths and can crush a sheet that's too wet. This pattern prefers the knife because it works for every width. Use the machine when you're cutting a lot of the standard sizes.

## Usage

**Getting the timing right:** lay the rolled sheets on a dusted cloth or tray and turn them once. Touch the surface: when it no longer feels tacky but the sheet still bends without cracking, it's ready. In a dry or warm kitchen this can take under 10 minutes, so check early. If a sheet has gone brittle, it can't be rescued for ribbons; break it into rough pieces (*maltagliati*, badly cut) for soup.

**Cutting:** dust the sheet, fold or roll it loosely into a flat log about 6 to 8 cm wide, and use a large, sharp knife on a stable board (see [Sharp knife, stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/)). Press straight down, then lift; don't drag the blade. Keep your guiding hand in a claw so your fingertips stay behind the knuckles. For pappardelle, a fluted pastry wheel along a ruler gives a pretty edge.

**Opening and nesting:** slide the flat of the knife under the cut log, lift and shake so the ribbons fall open, or pick up a few at a time from the middle and let them unfurl. Toss with semolina, then wrap a portion (about 100 g) around your fingers into a loose nest and set it on a dusted tray. Don't pile nests on top of each other. Cook within a few hours, or freeze or dry them (see [Drying and storing fresh pasta](../drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta/)).
