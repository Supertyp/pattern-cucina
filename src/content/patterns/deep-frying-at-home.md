## Summary

This pattern is for anyone frying food in a pan of hot oil at home. It describes how to fry safely and well: the right oil and pan, a thermometer at 170 to 180 °C, small batches, proper draining, what to do if oil catches fire and how to get rid of the oil afterwards.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a deep, heavy saucepan filled no more than a third full, or a thermostat-controlled electric fryer, and never leave hot oil unattended. | Oil bubbles up when food goes in, and a third full leaves room for that. A thermostat stops oil overheating, the usual cause of kitchen fires. |
| Choose an oil with a high smoke point: peanut, sunflower, rice bran or regular olive oil. | They stay stable at frying temperatures. If oil smokes, it's breaking down and getting close to catching fire. |
| Keep the oil at 170 to 180 °C with a thermometer, and fry in small batches. | Too cool and the food soaks up oil, too hot and it burns before the inside cooks. Each batch cools the oil, so smaller batches recover faster. |
| Dry the food well, lower it in gently with a slotted spoon or spider, and drain it on a rack or paper towels. | Water makes hot oil spit and splash. Draining stops the crust going soggy. |
| If oil catches fire, turn off the heat and cover the pan with a lid or fire blanket. Never use water or move the pan. If it doesn't go out, get out and call 000. | Water turns to steam instantly and throws burning oil across the room. Without air, the fire goes out. |

## Context

This pattern suits Italian *fritti* (fried foods): *arancini* (stuffed rice balls), *fritto misto* (mixed fried seafood and vegetables), calamari, *crocchette*, *fiori di zucca* (zucchini flowers), and breaded cutlets or eggplant fried in a deep layer of oil.

It does not suit cooking with a tablespoon or two of oil in a pan, which is sautéing (see [Pan-frying thin cutlets and making a pan sauce](../pan-frying-thin-cutlets/)), or air fryers and oven "frying", which work differently.

There's a trade-off between a pan and an electric fryer. Southern Italian cooks often fry in a deep pan of olive oil on the stove, which is traditional and flexible. An electric fryer with a thermostat is safer and holds its temperature better. This pattern works with either, but if you fry often, choose the fryer.

## Usage

**Setting up:** use a back burner with the handle turned in, and keep children and pets away. Clip a thermometer to the side of the pan. Have a lid or fire blanket, a spider and a draining tray ready before you start (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)).

**Frying:** coat the food just before it goes in (see [Pounding and breading cutlets](../pounding-and-breading-cutlets/)). Lower each piece away from you. It should sizzle steadily straight away. Fry arancini at about 170 °C for 3 to 5 minutes so the centre gets hot, and small seafood at 180 °C for 1 to 2 minutes (see [Cooking squid and prawns](../cooking-squid-and-prawns/)). Let the oil recover between batches, and salt the food as it comes out.

**Signs of trouble:** greasy, pale food means the oil was too cool or the pan too full. Dark outside and raw inside means it's too hot. If the oil smokes, turn off the heat and let it cool.

**Reusing and disposing of oil:** let the oil cool completely, which takes hours. Strain it, store it sealed in a dark cupboard and reuse it two or three times for similar foods, until it's dark, smells stale or foams. Never pour oil down the sink or toilet, where it sets and blocks pipes. Seal small amounts in a container for the bin, and take larger amounts to a council collection point.
