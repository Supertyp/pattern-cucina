## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who browns food and then builds a sauce in the same pan. It describes how to use wine to lift the browned crust into the sauce and cook off the raw alcohol taste before the main liquid goes in.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pour in the wine while the pan is still hot after browning, about 100 to 150 ml for a family-sized pot. | The hot pan makes the wine boil on contact, and the bubbling liquid dissolves the browned crust (the fond), which is concentrated flavour. |
| Scrape the bottom with a wooden spoon or flat spatula as it bubbles. | The fond loosens as it softens, and scraping makes sure all of it ends up in the sauce rather than burning onto the pan. |
| Let it bubble until the sharp alcohol smell has gone and the wine has almost evaporated, before adding other liquid. | Italian cooks call this *sfumare* (letting it evaporate). Raw wine tastes harsh, and reducing it leaves its acidity and fruit behind. |
| Only deglaze a brown crust, never a black one. | Burnt bits taste bitter and acrid, and wine will carry that bitterness through the whole dish. |

## Context

This pattern suits ragù, braises such as ossobuco and brasato, risotto (where the wine goes in after toasting the rice), and quick pan sauces after frying cutlets or sausages.

It does not suit sauces where wine is meant to stay a main liquid for long, slow cooking, such as meat braised in a whole bottle (see [Braising meat](../braising-meat/)), or dishes where wine isn't wanted at all. Which wine to choose, and cooking with fortified and sweet wines, is covered in [Cooking with wine](../cooking-with-wine/).

There's a trade-off between wine and other liquids. Wine adds acidity and aroma, but stock, water or even a splash of the tomato will lift the fond just as well, without alcohol. This pattern uses wine because it adds flavour that balances rich meat. Choose stock or water when cooking for someone avoiding alcohol, and remember that some alcohol remains even after long cooking.

## Usage

**Timing and quantities:** have the wine measured out before you start browning (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)). As soon as the meat is browned, add the wine all at once. It should sizzle and steam immediately. Use a dry wine you'd happily drink: white for lighter meats, fish and most Bolognese-style ragù, red for beef, game and richer braises. Roughly 100 to 150 ml is enough to deglaze a pot of ragù for six, and a few tablespoons are enough for a pan sauce for two.

**What it looks, sounds and smells like:** the hiss quickly settles to a lively bubble. The brown crust lifts as you scrape, colouring the liquid. At first the steam smells sharp and boozy. After 2 to 4 minutes it smells fruity and winey instead, and the liquid has reduced to a few spoonfuls of syrupy glaze. That's the moment to add tomato, stock or milk.

**Safety:** take the pan off the heat when you pour, especially over a gas flame, because alcohol vapour can catch and flare. Pour from a jug or glass, never straight from the bottle, and keep your face back from the first burst of steam. If it does flame, stay calm and slide a lid over the pan. In a non-stick pan, use a wooden or silicone spoon to protect the coating.
