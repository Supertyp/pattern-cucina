## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who prepare the onion, carrot and celery base of many Italian dishes. It describes how to cut each vegetable into a fine, even dice quickly and safely, and how to choose the size for the dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Cut all three vegetables to the same size. | Even pieces soften at the same rate, so nothing burns while the rest catches up. |
| Choose the size by the dish: about 2 to 3 mm for ragù and smooth sauces, about 5 mm for most sauces and braises, about 1 cm for soups such as minestrone. | Fine dice melts into a long-cooked sauce, while larger pieces keep their shape and texture in a soup where you see and taste them. |
| Halve the onion through the root and leave the root on while you cut. | The root holds the layers together, so the onion doesn't fall apart as you dice it. |
| Turn carrot and celery into flat planks, then sticks, then dice. | Flat pieces sit still on the board and can't roll under the knife, and sticks of equal width give equal cubes. |
| Start with roughly 2 parts onion to 1 carrot and 1 celery, or equal parts. | Onion gives sweetness and body, carrot adds sweetness and colour, and celery a fresh, savoury note. Recipes and regions vary, so follow the dish. |

## Context

This pattern suits the *soffritto* (onion, carrot and celery gently cooked in fat) and its raw, chopped stage, the *battuto*, which start ragù, braises, bean soups and many risotti. Cooking them is covered in [Building a soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/).

It does not suit dishes that want sliced onion, such as a slow onion sauce, or rough chunks that will be strained out of a stock.

There's a trade-off between knife and machine. A food processor is quick for big batches, but it bruises the vegetables, gives uneven pieces and turns them watery if you run it too long, so they stew rather than soften. This pattern prefers a knife (or a *mezzaluna*, the curved rocking blade) for everyday amounts. For a big pot of ragù, pulsing roughly cut pieces in short bursts is a reasonable shortcut.

## Usage

**Onion:** cut off the top, halve through the root, peel, and lay each half flat. Make thin vertical cuts from just short of the root to the tip, the spacing equal to the dice you want. Optionally make one or two horizontal cuts towards the root. Then slice across, keeping your fingers in the claw grip (see [Sharp knife, stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/)). Discard the root end. A sharp knife also means fewer tears, because it crushes fewer cells.

**Carrot and celery:** peel the carrot, cut it into lengths of about 6 cm, slice a thin piece off one side so it sits flat, then cut it into planks, the planks into sticks, and the sticks across into cubes. For celery, pull the tough strings from the outer stalks with a peeler, split each stalk lengthwise into strips, then cut across. The pale inner stalks and leaves are tender and full of flavour, so use them too.

**Getting it fine:** for a ragù-fine dice, cut as above and then run the knife over the pile, rocking it, until the pieces are about 2 to 3 mm. Don't keep chopping until it becomes a wet paste. Collect the three vegetables in one bowl, since they usually go into the pan together.

**Planning ahead:** diced soffritto keeps for a day or two covered in the fridge, and freezes well in portions for a few months. Italian supermarkets sell it ready-diced and frozen for the same reason. Add it to the pan straight from frozen over low heat.
