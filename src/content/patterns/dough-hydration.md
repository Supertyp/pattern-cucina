## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making dough at home. It describes how to express water as a percentage of the flour, choose that percentage for the style you're making, and adjust it for your flour and kitchen.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Weigh everything and write hydration as water divided by flour: 650 g water to 1 kg flour is 65 %. | Baker's percentages scale to any batch size and let you compare recipes at a glance, which cups and spoons can't do (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). |
| Use 58 to 65 % for pizza stretched by hand, and start at about 60 % if you're new to it. | A firmer dough holds its shape on the bench and on the peel. Traditional Neapolitan dough sits around 55 to 62 %. |
| Use 70 to 80 % for tray-baked focaccia and pan pizza. | Extra water makes steam in the oven, which opens a lighter, airier crumb, and the tray holds the slack dough in shape. |
| Use strong flour (12 % protein or more) for doughs above about 68 %, and add salt at 2.5 to 3 % of the flour. | Strong flour forms more gluten, which traps gas and holds a wet dough together. Salt tightens the gluten and seasons the bread. |

## Context

This pattern suits yeasted doughs for pizza, focaccia and Italian breads, made by hand or in a mixer.

It does not suit fresh pasta dough, which is much drier and measured by eggs (see [Mixing egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/)), or pastry such as pasta frolla.

There's a trade-off between lightness and handling. Wetter doughs bake lighter, with bigger holes and a crisper crust, but they stick, spread and punish rough handling. Drier doughs are easy to shape but can bake dense and bready. This pattern starts moderate for hand-stretched pizza and goes wetter only where a tray does the shaping. Raise the hydration 2 % at a time as your handling improves.

## Usage

**Working out the numbers:** start from the flour weight. For four 250 g pizzas at 62 % hydration you need roughly 600 g flour, 372 g water, 17 g salt and a small amount of yeast (see [Using yeast and preferments](../using-yeast-and-preferments/)). Weigh the water too, because 1 ml of water weighs 1 g. If a recipe uses olive oil, it softens the dough, so count it as part of the liquid when you compare doughs.

**Adjusting for flour and season:** flours absorb different amounts of water. Wholemeal and strong bread flours drink more than fine soft 00 or plain flour (see [Choosing flour for pasta, pizza and bread](../choosing-flour/)). On humid days or in summer, hold back a little water, as the dough will feel stickier and ferment faster. In cold, dry weather, you may need a little more. Add the last 5 to 10 % of the water gradually, and keep notes so you can repeat a dough you like.

**Reading the dough:** a 60 % dough feels smooth and slightly tacky, and holds a ball. At 70 % it slumps and sticks to dry hands. Wet hands or a dough scraper make it manageable, and folds every 30 minutes build strength without kneading. If the dough tears when stretched, it needs more rest, not less water. If it spreads into a puddle, use less water next time or a stronger flour.
