## Summary

This pattern is for anyone serving a simple green or mixed salad (*insalata*) with an Italian meal. It describes the everyday Italian way: no bottled dressing and no whisked vinaigrette, just salt, oil and vinegar added straight to dry leaves at the last moment.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Wash the leaves and dry them completely, ideally in a salad spinner. | Oil can't cling to wet leaves, so the dressing slides off and pools at the bottom, and the water dilutes the flavour. |
| Dress in a large bowl just before it goes to the table, never ahead. | Salt and vinegar draw water out of the leaves within minutes, so a dressed salad turns limp and watery. |
| Use salt, extra virgin olive oil and wine vinegar (or lemon), roughly 3 to 4 parts oil to 1 part vinegar. | Italians want the oil's flavour to lead and the vinegar to lift it, not the sharp, acid-forward balance of many bottled dressings. |
| Toss well after each addition, with hands or two servers, until every leaf is just shiny. | Thorough tossing coats each leaf thinly, so you need less dressing and nothing is left soggy at the bottom. |

## Context

This pattern suits leafy salads and simple mixed salads with tomato, cucumber, fennel, carrot or radish, served as a *contorno* (side dish), usually with or after the main course (see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/)).

It does not suit composed salads that are dressed ahead on purpose, such as bread salad (see [Using day-old bread](../using-day-old-bread/)), bean salads or rice salads, nor salads built around a creamy dressing.

There's a trade-off between dressing in the bowl and a pre-mixed vinaigrette. Adding salt, oil and vinegar separately is the everyday Italian habit and lets you adjust each one by taste, but it needs a last-minute moment at the table. A jar of oil, vinegar and salt shaken together is easier to make ahead for a crowd or a picnic. This pattern takes the Italian side for everyday meals and accepts a pre-mixed dressing when you're catering for many.

## Usage

**Getting the order right:** the order is disputed. One Italian saying asks for a miser with the vinegar, a wise person with the salt and a spendthrift with the oil. Many cooks put the salt on first, because it dissolves on damp leaves or in vinegar but not in oil, then add the oil, then the vinegar last, so the oil coats the leaves before the acid reaches them. Others add the vinegar with the salt first. Both work if you toss well; what matters is tasting a leaf and adjusting.

**Getting the amounts right:** for a family salad of about 4 big handfuls of leaves, start with a good pinch of salt, about 3 tablespoons of [extra virgin olive oil](../choosing-extra-virgin-olive-oil/) and 2 to 3 teaspoons of red or white wine vinegar. Lemon juice is lovely with rocket, fennel or seafood. A little balsamic vinegar is common in some homes, but use it sparingly, as its sweetness can take over. Pepper is optional, and garlic, cheese and herbs aren't part of an everyday dressing. Taste a leaf: it should taste of good oil and be bright, not puckering (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)).

**Fixing common problems:** if the salad is sharp, add more oil and a few more leaves; if it's flat, a pinch of salt fixes it more often than more vinegar. If dressing collects in the bottom of the bowl, the leaves were wet or there was too much. In Italian restaurants the salad often arrives undressed with oil, vinegar and salt on the table, so you dress your own portion the same way.
