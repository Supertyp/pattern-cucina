## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who makes fresh pasta ahead of time. It describes how to hold it for a few hours, freeze it for later and dry it for the pantry, and which method suits which pasta.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Best of all, cook fresh pasta the same day, and keep it on a semolina-dusted tray or cloth in a single layer, loosely covered, until then. | Fresh pasta is at its best within hours. Spread out and dusted, the pieces don't touch, so they can't stick together. |
| To keep it longer, freeze it: spread it on a dusted tray, freeze until hard (1 to 2 hours), then bag it and use it within about a month. | Freezing pieces separately stops them fusing into a block, and it's the safest way to keep pasta made with egg or a perishable filling. |
| Cook frozen pasta straight from the freezer, adding a minute or two to the cooking time. | Thawing makes the surface wet and sticky, so the pieces clump and tear. |
| Dry only egg ribbon nests and eggless semolina shapes, in a cool, airy place, until completely brittle, then store them airtight. | Fully dried pasta has too little water for microbes to grow, but filled pasta and gnocchi can't dry evenly and spoil from the inside. |
| Don't keep fresh pasta in the fridge for days, and don't pile it in a heap. | In the fridge the moisture makes it soggy, grey and mouldy. Piled up, the weight presses the pieces together into a lump. |

## Context

This pattern suits homemade egg ribbons, filled pasta, eggless semolina shapes and potato gnocchi, made a few hours to a few weeks before you cook them.

It does not suit bought fresh pasta in sealed packs, which should be kept as the label says, or cooked pasta, which is leftovers and should be cooled quickly and refrigerated like any other cooked food.

There's a trade-off between drying and freezing. Drying is the traditional way to keep pasta in Italian homes and needs no freezer space, but it depends on the weather: in a humid kitchen in summer, pasta may stay soft for too long and start to spoil. Freezing works every time. This pattern makes freezing the default and keeps drying for egg ribbons and eggless shapes in dry, cool conditions.

## Usage

**Holding for a few hours:** after shaping, toss the pasta with semolina and spread it out on a tray or clean cloth. Ribbons go into loose nests (see [Cutting tagliatelle and pappardelle](../cutting-pasta-ribbons/)), filled pasta and gnocchi in a single layer, not touching. Cover loosely with a tea towel. Pasta made with egg or a fresh filling is perishable: food safety guidance is to keep perishable food at 5 °C or colder and not to leave it at room temperature for more than a couple of hours. If it'll be longer than that, put the tray in the fridge, uncovered or loosely covered, and cook it the same day.

**Freezing:** open-freeze the tray until the pieces are hard and rattle, then tip them into a labelled freezer bag or container and press out the air. Nests freeze well too. For filled pasta, freeze it soon after shaping, before the filling has a chance to make the pasta damp (see [Shaping and sealing filled pasta](../shaping-filled-pasta/)). Gnocchi are best frozen raw on the tray straight after shaping (see [Making potato gnocchi](../potato-gnocchi/)). Drop them frozen into plenty of boiling water.

**Drying:** hang ribbons over a pasta rack, a clean broom handle or a coat hanger, or lay nests on a cloth-lined rack, and turn them now and then. Thin pasta usually dries in 12 to 24 hours; eggless shapes such as orecchiette dry well spread out on a tray (see [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/)). It's ready when it snaps cleanly with no bendy core. If anything smells sour or shows mould, throw it out. Dried pasta takes a few minutes longer to cook than fresh.
