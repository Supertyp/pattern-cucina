## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants to eat pasta neatly and in the way most Italians do. It describes how to twirl long pasta with just a fork, what to do with short shapes and soups, and which habits raise eyebrows at an Italian table.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Eat pasta with a fork only, and leave the knife alone. | Long pasta is made long on purpose. Cutting it is seen as a faux pas in Italy, and it turns a clean forkful into a messy pile of short ends. |
| Twirl long pasta against the edge or base of the plate, catching just 3 or 4 strands at a time. | A few strands make a compact, bite-sized bundle. A big tangle won't wrap tightly and leaves strands dangling and dripping sauce. |
| Treat a spoon as optional, not standard. | Many Italians see twirling into a spoon as unnecessary or a little childish, though others find it elegant. The plate does the same job. |
| Pick up short shapes such as penne or rigatoni on the tines, and use a spoon only for pasta in broth. | Short shapes are designed to be speared or scooped with a fork, while *pasta in brodo* (pasta in broth) is a soup and needs a spoon. |

## Context

This pattern suits everyday family meals, dinner parties and restaurants, with any pasta served as a *primo* (first course). It's also the way to set an example for children once they're ready to learn the twirl.

It does not suit very young children, for whom cutting pasta is perfectly accepted, even in Italy. It also doesn't cover baked pasta such as lasagne, which you eat with a fork and, if needed, the side of the fork to cut it, or filled pasta, which you can halve with the fork.

There's a trade-off between custom and comfort. Twirling with a fork alone takes a little practice, and a spoon or a knife feels easier at first. This pattern follows the Italian custom because it's neater once learnt and avoids awkward moments abroad. If you're still learning, or a guest struggles, a short shape is the friendly choice (see [Matching pasta shape to sauce](../matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce/)), and at home nobody should be made to feel bad about a spoon.

## Usage

**The twirl:** use the tines of your fork to separate a few strands from the edge of the pile, not from the middle where they're tangled. Hold the fork at a slight angle with the tips resting on the plate, and turn it steadily so the strands wrap around. When it's right, you get a tight little parcel with at most one short end. If a long strand hangs off, lift the fork slightly and keep turning, or start again with fewer strands. A shallow pasta bowl or a plate with a rim makes this much easier, which is why Italians warm and use them (see [Setting the table](../setting-the-italian-table/)).

**Sauce and mess:** bring the plate to you rather than leaning far over it, and keep your napkin on your lap. A well-made pasta dish has sauce clinging to the pasta, not pooled underneath, so there's less to splash. Don't slurp the last strand in; if it won't wrap, bite it off cleanly.

**Finishing the plate:** once the pasta is gone, a little sauce often remains. Wiping it up with bread is the habit known as *fare la scarpetta*, and when it's acceptable is covered in [Bread at the Italian table](../bread-at-the-italian-table/). Whether to ask for cheese is covered in [When to add cheese](../cheese-on-pasta/).

**Serving the right amount:** an over-full plate is hard to eat neatly whatever your technique. Italian portions of pasta as a first course are modest, so see [Portioning pasta](../portioning-pasta/) before you serve.
