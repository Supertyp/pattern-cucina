## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making fresh egg pasta from scratch. It describes the ratio, the mixing method and how to adjust the dough by feel so it's firm, smooth and easy to roll.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with 100 g of flour per large egg (about 55 to 60 g of egg out of the shell), weighed on a scale. | The egg is the only liquid, so the ratio by weight decides the texture. Cups and "a large egg" vary too much to be reliable (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). |
| Use Italian 00 or plain flour, or swap up to a third of it for fine semolina (*semola rimacinata*). | Soft wheat flour gives silky, tender pasta, and a little durum semolina adds bite and makes the dough easier to handle (see [Choosing flour](../choosing-flour/)). |
| Make a well (*fontana*, fountain) in the flour, beat the eggs inside it with a fork, and draw the flour in from the inner wall. | The egg gradually thickens into a batter and then a shaggy dough without running across the board, and you can stop adding flour when the dough feels right. |
| Adjust at the end: wet hands or a teaspoon of water if it's crumbly, a tablespoon of flour if it's tacky. | Egg size, flour absorption and the weather all shift the balance. The target is a firm dough that doesn't stick to clean, dry fingers. |

## Context

This pattern suits tagliatelle, pappardelle, lasagne sheets and filled pasta such as ravioli and tortellini, made by hand on a wooden board or in a large bowl.

It does not suit eggless durum wheat dough for orecchiette, cavatelli or pici, which has its own method (see [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/)), or doughs made in a food processor or stand mixer, which mix faster but follow the same ratio.

There's a trade-off between richness and ease. Extra yolks (for example 1 whole egg plus 2 yolks per 150 g flour, as in parts of Piedmont) give a richer, deeper yellow pasta, but the dough is stiffer and harder to roll by hand. This pattern takes the simple one-egg-per-100-g route, which is forgiving for beginners. Choose yolk-rich dough once you're comfortable rolling thin. Whether to add salt or a little olive oil is disputed: many Emilian cooks use only flour and eggs, and neither is necessary.

## Usage

**Building the well:** heap the flour on a board and push a wide crater into the centre with your knuckles, with walls high enough to hold the egg. Crack in the eggs and beat them with a fork, keeping the fork flat and moving it in small circles. Pull a little flour from the inside wall with each pass. If a wall breaks, scoop the flour back with a bench scraper rather than chasing the egg across the board. Wash your hands after handling raw egg.

**Bringing it together:** when the centre looks like thick porridge, fold the remaining flour over it with a scraper and start pressing the mass together with your hands. It will look shaggy and dry at first. Don't panic and add water straight away: give it a minute of pressing, as the flour takes a little time to absorb the egg. Leave behind any dry crumbs that won't join in, since forcing them in makes hard specks.

**Reading the dough:** the right dough feels like firm modelling clay. If it's still crumbly after a minute of kneading, wet your hands and keep going, or spritz it with water. If it sticks to the board or your fingers, dust over a little flour. Then knead it until smooth (see [Kneading and resting pasta dough](../kneading-and-resting-dough/)). A dough that's slightly too firm is easier to fix than one that's too soft, because it softens as it rests.
