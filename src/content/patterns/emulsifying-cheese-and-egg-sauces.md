## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making pasta sauces from cheese and egg, such as cacio e pepe, gricia and carbonara. It describes how to control heat so cheese melts smoothly and egg thickens without scrambling, and how to keep the egg safe.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Grate the cheese very finely, just before using it. | Fine, fresh cheese melts quickly and evenly. Coarse or pre-grated cheese melts slowly and clumps (see [Grating hard cheese](../grating-hard-cheese/)). |
| Mix the cheese (and egg) with a little warm pasta water into a loose paste before it meets the pasta. | Starch in the water coats the cheese proteins and stops them sticking together, so the cheese disperses instead of forming strings. |
| Combine everything off the heat, or over very gentle heat, and keep tossing. | Pecorino and Parmigiano clump when the sauce gets too hot, and egg starts to set from about 65 °C and scrambles fast in a hot pan. Residual heat from the pasta is usually enough. (Diluted with cheese and water and stirred constantly over a water bath, an egg sauce can reach 72 °C and thicken without scrambling.) |
| Fix a sauce that's too thick with warm water, and one that's too thin with more tossing, not more heat. | Small changes in water content adjust the texture, while extra heat is what breaks it. |
| Serve egg sauces to vulnerable people only if the egg is pasteurised or the sauce reaches 72 °C. | Raw or lightly cooked eggs can carry *Salmonella*, which is most dangerous for pregnant women, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity. |

## Context

This pattern suits Roman-style sauces built on pecorino, Parmigiano, egg yolk and pork fat or butter, and any sauce finished with grated cheese.

It does not suit sauces thickened with flour, such as besciamella (see [Making besciamella](../making-besciamella/)), or cream sauces, which are stable on their own. Cream in carbonara is disputed: Roman cooks leave it out, but it makes the sauce more forgiving.

There's a trade-off between tradition and reliability. The traditional method relies on feel and residual heat. A *bagnomaria* (a bowl over barely simmering water) and a thermometer are slower but nearly foolproof, and the way to bring an egg sauce to a safe temperature. This pattern teaches the traditional method and recommends the water bath when you're learning or cooking for someone vulnerable.

## Usage

**Heat control:** toss the slightly underdone pasta with the fat and a splash of [pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/) (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). Take the pan off the heat and wait 20 to 30 seconds, until it stops sizzling, before the cheese or egg goes in. Then toss quickly and continuously. If you see white strings or the cheese gathering on the spoon, it's too hot: add a spoonful of cooler water and keep moving.

**The cheese paste:** in a bowl, mix finely grated cheese with a few spoonfuls of warm (not boiling) pasta water until it looks like thick cream. For an egg sauce, beat the yolks (or yolks and whole eggs) with the cheese first, then loosen with water. The finished sauce should look creamy and coat a spoon. It thickens as it sits, so stop slightly loose.

**Egg safety:** use fresh, clean, uncracked eggs kept in the fridge. Eat egg sauces straight away and don't keep leftovers. For pregnant women, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity, either use pasteurised eggs or stir the sauce over a water bath until it reaches 72 °C on a thermometer (it will be thicker and less glossy), or choose a different sauce such as cacio e pepe.

**Fixing mistakes:** a few small clumps can be rescued by tossing with more warm water off the heat. Scrambled egg or a rubbery mass of cheese can't be reversed, so use less heat next time.
