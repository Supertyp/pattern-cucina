## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who serves pasta with a sauce. It describes how to finish cooking the pasta in the sauce so the two become one glossy dish, the way Italian cooks do it.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Take the pasta out of the water about 1 to 2 minutes before it's al dente. | It finishes cooking in the sauce and absorbs its flavour instead of plain water, and ends up just right rather than soft. |
| Move it straight into the sauce in a wide pan over medium to high heat, with a splash of [pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/). | The heat and starchy water bind the fat and liquid in the sauce into an emulsion that coats every piece. |
| Toss or stir hard for 1 to 2 minutes, adding water a few tablespoons at a time, until it's glossy and loose. | Movement and heat are what turn sauce and water into a creamy coating. Without them the sauce stays watery or oily. |
| Add delicate finishes off the heat, then serve at once in warm bowls. | Grated cheese, fresh herbs and raw olive oil turn stringy, dull or flat over high heat, and the sauce tightens as it cools. |

## Context

This pattern suits nearly every sauced pasta: tomato sauces, ragù, oil and garlic, seafood, vegetable sauces and pesto (finished off the heat). It's the step that turns "pasta with sauce on top" into a dish.

It does not suit baked pasta (see [Assembling baked pasta](../assembling-baked-pasta/)), pasta in brodo, or filled pasta such as ravioli, which is too delicate to toss hard and is better gently coated with melted butter or sauce. Cheese and egg sauces use the same idea but need lower heat, covered in [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/).

There's a trade-off between control and convenience. Spooning sauce over drained pasta at the table is easier when people serve themselves or a pot of sauce needs to feed different eaters. This pattern takes the pan route because it gives better texture and seasoning. Choose the other when you're feeding a crowd from a big pot of ragù.

## Usage

**Getting the timing right:** start the sauce first, so it's simmering by the time the pasta goes in the water (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)). Taste a piece of pasta from 3 minutes before the packet time. When it's still a little too firm in the centre, lift it out with tongs or a spider straight into the sauce pan. Fresh egg pasta needs only 30 seconds in the sauce.

**Quantities:** use a frying pan or wide sauté pan, about 28 to 30 cm across for four portions, so you can toss without spilling. Plan on about 2 to 4 tablespoons of pasta water per portion, more for oil sauces and less for wet tomato sauces. Keep the rest of the water nearby in case the sauce tightens. For sauce-to-pasta amounts, see [Portioning pasta](../portioning-pasta/).

**What right looks like:** the sauce bubbles gently around the edges, then turns from thin and watery to creamy and shiny as you toss. Each piece is coated, and when you tilt the pan a little sauce flows slowly, like thick cream. If it's dry and sticky, add water. If it's soupy, keep tossing over the heat for another 30 seconds. If an oily sauce separates, add a splash of water and toss harder.

**Safety and serving:** keep the pan handle turned in, and use a long spoon or tongs if you can't flip the pan. Warm the bowls with a little hot pasta water or in a low oven. Taste for salt before plating (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)), and call people to the table before you start tossing, not after.
