## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making risotto. It describes the *mantecatura* (beating in butter and cheese off the heat), the last minute and a half that turns cooked rice into a creamy, glossy risotto that flows on the plate.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Take the pan off the heat as soon as the rice is al dente and still loose, and let the bubbling stop. | Butter and cheese added over the heat melt too fast, separate and turn oily, and the rice keeps cooking past al dente. |
| Add cold, diced butter and freshly grated cheese, roughly 10 g butter and 15 g cheese per 80 g of rice. | Cold butter melts slowly while you beat it, so it emulsifies with the starchy liquid into a creamy sauce instead of a greasy film. |
| Beat hard with a wooden spoon for 30 to 60 seconds, shaking the pan back and forth. | The movement and the starch bind fat and liquid into an emulsion that coats every grain. |
| Cover and rest for 1 to 2 minutes, then check it flows like a gentle wave (*all'onda*) and loosen with a splash of hot stock if needed. | The risotto keeps thickening, so it should be a little looser than you want on the plate. |
| Serve at once on warmed flat plates, spread out, and eat from the edges. | A flat layer cools evenly so you can eat it hot without burning your mouth, and the risotto stays loose instead of setting in a mound. |

## Context

This pattern suits classic risottos: parmesan, saffron, mushroom, vegetable.

It does not suit rice salads, baked rice dishes or [arancini](../deep-frying-at-home/) filling, which you want to set firm. Seafood risottos are usually finished without cheese, by custom rather than rule, using butter or good olive oil alone.

There's a trade-off between richness and lightness. A traditional mantecatura uses generous butter and cheese, and it's what makes restaurant risotto taste so good. Some cooks finish with olive oil, or with less fat, for a lighter dish. This pattern takes the classic butter and cheese route, and you can halve the butter or swap in olive oil when you want something lighter, accepting a slightly less creamy result.

## Usage

**Preparing ahead:** dice the butter and keep it in the fridge until the last minute. [Grate the cheese](../grating-hard-cheese/) finely just before you need it, as pre-grated cheese clumps and doesn't melt smoothly. Parmigiano Reggiano or Grana Padano are the usual choices (see [Buying Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana and Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/)).

**Beating it in:** with the pan off the heat, scatter over the butter and cheese and beat with energy, pushing the spoon around and through the rice. You'll see it turn from wet and grainy to glossy and creamy, and it starts to flow as one mass. Taste now and add salt if needed, remembering the cheese is salty.

**Checking the texture:** spoon a little onto a plate and tap the plate from underneath. A good risotto spreads out in a soft wave and settles flat. If it stands in a stiff mound, beat in a splash of hot stock. If it's runny with liquid pooling around it, rest it another minute. Serve straight away: a risotto that waits 10 minutes goes stiff and claggy, which is why it's always one of the last things to cook in a meal (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)).

**Common mistakes:** finishing over the heat, using soft butter, and adding cheese to a risotto that was already too thick. Cool leftovers quickly and refrigerate; they make better arancini than reheated risotto.
