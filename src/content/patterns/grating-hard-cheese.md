## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who use hard Italian cheeses. It describes grating them at the right moment, with the right grater, and making use of the rind.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Grate cheese just before you use it, from a wedge. | Aroma escapes from the huge surface of grated cheese within hours, and it dries out and loses its savoury depth. |
| Use a fine rasp for fluffy cheese that melts at once, and the small holes of a box grater for denser, finer cheese to mix into fillings and sauces. | The rasp makes thin, airy strands that melt on contact. The box grater's small holes give compact grains that measure more consistently. |
| Avoid cheap shelf-stable grated "parmesan" for sauces. | It usually contains anti-caking agents such as cellulose, which stop it melting smoothly and make sauces grainy. |
| Weigh grated cheese rather than measuring it by the cup. | Fluffy rasp-grated cheese takes up two or three times the volume of the same weight from a box grater. |
| Keep the rind of Parmigiano Reggiano and simmer it in soups, beans and sauces. | The rind is cheese with no wax or additives, and it slowly releases savoury flavour into the liquid. |

## Context

This pattern suits Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana Padano, Pecorino Romano and other aged grating cheeses.

It does not suit soft or semi-soft cheeses such as mozzarella or young Asiago, which are sliced, torn or coarsely grated instead.

There's a trade-off between freshness and convenience. Grated Parmigiano Reggiano in a fridge pack (it must be grated in its area of origin, without additives) is real cheese and acceptable for cooking into a dish. But it has lost aroma and dries further once opened. This pattern grates fresh for anything where the cheese is the main flavour, such as a cacio e pepe or a risotto. For a baked pasta, a good pre-grated pack is a reasonable shortcut.

## Usage

**Choosing the grater:** a rasp (often called a Microplane) gives the light, snowy cheese that melts instantly over hot pasta and into emulsions (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)). The small round holes of a box grater give finer, heavier grains, good for meatballs, fillings and stirring into risotto (see [Finishing risotto](../finishing-risotto/)). A vegetable peeler makes shavings for salads and carpaccio. The coarse holes of a box grater are for softer cheeses.

**Grating safely and well:** hold the grater firmly on the board or over a plate, and press the cheese against it with light, even strokes. Stop when the piece gets small, since that's when knuckles get grated, and keep the stub for soup. Hard cheeses grate best straight from the fridge. Allow roughly 5 to 10 g per person for the table, and more for a sauce made with cheese.

**Using the rind:** the Parmigiano Reggiano consortium confirms the rind is edible, as the cheese is made only from milk, salt and rennet. Scrape or cut off the outer surface with the printed markings, then drop a piece into minestrone, bean soup, [brodo](../making-brodo/) or tomato sauce while it simmers. Lift it out before serving. Rinds keep for months in a bag in the freezer. Check other cheeses first, as some are coated in inedible wax or plastic.

**Common mistakes:** grating cheese into a boiling sauce, which makes it stringy and clumpy (take the pan off the heat first), grating hours ahead and leaving it uncovered, and putting too much on the table so it fills the plate with salt. Keep leftover grated cheese covered in the fridge and use it within a few days (see [Storing cheese](../storing-cheese/)).
