## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who builds a dish on oil flavoured with garlic and chilli. It describes how to draw the flavour into the oil slowly, stop before the garlic turns bitter, and turn the oil into a sauce that coats pasta.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Put the garlic and chilli into cold oil (about 60 to 80 ml for 400 g of pasta), then heat it gently over low to medium heat. | Garlic's flavour compounds dissolve in fat, so a slow rise in temperature gives them time to move into the oil before the outside of the garlic scorches. |
| Take it off the heat when the garlic is pale gold, not brown. | Garlic's sugars burn within seconds once it browns, and burnt garlic tastes acrid and bitter, which no amount of pasta or cheese hides. |
| Stop the cooking with a ladle of starchy pasta water. | The water drops the temperature at once, and the starch helps the oil and water form a light, creamy emulsion instead of a greasy film (see [Saving the pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/)). |
| Make garlic oil to use straight away, and refrigerate any leftover within 2 hours and use it within a few days. | Garlic can carry spores of *Clostridium botulinum*, and oil keeps out air, so garlic in oil left at room temperature can let the bacteria grow and make toxin. |

## Context

This pattern suits spaghetti aglio, olio e peperoncino, the base for clams or mussels, sautéed greens such as broccoli or cime di rapa, and any sauce that starts with garlic and chilli in oil.

It does not suit soffritto-based sauces, where onion, carrot and celery need longer, slower cooking (see [Building a soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/)), or flavoured oils made to keep in a bottle, which carry a real food safety risk at home.

There's a trade-off between gentleness and depth. Very low heat gives a sweet, mellow garlic oil, while taking the garlic to a deeper gold gives a nuttier, stronger taste with a higher risk of bitterness. This pattern takes the pale gold side because it's forgiving. Once you can read the colour change reliably, go a shade further if you like a bolder flavour.

## Usage

**Getting the heat right:** slice the garlic thinly and evenly so every piece cooks at the same rate (see [Preparing garlic](../preparing-garlic/)). Use a wide pan and enough oil to cover the slices, or tilt the pan so they sit in a pool. Fresh chilli goes in with the garlic. Dried chilli flakes burn faster, so add them just as the garlic starts to colour. You should hear a gentle, steady sizzle, never a crackle. If the garlic colours before the oil smells strongly of it, the heat is too high. Pull the pan off for a moment and carry on more gently.

**Timing it with the pasta:** start the oil when the pasta goes into the water, so the garlic is ready about a minute before the pasta. If the garlic is done early, take the pan off the heat and let it wait. When it's pale gold and fragrant, add a ladle of pasta water. Then add the drained pasta and toss it over the heat until the liquid turns cloudy and clings (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). Parsley goes in at the end. Whether cheese belongs on aglio e olio is disputed: many cooks leave it off, others add a little pecorino.

**Staying safe:** water hitting hot oil spits violently. Pour the pasta water in from the side of the pan, away from you, and keep your face back. Never leave a pan of hot oil unattended, and keep a lid close by to smother flames. If the garlic burns, start again with fresh oil. It costs less than a ruined dish.
