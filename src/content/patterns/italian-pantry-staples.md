## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who want to make most everyday Italian dishes without a special trip to the shops. It describes a short core list of long-keeping ingredients, how much to keep, and how to store them so they stay good.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep the core eight: extra virgin olive oil, tinned tomatoes, dried pasta, risotto rice, anchovies, capers, garlic and a wedge of hard cheese. | Together they cover pasta sauces, risotto, quick antipasti and seasoning. Most Italian everyday dishes are built from a few of these plus one fresh ingredient. |
| Add a few supporting items: onions, dried chilli, dried oregano, tinned or dried beans, red or white wine vinegar and coarse and fine salt. | These are the cheap, long-keeping extras that turn the core list into soups, salads and *contorni* (side dishes). |
| Buy quality where it's tasted most: the oil you finish dishes with, the tomatoes and the cheese. | In a dish with four ingredients each one carries the flavour, so a good tin of tomatoes matters more than an expensive pan. |
| Keep about two meals' worth in reserve and replace as you open: two tins of tomatoes, two packets of pasta, one of rice. | You never run out mid-recipe, and stock turns over before it goes stale or the oil turns rancid. |
| Store each item right: oil away from light and heat, garlic and onions somewhere cool, dry and airy, opened anchovies and cheese in the fridge. | Heat and light make oil rancid, the fridge makes garlic sprout and go rubbery, and opened anchovies in oil spoil at room temperature. |

## Context

This pattern suits anyone cooking simple Italian food at home regularly, especially pasta, risotto, soups and vegetable dishes.

It does not suit baking and pizza, which need their own flours and yeast (see [Choosing flour for pasta, pizza and bread](../choosing-flour/)), or specialised regional cooking that relies on particular local products.

There's a trade-off between range and freshness. A big pantry with every shape of pasta and five vinegars looks well stocked, but things go stale and get forgotten. This pattern keeps a small list that you use up and replace. If you cook Italian food daily for a big household, a wider range is worth it.

## Usage

**The core list in practice:** for oil, keep an everyday extra virgin olive oil for cooking and, if you can, a better one for finishing (see [Choosing extra virgin olive oil](../choosing-extra-virgin-olive-oil/)). For tomatoes, whole peeled tins are the most versatile because you can crush them yourself (see [Choosing fresh and tinned tomatoes](../choosing-tomatoes/)). For pasta, two shapes are enough to start: one long (spaghetti) and one short (rigatoni or penne). For rice, Carnaroli or Arborio. Anchovies come in oil (ready to use) or salt (rinse and fillet first), and capers in brine or salt (rinse salted ones well, or the dish turns too salty).

**The hard cheese:** keep a wedge, not a bag of pre-grated cheese. Parmigiano Reggiano or Grana Padano suit most dishes, and Pecorino Romano suits Roman pasta (see [Buying Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana and Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/)). A wedge keeps for weeks in the fridge (see [Storing cheese](../storing-cheese/)).

**What the list lets you make:** with nothing fresh at all you can make spaghetti aglio, olio e peperoncino, pasta with tomato and anchovy, a quick tomato sauce (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)), pasta e fagioli or a simple risotto with cheese. Add one fresh thing, such as a bunch of greens, some fresh herbs or an egg, and the options multiply.

**Common mistakes:** buying huge bottles of oil that go rancid before you finish them, keeping garlic in the fridge, and letting spices and dried herbs sit for years. Dried oregano and chilli lose their aroma after about a year, so buy small packets. Opened tins of tomatoes should go into a covered container in the fridge, not stay in the can.
