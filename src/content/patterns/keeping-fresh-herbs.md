## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who buy or grow the herbs of Italian cooking. It describes how to keep each kind fresh for as long as possible, and when the dried version is just as good.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep basil out of the fridge, stems in a glass of water at room temperature, out of direct sun. | Basil is damaged by cold: below about 10 °C its leaves blacken and wilt. |
| Keep parsley like a bunch of flowers in the fridge: trimmed stems in a glass of water, a loose bag over the top. | Soft-leaved herbs lose water fast. Water at the stems and a humid bag keep the leaves firm, and the cold slows yellowing. |
| Wrap woody herbs (rosemary, sage, thyme) in barely damp paper in a container in the fridge. | Their tough leaves don't need standing water, which only rots the stems, but they still dry out in open air. |
| Keep herbs away from the back of the fridge and wash them only just before use. | The back can drop to freezing, which turns leaves to mush, and leaves stored wet go slimy and mouldy. |
| Use dried oregano and bay leaves happily, but buy basil and parsley fresh. | Oregano and bay keep their aroma well when dried, and dried oregano is traditional in southern Italian cooking. Dried basil and parsley taste of very little. |

## Context

This pattern suits bunches of cut herbs bought at the shop or market, and herbs you pick from the garden or a pot.

It does not suit potted herbs, which are living plants that need light, water and often a bigger pot.

There's a trade-off between convenience and shelf life. Glasses of water take up space and need fresh water every couple of days, while damp paper in a bag is simpler. This pattern prefers the glass for basil and parsley because they last longer, but if space is tight, damp paper in the fridge works for everything except basil.

## Usage

**Basil:** trim the stems and stand the bunch in a glass with a few centimetres of water, so only the stems are wet. Keep it on the bench away from the stove and sunny windows, change the water every day or two, and pick leaves from the top as you need them. It keeps three to five days like this. Tear or slice basil just before it goes into the dish, because cut leaves bruise and darken quickly, and add it at the end of cooking (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)). For [pesto](../making-pesto/), buy it on the day.

**Parsley and other soft herbs:** Italian cooking mostly uses flat-leaf parsley. Pick out yellow or slimy stems, trim the ends, stand the bunch in water on a shelf near the front of the fridge, and cover it loosely with a bag. It should stay fresh for a week or more. If it has wilted, a few minutes in cold water usually revives it.

**Woody herbs:** wrap rosemary, sage and thyme loosely in a sheet of damp (not wet) paper towel, put them in a container or bag and keep them in the vegetable drawer. They last about a week, often longer.

**Dried herbs and freezing:** dried oregano is classic on pizza, in tomato sauces and with grilled vegetables. Buy small packets and replace them yearly, since the aroma fades. To keep a glut of basil or parsley, chop it, pack it into ice cube trays, cover it with olive oil and freeze. Keep these cubes in the freezer and use them straight from frozen, because herbs in oil left at room temperature can be a food safety risk.
