## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making fresh pasta dough by hand. It describes how long to knead, how to tell when the dough is ready and why resting it makes rolling much easier.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Knead for 8 to 10 minutes with the heel of your hand, pushing away, folding back and turning a quarter turn each time. | Kneading lines up the gluten into a stretchy network and spreads the moisture evenly, so the dough rolls thin without tearing. |
| Stop when it's smooth, slightly silky and springs back slowly when you press a finger into it. | A rough, lumpy surface or a dent that stays put means the gluten isn't developed yet, while slow spring-back shows it's elastic. |
| Wrap it tightly in plastic film or a beeswax wrap, or cover it with an upturned bowl, and rest it for at least 30 minutes at room temperature. | Resting lets the gluten relax and the flour finish absorbing the liquid, so the dough stops fighting back and becomes soft and pliable. |
| Keep the dough covered whenever you're not working it. | Pasta dough dries out within minutes in open air, and a dry skin cracks when rolled. |

## Context

This pattern suits egg pasta dough (see [Mixing egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/)) and eggless semolina dough (see [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/)), kneaded by hand on a board.

It does not suit potato gnocchi, where kneading makes them tough and the dough should be mixed as little as possible (see [Making potato gnocchi](../potato-gnocchi/)), or pizza and bread dough, which need a different kind of development and a rise.

There's a trade-off between hand work and the machine. Some cooks knead only briefly and let repeated folding through the pasta machine finish the job. That works and saves your wrists, but the dough can stay patchy and you'll need more passes. This pattern prefers a proper hand knead, because you learn what good dough feels like. If kneading is hard on your hands, knead for 3 or 4 minutes, rest the dough, then laminate it in the machine (see [Rolling pasta sheets](../rolling-pasta-sheets/)).

## Usage

**Kneading:** work on a lightly floured wooden board or bench. Press the heel of your hand into the dough and push it away, fold it back over itself, turn it a quarter turn and repeat in a steady rhythm. Use your body weight rather than just your arms. Egg dough is firm and takes real effort for the first few minutes. Around the halfway point it changes: the surface goes from matt and crumbly to smooth, and the colour evens out. Don't keep adding flour; a little tackiness disappears as you knead.

**The touch test:** press a fingertip about 1 cm into the dough. If the dent slowly fills back in, it's ready. If it stays, knead for another couple of minutes. Cut the ball in half: the inside should be even, with no dry streaks or air pockets.

**Resting:** wrap the ball and leave it at room temperature for 30 minutes to 1 hour. You'll notice it feels softer and more relaxed afterwards. You can keep it in the fridge for up to a day, but let it come back to room temperature for 20 to 30 minutes before rolling, as cold dough is stiff and cracks. Egg dough that sits for too long in the fridge can turn grey on the surface, so roll it out the same day or the next.

**Fixing problems:** if the dough is too stiff to knead, wrap it and rest it for 10 minutes, then try again. If it tears when stretched after resting, it needs more kneading. If it's sticky, knead in a tablespoon of flour at a time.
