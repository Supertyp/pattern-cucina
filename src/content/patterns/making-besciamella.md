## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making the white sauce that binds baked pasta and gratins. It describes how to make a smooth *besciamella* (the Italian béchamel) that tastes cooked and has the right thickness for the dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use equal weights of butter and flour: about 100 g of each per litre of milk for a medium sauce, 70 to 80 g each for a looser lasagne sauce. | The flour's starch thickens the milk, so the amount of *roux* (butter and flour cooked together) sets the final thickness. |
| Cook the roux for 1 to 2 minutes over low heat, stirring, without letting it colour. | Cooking coats the flour in fat and removes the raw, pasty taste, and keeping it pale gives a white sauce with a clean, milky flavour. |
| Add warm milk gradually, whisking constantly, a splash at first and then in larger amounts. | Starch that meets liquid all at once forms dry pockets wrapped in gel, which become lumps. Adding milk slowly lets each addition disperse. |
| Simmer gently for 5 to 10 minutes after it thickens, then season with salt and a little nutmeg. | Starch only reaches full thickness near the boil, and a short simmer finishes cooking the flour. Nutmeg is the traditional Italian seasoning. |

## Context

This pattern suits lasagne, cannelloni, baked pasta, vegetable gratins and any dish that bakes under a creamy layer.

It does not suit cream sauces made by reducing cream, or cheese-and-egg emulsions such as cacio e pepe and carbonara (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)).

There's a trade-off between thickness and spreadability. A thick besciamella holds layers firmly and cuts cleanly, but it can make a lasagne dry and stodgy. A loose one spreads easily and keeps the pasta moist, but the layers can slide. This pattern leans slightly loose for lasagne, because the sauce thickens as it bakes and as the dish rests. Make it thicker when it has to bind a filling, as in cannelloni.

## Usage

**Making the roux:** weigh the butter and flour (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). Melt the butter over low heat until it foams, add the flour all at once, and stir for a minute or two. It should smell like biscuits, not like toast. If it starts to turn golden, take the pan off the heat. Meanwhile, warm the milk in a separate pan until it steams, not boils. Warm milk thickens faster and evenly, though cold milk works if you whisk harder.

**Adding the milk:** take the roux off the heat, pour in a splash of milk and whisk until smooth. It will seize into a thick paste at first, which is normal. Keep adding milk in larger amounts, whisking each time. Then return the pan to medium-low heat and whisk until it bubbles, getting into the corners where it catches. It's ready when it coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through leaves a clean line. If lumps form, whisk hard off the heat or push the sauce through a fine sieve.

**Holding and storing:** besciamella forms a skin as it cools. Press baking paper onto the surface if you won't use it at once. It stiffens as it cools, so loosen it with a little warm milk before spreading. Leftover sauce is a milk dish: cool it quickly, refrigerate it within 2 hours and use it within 2 to 3 days. For layering, see [Assembling baked pasta](../assembling-baked-pasta/).
