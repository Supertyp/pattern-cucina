## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants a homemade stock for Italian cooking. It describes how to make *brodo* (a light, clear Italian broth), with meat or vegetables, by simmering gently and skimming, and how to cool and store it safely.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Put the meat and bones in cold water, about 3 litres for 1.5 kg, with a halved onion, a carrot, a stick of celery and a few parsley stalks. | Starting cold lets the proteins dissolve slowly and rise as foam you can skim, and the aromatics give a rounded, not overpowering, flavour. |
| Bring it up slowly and keep it at a bare simmer, with just a bubble or two breaking the surface, for 2 to 3 hours (45 minutes for vegetables only). | A hard boil churns fat and protein into the liquid, turning it cloudy and greasy. A gentle simmer extracts the flavour and leaves it clear. |
| Skim the grey foam that rises in the first 20 to 30 minutes, and lift off fat as you go. | Removing the foam keeps the broth clear and clean-tasting, which matters most when it's served on its own. |
| Salt lightly, only at the end. | Brodo is used in risotto and soups that reduce and gain salt from cheese, so an unsalted base gives you control later. |
| Strain, cool quickly in shallow containers in the fridge, and use within 3 days or freeze. | Stock is an ideal home for bacteria. Cooling it fast through the danger zone (5 to 60 °C) keeps it safe, and freezing keeps it for about 3 months. |

## Context

This pattern suits Italian dishes where broth is a supporting background: risotto, soups such as minestrone, braises, and above all tortellini or other [filled pasta](../shaping-filled-pasta/) served in brodo.

It does not suit dark, roasted French-style stocks for reduced sauces, or fish stock, which needs only 20 to 30 minutes and different handling.

There's a trade-off between flavour and time. Homemade brodo takes hours, while a stock cube (*dado*) or bought stock is ready in seconds and is used in plenty of Italian homes. This pattern favours homemade when the broth is the star, such as pasta in brodo or a plain risotto. For a strongly flavoured dish, good bought stock diluted about half and half with water is fine.

## Usage

**Choosing the meat:** a classic *brodo di carne* uses a mix: a piece of beef shin or brisket, some chicken (an old hen gives the deepest flavour) and a beef or veal bone for body. Chicken alone makes a lighter brodo that suits risotto. For vegetable brodo, use onion, carrot, celery, a tomato and some parsley. Leave the onion skin on for a golden colour. Parmigiano rinds add savoury depth to a vegetable brodo.

**Keeping it gentle:** heat the pot over medium, then turn it right down as it nears simmering. Skim with a slotted spoon or ladle until the foam stops coming. Watch it now and then: if it starts to boil, lower the heat or set the lid ajar.

**Finishing and storing:** strain through a fine sieve, lined with a clean cloth for very clear broth. Taste and salt lightly. Divide hot stock into shallow containers so it cools fast, and get it into the fridge without leaving it out for hours. Next day, lift off the solid fat on top.

**Common mistakes:** boiling it hard, salting heavily at the start, and leaving a big pot of stock on the stove overnight to cool. That last one is a real food safety risk: if cooked food has been between 5 and 60 °C for more than 4 hours, throw it away.
