## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making coffee in a moka pot, the octagonal stovetop coffee maker found in nearly every Italian kitchen. It describes how to fill it, how to heat it gently and when to take it off, and how to look after it so it lasts for decades.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Fill the base with water to just below the safety valve, never above it. | The space above the water lets pressure build safely, and water over the valve can block it or spurt out. |
| Use a medium-fine grind and fill the basket level to the top without pressing the coffee down. | Steam pressure in a moka is low, and packed or very fine coffee chokes the flow, overheats the pot and tastes burnt and bitter. |
| Heat it on low to medium heat, with a gas flame no wider than the base. | Gentle heat gives a slow, steady stream of coffee instead of boiling bursts that over-extract it. |
| Take it off the heat as soon as it starts to gurgle and sputter. | The gurgle means the water has run out and steam is now pushing through, which scorches the coffee. |
| Rinse it with hot water only, dry it fully and store it unscrewed. | Detergent and the dishwasher dull and pit aluminium, and trapped damp leaves a stale, musty taste. |

## Context

This pattern suits making 1 to 6 small cups of strong coffee at home, to drink black, with a dash of milk, or after dinner in place of a bar espresso (see [Drinking coffee the Italian way](../ordering-coffee-in-italy/)).

It does not suit making true espresso with crema, which needs a machine and much higher pressure, or large mugs of filter-style coffee. A moka pot makes a strong coffee in between the two.

There's a trade-off between starting with cold or hot water. Bialetti and most Italian homes use cold water, which is simpler and safer. Many coffee roasters suggest hot (not boiling) water, which shortens the time the coffee sits on the heat and gives a less bitter cup, but you then handle a hot base. This pattern takes the traditional cold-water side and suggests hot water if your coffee keeps tasting harsh.

## Usage

**Filling and assembling:** fill the base to just under the valve, drop in the funnel and fill it with ground coffee, levelling it with a finger or knife without tamping. Wipe any grounds off the rim so the seal sits flat, then screw the top on firmly, holding the base with a cloth if the water is hot. Never run the pot without water and never block or tamper with the valve. Aluminium pots don't work on induction; use a steel model or an adapter plate.

**Watching it brew:** leave the lid open so you can see. After a few minutes the coffee rises in a slow, dark, syrupy stream that turns paler and honey-coloured as it goes. If it spurts or rushes out pale, the heat is too high. When it starts to gurgle and splutter, take it off the heat straight away and, if you like, run cold water over the base to stop the brewing. Stir the coffee in the top before pouring, because the first and last coffee taste very different. Use the handle and keep a cloth handy: the whole pot is scalding.

**Looking after it:** a new pot, or one with a new gasket, needs 2 or 3 brews thrown away before it tastes right. Many Italians never use detergent, letting a thin coffee film build up; others wash with a little mild soap. Both are fine, as long as it's rinsed and dried. Replace the rubber gasket and filter plate when coffee leaks from the join or the gasket hardens, usually every year or two.
