## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making *pasta frolla*, the Italian sweet shortcrust pastry behind the crostata (jam or fruit tart) and many biscuits. It describes how to mix it, rest it, roll it and bake it so it's crumbly, tender and crisp.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Weigh the ingredients and start from the classic ratio of 3 parts flour, 2 parts butter, 1 part sugar, plus about 1 egg per 300 g of flour. | The proportions decide whether pastry is tender or tough, and cups measure flour too unreliably for that. |
| Rub cold butter into the flour and sugar until sandy, then add the egg and bring it together quickly, without kneading. | Fat coating the flour limits gluten, and the less you work it, the more tender and crumbly the result. |
| Wrap and chill the dough for at least 1 hour (or overnight), and roll it between two sheets of baking paper about 4 mm thick. | Chilled butter keeps the dough from turning greasy and sticky, and resting relaxes the gluten so it doesn't shrink. |
| Bake at about 180 °C (160 °C fan-forced), and blind-bake the case first for wet fillings. | Moderate heat cooks the butter-rich dough through without burning the sugar, and a pre-baked base stays crisp under custard or fruit. |

## Context

This pattern suits a jam crostata with a lattice, tarts filled with pastry cream or ricotta, and cut-out butter biscuits.

It does not suit savoury pastry for pies and quiche, which has no sugar and more water, or puff and flaky pastries, which rely on layers of unblended butter.

There's a trade-off between hand-mixing and the food processor. Working the butter in with your fingertips (*sabbiatura*, "making sand") is the traditional method and lets you feel when it's right, but warm hands soften the butter. A food processor is faster and keeps everything colder, but it's easy to overwork the dough once the egg goes in. This pattern accepts either, as long as you stop as soon as the dough holds together.

## Usage

**Mixing:** for one 24 cm crostata, use 300 g plain flour (Italian 00 or a local plain flour, see [Choosing flour](../choosing-flour/)), 200 g cold butter in cubes, 100 g sugar (icing sugar gives a finer crumb), 1 egg, a pinch of salt and grated lemon zest. Rub the butter into the flour and sugar until it looks like coarse sand, add the beaten egg and press it together with a fork, then with your hands, just until there are no dry patches. Some Italian home recipes add a little baking powder for a softer, more cake-like pastry; others leave it out for crispness. Both are traditional.

**Resting and rolling:** flatten the dough into a disc, wrap it and chill it. If it's rock hard from the fridge, leave it 10 minutes before rolling. Roll it between baking paper, turning it a quarter turn now and then, and lift it into the tin on the paper. If it cracks, it's too cold or too dry: press the cracks together, since frolla patches easily. If it sticks and smears, it's too warm, so chill it again for 15 minutes. Chill the lined tin for 20 minutes before baking.

**Baking:** prick the base, line it with baking paper and fill it with baking beads, rice or dried beans. Bake for about 15 minutes, remove the weights and bake 5 to 10 minutes more until the base is dry and pale gold. A jam crostata doesn't need blind-baking: fill it, add the lattice and bake for 30 to 35 minutes until golden. Let it cool in the tin, because warm frolla is fragile and firms up as it cools.
