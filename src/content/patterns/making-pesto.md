## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making basil pesto. It describes the order of ingredients, how to avoid heating and bruising the basil so it stays green and sweet, and how to store it.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use young, small basil leaves, washed and dried completely. | Young leaves are sweeter and less minty, and water left on them dilutes the pesto and speeds darkening. |
| In a mortar, crush garlic and coarse salt first, then pine nuts, then basil with a gentle grinding motion, then the cheeses, and finally the oil. | Salt acts as an abrasive, and grinding rather than pounding tears the basil gently, releasing its aroma without bitterness. |
| In a blender, chill the jug, pulse in short bursts, and stir the cheese in by hand. | Blades heat and bruise the basil, which turns it dark and bitter. Short pulses and a cold jug keep the heat down. |
| Use a mild extra virgin olive oil and a mix of Parmigiano Reggiano and aged pecorino. | A peppery, intense oil overwhelms the basil, and the two cheeses balance savoury depth with a salty tang. |
| Store it in the fridge under a thin layer of oil and use within a few days, or freeze it. | Air darkens the surface, and fresh pesto with raw garlic in oil should stay cold for safety and flavour. |

## Context

This pattern suits classic pesto alla genovese from Liguria, and works for variations with other herbs, nuts or greens, such as rocket and walnut or a Sicilian pesto with almonds and tomato.

It does not suit cooked sauces, or using dried basil, which doesn't work for pesto at all. Keeping basil fresh before you use it is covered in [Keeping fresh herbs](../keeping-fresh-herbs/).

There's a trade-off between mortar and blender. The marble mortar and wooden pestle is the Ligurian tradition, and the Pesto Genovese Consortium's recipe still calls for it. It gives a more fragrant, slightly coarse pesto, but takes 20 minutes and some effort. A blender takes 2 minutes, at some cost to colour and aroma. This pattern describes both. Choose the mortar for small, special batches and the blender for large ones.

## Usage

**Proportions:** a good starting point for four servings of pasta is about 50 g of basil leaves, 1 small garlic clove, 1 to 2 tablespoons of pine nuts, 30 g of Parmigiano, 15 to 20 g of pecorino, a few grains of coarse salt and about 80 to 100 ml of olive oil. Adjust garlic to taste. Many Ligurian cooks use very little.

**Keeping it green:** work quickly and keep everything cool. In the mortar, rotate the pestle against the sides rather than bashing. The leaves should break down into a bright green paste that smells sweet and fresh. If it smells grassy or looks dark, it has been overworked or heated. Some cooks blanch the basil for a few seconds and chill it in iced water to fix the colour, which keeps it greener but softens the flavour.

**Saucing the pasta:** never cook pesto. Put it in a warm bowl, loosen it with 2 or 3 tablespoons of hot [pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/), and toss in the drained pasta off the heat. In Liguria it's often served with trofie or trenette and boiled potato and green beans.

**Storing:** pack it into a small jar, smooth the top and cover with a thin layer of oil. Keep it in the fridge and use it within 3 or 4 days. For longer, freeze it in ice-cube trays, ideally without the cheese, and stir the cheese in after thawing. Don't leave homemade pesto at room temperature for long.
