## Summary

This pattern is for anyone deciding which pasta to serve with a sauce. It describes the main families of shape, what kind of sauce each one carries well, and how to use regional pairings as a guide rather than a rule.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pair long, thin strands (spaghetti, linguine, bucatini) with smooth, oily or creamy sauces: olive oil and garlic, tomato, seafood, cheese and pepper. | Thin strands get coated evenly by a fluid sauce, but small chunks slide off them and sink to the bottom. |
| Pair wide egg ribbons (tagliatelle, pappardelle) with butter, cream and meat or game ragù. | Their broad, porous surface holds rich sauces, and their tenderness suits meltingly soft, slow-cooked meat. |
| Pair tubes and ridged shapes (rigatoni, penne, paccheri) with chunky and baked sauces, and short curled shapes (orecchiette, fusilli, trofie) with vegetables and pesto. | Hollows, ridges and curls trap pieces of meat and vegetables, and thicker walls stand up to a robust sauce and oven heat. |
| Use tiny shapes (ditalini, stelline, orzo) for soups and broths. | They fit on a spoon with the broth and cook evenly in it. |
| Choose rough, bronze-die dried pasta when the sauce needs to cling. | The rough, slightly porous surface holds a sauce far better than smooth, shiny pasta (see [Choosing dried pasta](../choosing-dried-pasta/)). |

## Context

This pattern suits everyday pasta cooking with dried or fresh pasta, whether you follow a classic pairing or work with what's in the pantry.

It does not suit filled pasta, where the filling matters more than the shape and the sauce is usually simple butter, sage or broth, or gnocchi, which take most sauces.

There's a trade-off between tradition and flexibility. Regional pairings are the result of generations of cooks finding what works: tagliatelle with ragù in Bologna, trofie with pesto in Liguria, orecchiette with *cime di rapa* (turnip tops) in Puglia, bucatini or rigatoni with amatriciana in Lazio. They're a reliable guide and worth learning. But they aren't laws, and the principle behind them (how a shape carries a sauce) lets you swap sensibly. This pattern follows the classic pairing when you're cooking a named dish, and the principle when you're improvising.

## Usage

**Reading a sauce:** ask two questions. Is it smooth or chunky? Is it light or rich? Smooth and light suits thin strands. Smooth and rich suits wider ribbons. Chunky suits tubes, ridges and curls. Very chunky, such as a sausage and broccoli sauce, suits short shapes with hollows that the pieces can nestle into.

**Swapping within a family:** if a recipe calls for a shape you don't have, stay in the same family. Swap linguine for spaghetti, penne for rigatoni, or fettuccine for tagliatelle. Moving across families, such as fine capellini with a chunky ragù, is where things go wrong. Egg pasta and dried pasta are not interchangeable in every case: fresh ribbons are softer and better with butter and meat sauces, while dried strands suit oil-based and seafood sauces.

**Making the match work:** even the right shape needs help. Finish the pasta in the sauce with a little of its cooking water so the two bind together (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). Use enough sauce to coat, not drown, the pasta (see [Portioning pasta](../portioning-pasta/)). Homemade shapes have their own natural partners (see [Cutting tagliatelle and pappardelle](../cutting-pasta-ribbons/) and [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/)).
