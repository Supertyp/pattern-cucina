## Summary

This pattern is for anyone choosing a wine for a dish who wants it to taste good with the food rather than despite it. It describes how to match the body of the wine to the weight of the dish, and how fat, tannin, salt and acid change each other.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Match weight with weight: light, crisp wines with light dishes, fuller wines with rich ones. | A heavy wine drowns a delicate dish, and a light wine tastes watery and sharp next to a rich one. Balanced weights let you taste both. |
| Judge a dish's weight by its fat, cooking method and sauce, not just its main ingredient. | Chicken poached with lemon is light, chicken braised in red wine is heavy. The sauce and the fat decide how much wine the dish can take. |
| Pair tannic reds with fatty, protein-rich food such as braised beef or aged cheese. | Fat and protein soften the drying grip of tannin, so a firm young red feels smoother with a rich dish than on its own. |
| Choose wines with good acidity for salty, fatty or acidic food. | Acidity cuts through fat and refreshes the mouth, salt makes a crisp wine taste rounder, and a wine less acidic than the food tastes flat. |

## Context

This pattern suits any Italian meal, from a light antipasto to a slow braise, and works whether or not you know much about particular wines.

It does not suit dishes with a very dominant single flavour that needs its own approach, such as tomato sauces (see [Wine with tomato dishes](../wine-with-tomato-dishes/)) or seafood (see [Wine with fish and seafood](../wine-with-seafood/)). Desserts follow their own rule: the wine should be at least as sweet as the dish.

There's a trade-off between a precise match for each course and the simplicity of one versatile bottle. Changing wine with each course shows off the food but means more open bottles and more drinking. This pattern favours choosing one medium-bodied wine with good acidity for a home meal, such as a Barbera, a Chianti or a Verdicchio, and saving course-by-course pairings for special occasions. Wine is always optional: water and non-alcoholic drinks belong on the table too.

## Usage

**Sizing up the wine:** body comes mainly from alcohol, extract and, in reds, tannin. As a rough guide, a wine around 11 to 12.5 % alcohol is light, 12.5 to 13.5 % medium and above 13.5 % full. Light whites include Pinot Grigio and Soave; fuller ones include oaked or aged whites. Light reds include Valpolicella, Dolcetto and Bardolino; full ones include Barolo, Amarone and Aglianico.

**Sizing up the dish:** raw, steamed, grilled or quickly fried food with oil and lemon is light. Butter, cream, cheese, slow cooking and browned meat add weight. A Ligurian pesto or seafood pasta is light to medium; a mushroom risotto finished with butter and cheese is medium; a ragù, ossobuco or meat braise is full.

**Fat, tannin, salt and acid in practice:** a fatty cut of meat or a cheesy baked pasta can take a firm, tannic red. Lean, delicate fish can't, because tannin tastes harsh and metallic without fat to soften it. Salty cured meats and hard cheeses love crisp whites, sparkling wines and fresh reds. Lemon, vinegar and tomato need a wine with at least as much acidity. Chilli makes high alcohol and tannin feel hotter and harsher, so go lighter with spicy dishes.

**Fixing a mismatch:** if a wine tastes too harsh, eat a little bread or cheese with it or move it to a richer course. If it tastes flat, chill it a little more (see [Serving wine at the right temperature](../serving-wine-at-the-right-temperature/)) or save it for a milder dish.
