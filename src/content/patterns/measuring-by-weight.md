## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who want reliable results, especially with doughs. It describes using a digital kitchen scale and grams instead of cups and spoons, and how baker's percentages make doughs easy to scale and adjust.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a digital scale that reads in 1 g steps, has a tare (zero) button and weighs up to at least 5 kg. | Tare lets you weigh each ingredient straight into the same bowl, and 1 g steps are precise enough for everything except yeast. |
| Weigh flour, sugar, pasta and cheese, and never measure flour by the cup. | A cup of flour can weigh anywhere from about 120 g to over 150 g depending on how it's filled, which is enough to wreck a dough. |
| Weigh water too: 1 ml of water weighs 1 g. | Weighing it into the bowl is faster and more accurate than reading a jug at eye level. |
| Write doughs as baker's percentages: flour is 100 %, everything else is a percentage of the flour. | You can scale any batch with one multiplication, and compare recipes at a glance: 65 % water is a firm dough, 80 % a wet focaccia. |
| For tiny amounts of yeast and salt, use a scale that reads to 0.1 g, or measuring spoons as a fallback. | A normal kitchen scale can be several grams out at the bottom of its range. |

## Context

This pattern suits all baking and dough work: [egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/), pizza and focaccia, bread, pastry and desserts. It also suits portioning dried pasta and grated cheese.

It does not suit everyday sauces, soups and seasoning, where Italian cooks traditionally work *a occhio* (by eye) and adjust by tasting (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)).

There's a trade-off between precision and feel. Weighing gives repeatable results and makes recipes from Italy easy to follow, since they are written in grams. Cooking by eye is faster and teaches you to read the food. This pattern weighs for doughs, baking and portions, and uses judgement for the rest. Even with a scale, you still adjust a dough by feel, because flour absorbs more or less water depending on its type and the weather.

## Usage

**Using the scale:** put the bowl on the scale, press tare, add the first ingredient, press tare again, add the next. Put the scale on a flat, hard surface, and replace the battery when readings start to drift. If you add too much, spoon some out rather than starting again.

**Baker's percentages in practice:** for a pizza dough with 1000 g flour at 65 % hydration you need 650 g water. With 2.5 % salt you need 25 g salt. To make half the batch, halve the flour and recalculate. This is also how you read and adjust dough recipes (see [Choosing dough hydration](../dough-hydration/)). For egg pasta the classic ratio is 100 g flour to one egg, and weighing the eggs shelled (about 50 to 60 g each, depending on size) explains why some batches are drier than others.

**Converting old recipes:** if a recipe gives cups, look for the writer's own weight conversion, since it depends on how they fill a cup. Note that spoon sizes differ too: an Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, while most other countries use 15 ml. That difference matters for salt and yeast.

**Common mistakes:** forgetting to tare after adding the bowl, weighing on a sloping bench, measuring oil by the cup (it weighs about 0.9 g per ml, so weigh it if the amount matters) and trusting the scale for half a gram of yeast. Weigh the pasta too: about 80 to 100 g dried per person as a first course (see [Portioning pasta](../portioning-pasta/)).
