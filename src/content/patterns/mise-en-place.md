## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who want cooking to feel calm instead of frantic. It describes getting everything measured, cut and within reach before the heat goes on, known in kitchens by the French name *mise en place* (everything in its place).

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Read the whole recipe before you start, and note every point where two things happen at once. | Surprises such as "while the pasta cooks, whisk the eggs" are where dishes go wrong. Knowing them in advance lets you do those jobs first. |
| Measure, wash, peel and cut every ingredient, and put each in its own bowl or pile, in the order you'll use it. | Once the pan is hot, the only thing left to do is add, stir and taste, so nothing burns while you chop. |
| Set out the tools too: colander, ladle, a cup for the [pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/), grater, tongs and warmed plates. | Many quick Italian dishes are finished in under a minute, and there's no time to search a drawer. |
| Start the slowest thing first: put the pasta water on as soon as you start your preparation. | A big pot takes 10 to 15 minutes to boil, which is about the time it takes to prepare a simple sauce. |
| Clear the bench, keep a bowl for scraps, and clean as you go. | A clear space is safer with knives and hot pans, and you finish with a tidy kitchen. |

## Context

This pattern suits fast Italian dishes, where the cooking is short and the timing is tight: pasta with oil, garlic and chilli, carbonara, cacio e pepe, quick tomato sauces, pan-fried cutlets and risotto.

It does not suit slow dishes in which long stretches of simmering leave plenty of time to prepare the next ingredient, such as a ragù or a braise. There you can safely prepare as you go.

There's a trade-off between calm and washing up. Proper *mise en place* uses extra bowls and takes 10 to 20 minutes before anything happens on the stove. This pattern accepts that for fast dishes, because a ruined sauce costs more than a few bowls. For slow dishes, or once you know a recipe well, prepare only the first few ingredients and do the rest while the pot simmers.

## Usage

**Why Italian cooking needs it most:** much of everyday Italian cooking is a handful of ingredients cooked very quickly. Sliced garlic in oil goes from pale gold to bitter in seconds (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)). The eggs and cheese for a carbonara must be ready the moment the pasta drains (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)). Risotto needs hot stock beside the pan for its whole 18 minutes. In each case, being ready is the technique.

**Setting up:** group things by when they go in: aromatics together, then the main ingredient, then what goes in at the end (herbs, cheese, butter, lemon). Weigh pasta and cheese rather than guessing (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). Grate the cheese and chop the herbs now, not at the table. Do the knife work on a stable board with a sharp knife (see [Sharp knife, stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/)).

**Safety while you prepare:** keep raw meat and seafood on their own board and plate, away from foods that won't be cooked, and wash your hands and the board after handling them. Keep prepared raw meat, seafood and dairy in the fridge until you need them, rather than sitting on a warm bench. Turn pan handles inward, away from the edge of the stove.

**Common mistakes:** starting to cook while still reading the recipe, and forgetting to scoop out pasta water before draining. If something gets ahead of you, take the pan off the heat while you catch up. For a whole meal with several courses, the same idea works on a larger scale (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)).
