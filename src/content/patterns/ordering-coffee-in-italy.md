## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants to drink coffee as most Italians do, whether at a bar in Italy or at the end of a meal at home. It describes what to order, when milk coffees fit, how the bar works and where coffee belongs in a meal.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Ask for *un caffè* when you want an espresso; that's what "coffee" means in Italy. | Espresso is the default, drunk several times a day, so there's no need to say "espresso". |
| Keep cappuccino and other milk coffees for the morning, usually with a pastry, and generally before about 11. | Milky coffee is treated as breakfast, a small meal in itself, and many Italians find it too heavy on a full stomach. |
| Drink it standing at the counter (*al banco*) unless you want to sit for a while. | It's quick and cheaper, and many bars charge more for table service. |
| After a meal, serve espresso after the dessert, not alongside it. | Coffee closes the meal and is thought to help digestion, and its bitterness follows the sweetness of the dolce. |

## Context

This pattern suits a visit to a bar (as cafés are called in Italy) and the end of a home-cooked Italian meal.

It does not suit speciality coffee shops, where pour-overs and flat whites have their own customs, or the world outside Italy, where a cappuccino in the afternoon is nobody's business.

There's a trade-off between custom and your own taste. These habits are customs, not laws: any bar in Italy will make you a cappuccino at 4 pm, and some Italians drink one. Following the custom helps you blend in and understand why Italians drink coffee the way they do; ignoring it is fine if you really want milk with your coffee. This pattern explains the custom and leaves the choice to you.

## Usage

**Ordering at the bar:** say "*un caffè, per favore*". Common variations are *ristretto* (shorter and stronger), *lungo* (a little longer), *macchiato* ("stained" with a dash of foamed milk, fine at any time of day), *corretto* ("corrected" with a shot of grappa or other spirit), *decaffeinato* and *caffè d'orzo* (a caffeine-free barley drink). Ask for a *latte* and you'll get a glass of milk; you want a *caffè latte*. Takeaway cups are uncommon, as coffee is drunk on the spot in a couple of minutes, often with a small glass of water.

**Paying and standing:** customs vary. In many busy city bars you pay first at the till (*cassa*), then hand your receipt to the barista; in smaller bars you order, drink, then pay on the way out. Watch what the locals do. Sitting at a table can cost noticeably more, especially in tourist squares, so stand if you just want a quick coffee.

**Coffee at home after a meal:** when the dolce plates are cleared, offer coffee, usually from a [moka pot](../making-moka-pot-coffee/) brewed while dessert is eaten, served in small cups with sugar on the side. Milk isn't usually offered, though a guest who asks should simply get it. A *digestivo* such as an amaro or grappa may follow (see [Aperitivo and digestivo](../aperitivo-and-digestivo/)). For where coffee sits in the order of courses, see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/).
