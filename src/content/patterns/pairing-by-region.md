## Summary

This pattern is for anyone choosing a wine for an Italian dish who wants a reliable starting point. It describes the "what grows together goes together" principle: start with a wine from the same region as the food, then check it suits the dish's weight.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Find where the dish comes from, then choose a wine from the same region. | Local food and wine developed side by side for centuries, so the region's wine has usually been shaped to suit its cooking. |
| Use a few anchor pairings to build confidence: Sangiovese (Chianti) with Tuscan food, Nebbiolo or Barbera with Piedmontese, Vermentino with Ligurian. | These classic matches show how the local wine's acidity, tannin and aroma fit the local ingredients, from beef and truffles to pesto and seafood. |
| Match the region's coast or hills too: seafood dishes from the coast with local whites, meat dishes from inland with local reds. | Even within one region, coastal and inland cooking differ, and so do the wines made nearby. |
| Check the weight of the specific dish before you buy. | A region makes light and heavy wines, and a delicate dish still needs a lighter wine (see [Matching wine weight to food](../matching-wine-weight-to-food/)). |

## Context

This pattern suits classic regional dishes such as Tuscan bean soups and grilled steak, Piedmontese braises and risotto, Ligurian pesto, Venetian seafood or Sicilian fish with capers and olives.

It does not suit dishes with no clear regional home, mixed-region menus, or Italian food cooked with ingredients from elsewhere. For those, think in terms of weight, acidity and the main flavour instead (see [Wine with tomato dishes](../wine-with-tomato-dishes/) and [Wine with fish and seafood](../wine-with-seafood/)).

There's a trade-off between authenticity of the match and availability and price. Regional Italian wines can be hard to find or expensive outside Italy, while good local wines made from the same grapes, such as Australian Sangiovese, Nebbiolo, Barbera or Vermentino, are often easier to get. This pattern starts with the regional wine because it's the most reliable guide, but a well-made wine from the same grape or a similar style is a sound substitute. Remember too that the rule is a rule of thumb: in Naples pizza is often eaten with beer.

## Usage

**Finding the region:** most classic dishes carry their home in the name or story: *alla genovese* (Genoa, Liguria), *alla romana* (Rome, Lazio), *alla milanese* (Milan, Lombardy), *alla siciliana* (Sicily). If you're unsure, a quick look at the recipe's origin is enough.

**A few reliable regional pairings:** in Tuscany, Chianti Classico or other Sangiovese with bean dishes, ragù and grilled meat. In Piedmont, Barbera or Dolcetto with everyday dishes and Barolo or Barbaresco (Nebbiolo) with braised beef and truffle dishes. In Liguria, Vermentino (locally also Pigato) with pesto and seafood. In Lazio, Frascati with Roman pasta such as cacio e pepe or carbonara and Cesanese with lamb. In the Veneto, Soave with risotto and seafood and Valpolicella with meat. In Abruzzo, Trebbiano d'Abruzzo with coastal fish and Montepulciano d'Abruzzo with meat and tomato dishes. In Campania, Falanghina or Fiano with seafood and Aglianico with rich meat. In Sicily, Grillo or Etna whites with fish and Nero d'Avola with heartier food.

**Reading the bottle:** use the label to confirm the region (see [Reading Italian wine labels](../reading-italian-wine-labels/)). When a shop has a small Italian range, ask for "a red from Piedmont" or "a coastal white from the south" rather than a specific bottle. Wine is optional at any Italian meal; serve plenty of water alongside, and offer a non-alcoholic drink to anyone who prefers it.
