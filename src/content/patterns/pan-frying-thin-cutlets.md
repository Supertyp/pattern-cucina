## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking thin escalopes in a frying pan. It describes how to cook them fast enough to colour without drying out, then turn the pan juices into a glossy sauce in a couple of minutes.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Flatten the meat to about 5 mm, pat it dry and dust it lightly in flour just before cooking. | Thin, even slices cook in a minute or two. Flour browns quickly, protects the surface and later thickens the sauce slightly. |
| Heat a wide pan with a little oil and butter until the butter foams, and cook in batches with space around each slice. | Thin meat needs high heat to brown before the inside overcooks. A crowded pan cools down and the meat steams instead. |
| Cook 1 to 2 minutes a side, then move the meat to a warm plate. | Escalopes overcook in seconds. Taking them out while you make the sauce stops them toughening. |
| Deglaze with about 100 ml dry white wine (or lemon juice and a little stock), scrape and reduce by half. | The browned bits (*fondo*) dissolve into the liquid and carry the flavour, and reducing cooks off the raw alcohol. |
| Take the pan off the heat and swirl in cold butter, then return the meat and turn it in the sauce. | Cold butter added off the boil emulsifies into a glossy, slightly thick sauce instead of splitting into grease. |

## Context

This pattern suits Italian quick dishes built on thin slices: *scaloppine al limone* (escalopes with lemon), *piccata* with capers, scaloppine with Marsala, and *saltimbocca alla romana* (veal with prosciutto and sage).

It does not suit breaded cutlets (see [Pounding and breading cutlets](../pounding-and-breading-cutlets/)), thick steaks (see [Grilling a thick steak](../grilling-steak/)) or tough cuts that need slow cooking (see [Braising meat](../braising-meat/)).

There's a trade-off between butter and oil. Butter gives the classic nutty flavour but burns at high heat, while oil copes with the heat but tastes of little. This pattern uses a mix to start and pure butter to finish the sauce. If your pan runs very hot, brown in oil alone and add all the butter at the end.

## Usage

**Getting the heat right:** the fat should shimmer and the butter foam and just start to smell nutty. The meat should sizzle loudly the moment it goes in. If it hisses quietly and liquid pools around it, the pan is too cool or too full: take some out. Flour the meat only as you go, because flour left on damp meat turns gluey. Don't move a slice until it releases easily with a golden underside (see [Browning meat](../browning-meat/)).

**Making the sauce:** pour off any burnt fat first, but keep the brown residue. Add the wine and scrape the pan with a wooden spoon as it bubbles (see [Deglazing with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/)). When it has reduced and no longer smells sharply of alcohol, take the pan off the heat and swirl in about 20 g cold butter per two serves. Add lemon juice, capers or parsley at the end, then slide the meat back in for 30 seconds to warm and coat. Taste for salt last, as capers and prosciutto are salty. Choose a dry wine you'd drink (see [Cooking with wine](../cooking-with-wine/)).

**Saltimbocca and variations:** for saltimbocca, lay a sage leaf on each slice, cover it with prosciutto and fix it with a toothpick. Cook it prosciutto side down first. Marsala gives a sweeter, darker sauce than white wine. Chicken and pork work as well as veal.

**Safety and common mistakes:** chicken and turkey must reach 75 °C in the centre (FSANZ), which thin slices do in a couple of minutes a side. Cut into the thickest one to check it's no longer pink, or use a thermometer (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)). A greasy, split sauce means the butter went into a boiling pan. Fix it with a splash of water and a quick swirl off the heat.
