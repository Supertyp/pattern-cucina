## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks using fresh tomatoes. It describes how to peel them quickly by scoring, blanching and shocking, when to remove the seeds, and when not to bother with either.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Cut out the core and score a shallow cross in the base of each tomato. | The cross gives the skin a starting point to split and peel from, and a shallow cut doesn't break the flesh. |
| Blanch in a large pot of boiling water for 15 to 60 seconds, until the skin at the cross starts to curl. | Brief heat loosens the layer between skin and flesh. Ripe tomatoes need only seconds, firmer ones longer. Any more and the flesh cooks and turns mushy. |
| Lift them straight into iced water for about a minute, then slip the skins off. | The cold stops the cooking before the flesh softens and makes the tomatoes cool enough to handle. |
| Seed tomatoes for raw toppings, fillings and quick, thick sauces, but keep the seeds and jelly for most cooked sauces. | Seeds and jelly hold a lot of water, which makes raw dishes soggy, but the jelly is also rich in savoury flavour. |
| Skip peeling for cherry tomatoes, for sauces passed through a food mill, and when using good tinned peeled tomatoes. | Small skins soften in cooking, a food mill removes skins and seeds for you, and tinned whole tomatoes are already peeled. |

## Context

This pattern suits fresh, ripe tomatoes in season, used for sauces, soups, raw toppings, fillings and preserves.

It does not suit choosing between fresh and tinned tomatoes, which is covered in [Choosing fresh and tinned tomatoes](../choosing-tomatoes/), or salads, where the skin helps the pieces keep their shape.

There's a trade-off between texture and effort. Peeling and seeding gives the smoothest sauce and the driest topping, but takes time and throws away some flavour with the seeds. This pattern peels for silky sauces and seeds only when water is the problem. Whether seeds make a sauce bitter is disputed among Italian cooks, so taste and decide for yourself.

## Usage

**Blanching:** bring a large pot of water to a rolling boil and set a bowl of water and ice beside it. Core the tomatoes and score the base. Lower a few at a time into the water with a slotted spoon, so the boil doesn't stop. Watch the cross: as soon as the skin lifts and curls (15 seconds for very ripe tomatoes, up to a minute for firm ones), move them to the iced water. Peel from the cross with your fingers or a small knife. It's the same blanch-and-shock idea used for green vegetables (see [Blanching and shocking vegetables](../blanching-and-shocking-vegetables/)). Keep children away from the pot, and lower the tomatoes in rather than dropping them, to avoid splashes of boiling water.

**Seeding:** cut each peeled tomato in half across its middle, through the equator, then gently squeeze or scoop out the seeds with a finger or teaspoon. Do it over a sieve set on a bowl: the juice that drains through is full of flavour and can go into the sauce or a soup. What's left is firm flesh (*concassé* when it's diced), ready for bruschetta, a raw sauce or a filling.

**When not to bother:** for a long-cooked sauce, cook unpeeled tomatoes and pass them through a food mill (*passaverdure*), which removes skins and seeds in one step. In winter, use good tinned whole peeled tomatoes rather than pale fresh ones (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)). For a few tomatoes, freezing them whole and running them under warm water also loosens the skin, though the flesh is then only good for cooking.

**Common mistakes:** cutting the cross too deep, blanching so long the outside cooks, skipping the iced water, and crowding the pot.
