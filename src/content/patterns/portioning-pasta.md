## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking pasta for one or a crowd. It describes how much dried, fresh and filled pasta to cook per person, and how much sauce to make for it.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Weigh dried pasta: about 80 to 100 g per person as a first course (*primo*), and about 120 g when pasta is the whole meal. | A primo is followed by a main course, so it's a modest plate. Weighing beats guessing, because shapes take up very different volumes. |
| Allow about 100 to 120 g of fresh egg pasta per person as a primo, and about 150 g as a main. | Fresh pasta contains water and egg, so it weighs more than dried for the same amount of food. |
| Allow about 120 to 150 g of filled pasta (ravioli, tortellini) per person as a primo, and 40 to 50 g of small pasta per person in soup. | Filling is heavier and more filling than pasta alone, and soup pasta is a minor part of a bowl of broth. |
| Plan on roughly 100 to 125 ml of tomato sauce or ragù, or about 2 tablespoons of pesto, per 100 g of dried pasta. | Italian pasta is coated with sauce, not swimming in it. The pasta should be the main event and the sauce its dressing. |

## Context

This pattern suits everyday pasta meals and dinners where pasta is one course among several (see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/)).

It does not suit baked pasta, where the size of the dish decides the portions, or very light sauces such as oil and garlic, where you simply make enough oil to coat the pasta.

There's a trade-off between the Italian portion and local habits. In Italy a primo of 80 to 100 g is normal because a main course follows. Many home cooks elsewhere serve pasta as the whole meal and expect a bigger plate. This pattern gives both. Choose the smaller amount when another course follows or the sauce is rich, and the larger amount for hungry eaters, a light sauce, or a one-dish dinner.

## Usage

**Weighing:** a digital scale is the easiest way (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). A standard 500 g packet makes 5 to 6 primo servings or 4 main servings. Without a scale, divide the packet by eye: half a 500 g packet feeds two hungry adults or three as a primo. Cup measures and spaghetti-measuring holes are unreliable, because a cup holds very different weights of small shells and large rigatoni.

**Matching sauce to pasta:** make the sauce for the weight of pasta, not the number of people. Rich sauces such as ragù, cream or egg go further than light ones. When you [finish the pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/), the sauce should coat each piece with only a little left in the pan. If you have sauce left over, it keeps better on its own than mixed with pasta. For a dish with large chunks, such as a sauce with sausage or seafood, add the pieces on top of these amounts.

**Adjusting for the meal:** for children, start at about half an adult portion. When serving pasta to more than six, cook in two pots rather than crowding one, because the water takes too long to return to the boil. Leftover cooked pasta can be refrigerated within 2 hours and eaten within a day or two. Leftover uncooked fresh pasta is better frozen (see [Drying and storing fresh pasta](../drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta/)).
