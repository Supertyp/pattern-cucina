## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making potato gnocchi at home. It describes how to choose and cook the potatoes, how much flour to use and how to mix and shape the dough so the gnocchi are light and hold together.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use floury, starchy potatoes (such as Russet, Maris Piper, King Edward or Désirée) and cook them whole in their skins, either baked or boiled. | Floury potatoes break into dry, fluffy crumbs, and the skin keeps water out. Waxy or watery potatoes need much more flour, which makes gnocchi heavy. |
| Peel and press the potatoes through a ricer while still hot, then spread them out to let the steam escape. | Ricing gives a light, lump-free mash without the glue that comes from mashing or blending, which bursts the starch cells. Letting steam escape dries the potato further. |
| Add as little flour as possible, about 200 to 300 g per kilo of potatoes, and mix only until it just holds together. | Every extra spoonful of flour and every extra minute of kneading makes the gnocchi denser and chewier. |
| Cook a test gnocco first, then cook the rest in batches in salted, gently boiling water and lift them out about 30 seconds after they float. | The test shows whether the dough needs a little more flour before you shape the whole batch. Floating means they're cooked, and a short extra moment firms the centre. |

## Context

This pattern suits classic potato gnocchi made with potato, flour and optionally egg, served with simple sauces such as butter and sage, tomato or pesto.

It does not suit ricotta gnocchi, semolina gnocchi baked Roman-style, or gnocchi made with pumpkin or sweet potato, which have different ratios and need their own methods.

There's a trade-off with the egg. An egg yolk or whole egg binds the dough, so it's more forgiving and holds together even if the potatoes are a little wet. Many cooks in northern Italy leave the egg out for the lightest result, and whether egg belongs in gnocchi is genuinely disputed. This pattern treats the egg as optional: leave it out once you're confident with your potatoes, and add a yolk if your gnocchi tend to fall apart.

## Usage

**Cooking the potatoes:** bake them on a tray at 200 °C (180 °C fan-forced) for about an hour until a knife slides in easily, or boil them whole and unpeeled in salted water until tender, then drain well. Baking gives drier flesh. Hold the hot potato in a tea towel to peel it, as the inside can scald. Rice it onto a clean bench in an even layer and let it cool for a few minutes until warm, not hot.

**Mixing and shaping:** weigh the potato (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). Sprinkle over about three-quarters of the flour and a pinch of salt (and the egg, if using). Gently bring it together with a bench scraper and your fingertips, folding rather than kneading, until it's a soft, slightly tacky dough. Don't knead as you would pasta dough. Roll pieces into ropes about 2 cm thick and cut them into 2 cm pillows. You can leave them as they are, or roll each one over the back of a fork or a gnocchi board to make ridges that hold sauce.

**Testing and cooking:** drop one gnocco into salted boiling water (see [Salting the pasta water](../salting-pasta-water/)). If it falls apart, work in a little more flour; if it's tough, you've already used enough flour and the next batch can have less. Cook the rest in small batches in a wide pot, at a gentle boil, and lift them out with a slotted spoon straight into the warm sauce.

**Timing:** gnocchi are best shaped and cooked within an hour or two, because the dough turns wet and sticky as it sits. If you're making them ahead, freeze them raw on a tray (see [Drying and storing fresh pasta](../drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta/)).
