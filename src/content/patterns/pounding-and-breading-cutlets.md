## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who makes crumbed cutlets at home. It describes how to flatten meat to an even thickness and build a coating that sticks, so the cutlet cooks through as the crust turns golden.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Pound the meat between two sheets of baking paper or plastic to an even 5 to 8 mm, working from the centre outwards. | An even thickness cooks evenly, so the thin edges don't dry out while the thick middle is still raw. The sheets stop the meat tearing and sticking to the mallet. |
| Pat the meat dry, then coat it in a thin layer of flour, then beaten egg, then breadcrumbs. | Flour clings to damp meat and gives the egg something to grip. Egg is the glue for the crumbs, and each layer needs the one before to stick. |
| Press the crumbs on firmly with your palm, then rest the cutlets in the fridge for 15 to 30 minutes. | Pressing anchors the crumbs, and a short rest lets the egg set slightly so the coating holds in the hot fat. |
| Cook chicken to 75 °C in the centre and keep raw meat and its coating plates apart from cooked food. | Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) advises 75 °C for poultry, and the flour, egg and crumbs touched raw meat. |

## Context

This pattern suits thin cuts of veal, pork or chicken breast for a *cotoletta* (breaded cutlet), a schnitzel or a chicken parmigiana, and also fish fillets and vegetable slices.

It does not suit thick steaks or chops you want pink inside (see [Grilling a thick steak](../grilling-steak/)), or thin unbreaded escalopes, which are only floured (see [Pan-frying thin cutlets and making a pan sauce](../pan-frying-thin-cutlets/)).

There's a trade-off between tradition and a thicker crust. The classic *cotoletta alla milanese* is a bone-in veal chop dipped only in egg and breadcrumbs and fried in butter, and some Milanese cooks consider flour a mistake. Home cooks usually add the flour because it makes the coating far more reliable. This pattern takes the flour, egg, crumb route, but leave the flour out if you want the traditional thin, crisp shell.

## Usage

**Pounding:** trim off sinew at the edges, which shrinks and curls the cutlet. For chicken breast, cut it in half horizontally first, so you're flattening rather than smashing. Use the flat side of a meat mallet, a rolling pin or a small heavy pan with firm strokes that push the meat outwards rather than hammering straight down, which tears it. Work on a stable board (see [Sharp knife, stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/)).

**Setting up the line:** lay out three wide plates in a row: seasoned flour, eggs beaten with a pinch of salt (one egg does about two cutlets) and breadcrumbs, then a tray at the end (see [Prepare before you cook](../mise-en-place/)). Plain dry crumbs give a fine, even crust. Homemade crumbs from stale bread are coarser and crunchier (see [Using day-old bread](../using-day-old-bread/)). Keep one hand for the wet plate and one for the dry, so your fingers don't clump.

**Getting the coating to stay:** shake off excess flour, as a thick layer turns pasty. Let excess egg drip off before the crumbs, or the coating gets soggy. If the crumbs fall off in the pan, the meat was wet, the flour layer was missing or the cutlet was moved before the crust set. Fry until deep golden, about 2 to 4 minutes a side depending on thickness, then drain on a rack, not paper, so the underside stays crisp. For the frying itself, see [Deep-frying at home](../deep-frying-at-home/).

**Safety:** wash your hands, board and mallet after handling raw chicken, throw away leftover coating, and check chicken with a probe (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)).
