## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking eggplant (aubergine, *melanzana*). It describes when salting is worth it, how to slice for each dish, and how frying, baking and grilling change the result.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose firm, glossy, heavy eggplants with a green stem, and use them within a few days. | Fresh eggplants have fewer seeds and are rarely bitter. Light, soft ones are old, seedy and spongy. |
| Salt slices or cubes for 30 to 60 minutes before frying, then rinse or wipe and pat dry. Skip salting for baking and grilling. | Salt draws out water and collapses the air pockets, so the flesh absorbs less oil. Modern varieties are rarely bitter, so it isn't needed to remove bitterness. |
| Cut slices about 1 cm thick for parmigiana and grilling, and 2 cm cubes for caponata and pasta. | Thick enough to hold together when cooked, thin enough to cook through before the outside burns. |
| Fry in hot oil (170 to 180 °C), or brush with oil and bake at 200 °C (180 °C fan), until deep golden and completely soft. | Hot oil seals the surface so less soaks in. Eggplant must be cooked right through: undercooked, it's spongy and squeaky. |

## Context

This pattern suits large globe eggplants for *parmigiana di melanzane* (layered with tomato sauce and cheese), *caponata* (sweet and sour Sicilian eggplant), *pasta alla norma*, *involtini* and grilled eggplant for antipasti.

It does not suit long, thin Asian varieties, which are tender and rarely need salting, or whole eggplants charred for a smoky purée, which follow a different method.

There's a trade-off between tradition and lightness. Classic parmigiana and caponata use fried eggplant, which gives a rich, silky texture that holds the dish together. Baking or grilling the slices instead uses far less oil and is easier, but the result is drier and less luxurious. This pattern fries for those two dishes and bakes or grills for everyday cooking. Frying is the more traditional route, though baked parmigiana is common in Italian homes too.

## Usage

**Salting:** layer the slices in a colander, sprinkling each layer with salt (about 1 teaspoon per eggplant), and put a plate and a weight on top. After 30 to 60 minutes, beads of brown liquid appear. Rinse quickly if they taste very salty, then press dry in a tea towel. Wet slices spit in hot oil. Whether salting is necessary at all is disputed: tests show fresh eggplant browns well without it, but it does reduce oil absorption when frying.

**Frying:** use a wide pan with about 1 to 2 cm of oil (sunflower, peanut or olive oil) and fry in batches without crowding. Some cooks dust the slices with flour first for a lightly crisp coating. Turn once, when the underside is deep golden, and lift them out when the flesh is soft and creamy. Drain on paper towels. At first the eggplant soaks up the oil, but as it cooks through it releases some again, so don't keep adding oil to the pan. For oil safety and temperatures, see [Deep-frying at home](../deep-frying-at-home/).

**Baking and grilling:** brush slices lightly on both sides with olive oil, season with salt and spread them on a lined tray in one layer (see [Roasting vegetables](../roasting-vegetables/)). Bake for 20 to 25 minutes, turning once, until golden and soft. For grilling, use a very hot dry grill pan and brush with oil afterwards (see [Grilling vegetables](../grilling-vegetables/)).

**Building the dish:** for parmigiana, layer the cooked slices with a light tomato sauce (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)), basil, mozzarella and Parmigiano, and bake until bubbling. Let it rest 20 minutes before cutting so the layers set. For caponata, fry the cubes, then combine with softened onion and celery, tomato, capers, olives, vinegar and a little sugar.
