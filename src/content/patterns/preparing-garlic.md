## Summary

This pattern is for home cooks who use garlic in Italian dishes, which is most of them. It describes how each way of cutting garlic changes its strength, when to remove the green germ, and when to take the garlic out of the dish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose the cut by the strength you want: whole and lightly crushed for a hint, sliced for a medium note, minced or grated for the strongest flavour. | Garlic's sharp flavour forms only when its cells are broken, so the more you cut it, the stronger it gets. |
| Lightly crush a whole clove under the flat of the knife when you want garlic in the background, and lift it out before serving. | The clove scents the oil or sauce without anyone biting into a piece, which is a common Italian way with tomato sauces and greens. |
| Remove the green germ from older garlic, especially if you use it raw. | The sprout that grows in stored garlic can taste harsh and bitter. In fresh, young garlic it's small and mild, so leave it. |
| Start garlic in oil over gentle heat and stop when it's pale gold. | Garlic burns in seconds over high heat and turns bitter. Pale gold is sweet and nutty. |
| Prepare garlic shortly before you cook, and keep any garlic in oil in the fridge for no more than a few days. | Cut garlic loses its freshness quickly, and garlic in oil at room temperature can allow botulism bacteria to grow. |

## Context

This pattern suits fresh garlic used in sauces, oils, soups, greens, marinades and dressings.

It does not suit slow-roasted whole heads, which are a separate dish, or garlic powder, which has a different, flatter flavour.

There's a trade-off between strength and subtlety, and Italian cooks disagree about it. Many prefer whole or sliced garlic that is removed or barely noticed, and the use of garlic in some classic dishes is disputed. Others like more garlic. This pattern leans towards restraint, because it's easy to add more and impossible to take it out. If you love garlic, mince it, but cook it gently.

## Usage

**Peeling and crushing:** set a clove on the board, lay the flat of a broad knife on top and press down firmly with the heel of your hand, keeping your fingers clear of the edge. The skin cracks and slips off. For a lightly crushed clove, press just until it splits. In some recipes the clove is crushed with its skin on (*in camicia*, in its shirt), which keeps it whole and milder still.

**Slicing and mincing:** for slices, cut the peeled clove lengthwise into thin, even pieces of about 1 to 2 mm so they cook at the same rate. For mince, slice, then cut across, then rock the knife over the pile. A rasp grater or garlic press gives a fine paste, which is the strongest form and burns fastest. Halve each clove first and check for a green germ: if there is one, flick it out with the tip of the knife.

**Cooking it right:** put sliced or crushed garlic into cold or warm oil, not smoking hot oil, and let it sizzle gently until it just colours (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)). Then add the next ingredient, such as tomatoes, greens or pasta water, which stops the cooking. If garlic turns dark brown, start again, as the bitterness will run through the whole dish. Whole cloves can be fished out once the sauce is done.

**Storing:** keep whole bulbs somewhere cool, dry and airy, not in the fridge, where they sprout and turn rubbery. Don't keep garlic in oil at room temperature. Make it fresh, keep it in the fridge and use it within a few days, or freeze it.
