## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making an everyday tomato sauce. It describes how to turn good tomatoes, fat and one aromatic into a sweet, glossy sauce in 20 to 30 minutes.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with good tinned whole peeled tomatoes (or very ripe fresh ones in summer), about one 400 g tin for 2 to 3 servings of pasta. | Whole tinned tomatoes are picked ripe and are usually better quality than chopped ones. With so few ingredients, the tomato is the flavour. |
| Use a generous fat: 3 to 4 tablespoons of olive oil, or butter, per 400 g tin. | Fat carries the flavour, rounds off acidity and helps the sauce turn glossy instead of watery. |
| Choose one aromatic: garlic warmed gently in the oil, or onion softened first. | Garlic gives a sharp, bright sauce and onion a sweeter, rounder one. Both are traditional, and using one keeps the sauce clean-tasting. |
| Crush the tomatoes by hand and simmer uncovered for 20 to 30 minutes, until thick and the oil starts to bead on the surface. | Simmering drives off water and concentrates the sugars. Hand-crushing keeps some texture, while a blender can make the sauce pale and frothy. |
| Add basil at the end, torn, and season with salt to taste. | Basil's aroma fades in long cooking, and salt is easiest to judge once the sauce has reduced. |

## Context

This pattern suits pasta al pomodoro, a base for pizza or baked dishes, eggs poached in sauce, and a starting point for sauces with olives, capers, chilli or tuna.

It does not suit slow, meat-based sauces (see [Slow-cooking a ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/)) or raw tomato sauces for summer pasta and pizza.

There's a trade-off between brightness and depth. A short simmer keeps the fresh, slightly sharp taste of tomato. Longer cooking, an hour or more, gives a deeper, sweeter, jammier sauce. This pattern aims for the 20 to 30 minute middle ground. Choose a shorter cook for ripe summer tomatoes, and a longer one when tinned tomatoes taste thin or acidic.

## Usage

**Building the base:** for garlic, put 1 or 2 lightly crushed or sliced cloves into cold oil and warm gently until they just turn pale gold, then remove them if you want a mild sauce (see [Preparing garlic](../preparing-garlic/)). For onion, soften half a finely chopped onion in the fat over low heat for about 10 minutes until translucent (see [Building a soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/)). A well-known variation simmers a halved onion in butter with the tomatoes and lifts it out at the end, giving a mild, sweet sauce.

**Adding the tomatoes:** crush the tomatoes in a bowl with your hands, or squeeze them through your fingers into the pan. Rinse the tin with a splash of water and add that too. Hot oil spits when wet tomatoes go in, so take the pan off the heat first, pour away from you, and keep a lid nearby. Bring to a gentle simmer, with lazy bubbles rather than a rolling boil, and stir now and then, scraping the bottom so it doesn't catch.

**Knowing when it's ready:** the sauce thickens, darkens to a deeper red, and drops slowly from a spoon. Little beads of orange oil gather on the surface and at the edges. Taste it: if it's still sharp, cook another 10 minutes. A pinch of sugar to fix acidity is common but disputed, and better tomatoes or more cooking usually do the job. Toss it with the pasta in the pan (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). It keeps for 3 days in the fridge, or months in the freezer.
