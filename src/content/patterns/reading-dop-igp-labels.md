## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants to know what they're buying when a label carries an Italian name. It describes what the EU's protected-name seals guarantee, what they don't, and how to spot imitations.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Treat DOP (in English PDO) as the strongest promise: the raw material, the making and the ageing all happen in one defined area, to a written specification. | Every stage is tied to the place, so a DOP Parmigiano Reggiano or Prosciutto di Parma is made from local milk or pork, by a fixed method, and checked by an inspection body. |
| Read IGP (PGI) as "at least one stage happens here". | An IGP product such as Mortadella Bologna has a real link to its area, but some raw materials can come from elsewhere. |
| Read STG (TSG) as a protected recipe, not a place. | Pizza Napoletana STG and Mozzarella STG can be made anywhere, as long as the traditional method is followed. |
| Look for the full registered name plus the EU seal, and usually a consortium mark or fire-brand as well. | Imitations rely on a big Italian word, a flag or a "style" suffix, but they can't legally carry the seal. |
| Remember the seal guarantees origin and method, not that you'll like it or that it's the best on the shelf. | Quality still varies between producers and ages, and some excellent products simply aren't registered. |

## Context

This pattern suits shopping for the famous protected foods: hard cheeses, mozzarella di bufala, cured hams, balsamic vinegar, olive oils and San Marzano tomatoes. Wine has its own system (DOCG, DOC, IGT), covered in [Reading Italian wine labels](../reading-italian-wine-labels/).

It does not suit everyday ingredients with no protected name, such as most dried pasta, flour or fresh vegetables. For those, read the ingredient list and use the advice in [Choosing dried pasta](../choosing-dried-pasta/) or [Choosing flour for pasta, pizza and bread](../choosing-flour/).

There's a trade-off between price and certainty. Protected products cost more because of their rules and inspections. This pattern pays for the seal where the name really changes the dish, such as a hard cheese you'll taste on its own, and happily buys a good unbranded or local product where it doesn't, such as cheese melted into a sauce.

## Usage

**Reading the seals:** the DOP seal is red and yellow, and the IGP seal is blue and yellow. You'll often see the Italian abbreviations (DOP, IGP, STG) on Italian packs and the English ones (PDO, PGI, TSG) on export packs; they mean the same thing. Many products also carry the consortium's own mark: Parmigiano Reggiano has its name in dots on the rind, and Prosciutto di Parma has a five-pointed ducal crown branded on the skin. If you're unsure whether a name is registered, the EU's eAmbrosia database lists every one.

**Spotting imitations:** "Italian-style", "Parma-style", a green, white and red flag, or a name like "parmesan" with no seal are marketing, not origin. Inside the EU, "parmesan" counts as an imitation of Parmigiano Reggiano and is not allowed on other cheeses, but in Australia, the US and many other places it's a generic name for any hard grating cheese. Check where the product was made, not just where it was packed: "packed in Italy" can hide ingredients from anywhere.

**Using the rules sensibly:** a DOP specification fixes the minimum, such as at least 12 months of ageing for Parmigiano Reggiano, so the age and producer still matter (see [Buying Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana and Pecorino](../buying-hard-cheeses/)). Local cheeses and hams made in the Italian way can be very good, and are a fair substitute when you're cooking. Just pay for the real name when you're paying for the real taste.
