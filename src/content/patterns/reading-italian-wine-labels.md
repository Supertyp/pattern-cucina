## Summary

This pattern is for anyone choosing an Italian wine who wants to understand the label rather than guess. It describes the four classification levels, the three ways Italian wines are named, and what words like Classico, Riserva and Superiore actually promise.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Read the classification as a guide to rules, not to quality: DOCG, then DOC, then IGT, then plain Vino. | Higher levels set stricter rules on area, grapes, yields and ageing, but great wines exist at every level and poor ones can carry a DOCG. |
| Work out whether the name is a place, a grape-plus-place or a brand name. | Italian wines are often named by place (Chianti), by grape and place (Barbera d'Alba) or by a made-up name (often IGT), and each tells you something different. |
| Treat Classico as "from the historic heart of the zone", Riserva as "aged longer" and Superiore as "stricter rules, often a little more alcohol". | These words have legal meanings within each denomination, but the exact rules vary from one wine to another. |
| Look for the producer's name and the vintage, and trust a good producer over a grand-sounding label. | Within one denomination quality varies a lot by maker, and the vintage tells you how old the wine is. |

## Context

This pattern suits buying wine from a shop or wine list for drinking or cooking, and it applies to Italian wines sold anywhere.

It does not suit wines from other countries, which have their own systems, or detailed rules of individual denominations, which you can look up on each consortium's site. The wider EU system of protected names for foods is covered in [Reading DOP, IGP and STG labels](../reading-dop-igp-labels/).

There's a trade-off between the safety of the top tiers and the value lower down. A DOCG or DOC tells you the wine followed tested rules for its place, which is reassuring when you don't know the producer. But some of Italy's most interesting wines are IGT, where producers can use grapes or methods outside the local rules, and plenty of honest everyday wines are DOC or IGT at lower prices. This pattern uses the label to understand the wine and the producer to judge it.

## Usage

**The four levels:** *DOCG* (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita, controlled and guaranteed designation of origin) has the strictest rules, tasting and analysis, and a numbered government strip on the neck; Barolo, Brunello di Montalcino and Chianti Classico are examples. *DOC* is the large middle tier of several hundred zones with strict local rules. In EU terms both count as DOP (protected designation of origin). *IGT* (Indicazione Geografica Tipica, typical geographical indication, the Italian name for IGP) only requires the grapes to come from a named, usually broad region. Plain *Vino* or *Vino da Tavola* (table wine) can come from anywhere in Italy.

**Decoding the name:** if you see a grape name joined to a place by *di* or *d'*, such as Montepulciano d'Abruzzo or Barbera d'Asti, the first word is usually the grape. A place name standing beside a DOC or DOCG, such as Soave or Chianti, means the rules of that zone decide the grapes, so look them up if you're curious. A fancy name not next to the classification is a brand, often an IGT blend. Beware of traps: Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is a Sangiovese wine from Tuscany, while Montepulciano d'Abruzzo is a different grape from a different region.

**Other useful words:** *rosso* (red), *bianco* (white), *rosato* (rosé), *secco* (dry), *amabile* or *dolce* (sweet), *frizzante* (lightly sparkling), *spumante* (sparkling). *Tenuta*, *Cantina*, *Fattoria* and *Azienda Agricola* point to the producer. Use what you learn to match the wine to its region's food (see [Pairing wine by region](../pairing-by-region/)).
