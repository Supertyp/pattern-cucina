## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking guanciale or pancetta for pasta sauces such as gricia, carbonara and amatriciana. It describes how to melt out the fat slowly so the pieces turn crisp and golden and the fat becomes the base of the sauce.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Remove the hard rind and cut into strips or batons about 1 cm thick and 3 to 4 cm long. | Even pieces cook at the same rate, and this size stays crisp outside but tender and juicy inside. |
| Start in a cold, dry pan with no oil, over medium-low heat. | Gentle heat melts the fat before the meat browns. A hot pan sears the outside and traps the fat inside, leaving chewy, greasy pieces. |
| Cook for 8 to 12 minutes, stirring now and then, until the fat is translucent and golden at the edges. | The meat crisps in its own fat. Past golden brown, the meat turns hard and the fat tastes burnt. |
| Lift the pieces out once crisp and keep the fat in the pan for the sauce. | The pieces stay crisp if they're added back at the end, and the rendered fat carries their flavour into the sauce. |

## Context

This pattern suits Roman pasta sauces, and any dish that starts with cured pork: soups, beans, braised greens and the soffritto for some ragù.

It does not suit cured meats that are eaten raw, such as prosciutto or thinly sliced pancetta for an antipasto (see [Buying guanciale, pancetta and prosciutto](../buying-cured-meats/)), or fresh pork belly, which needs much longer cooking.

There's a trade-off with substitutes. Guanciale (cured pork cheek) has the most fat and the deepest, most porky flavour, and it's the traditional choice in Rome. Flat or rolled pancetta (cured belly) is leaner and renders faster and is easier to find. Bacon works but is usually smoked, which changes the taste of the dish, and often releases water before it browns. This pattern prefers guanciale and works the same way with the others. Choose pancetta when guanciale isn't available, and use bacon knowing it gives a smoky result.

## Usage

**Cutting:** guanciale is easier to slice when cold. Take off the tough rind with a sharp knife, keeping your fingers curled away from the blade. Some cooks also trim off the peppery outer coating, others keep it for flavour. Cut slices about 1 cm thick, then cut each into batons.

**Rendering:** spread the pieces in a single layer in a wide frying pan, then turn the heat on to medium-low. After a few minutes the fat starts to melt and gently sizzle. Stir every minute or two. When the fat looks translucent and the edges are golden and crisp, while the centres are still slightly soft, it's ready. Pancetta gets there a few minutes sooner, so watch it closely.

**Using the fat:** for most sauces, take the pieces out with a slotted spoon and leave 2 to 3 tablespoons of fat in the pan. If there's much more, pour some off into a heatproof bowl. Add a splash of [pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/) to the fat and toss the pasta through it (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)), or follow the sauce's own method (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)). Guanciale is salty, so salt the pasta water lightly.

**Safety:** hot fat spits, especially when water hits it. Keep a splatter guard nearby, and add pasta water off the heat or with the pan tilted away. Let leftover fat cool, then bin it or keep it in the fridge for cooking. Never pour it down the sink.
