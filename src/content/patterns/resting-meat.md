## Summary

This pattern is for anyone cooking steaks, chops or roasts. It describes how long to rest meat, why it helps, and how to carve across the grain, including for a *tagliata* (sliced steak).

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Rest steaks and chops for about 5 minutes, roasts for 10 to 20 minutes and a whole bird or large roast for up to 30 minutes, roughly 1 minute per 100 g. | As the meat rests, the juices thicken slightly and settle, so less runs out when you cut it. Tests show a rested roast loses far less juice. |
| Take the meat off the heat 3 to 5 °C before your target temperature. | Heat stored in the outer layers keeps moving inwards, so the centre rises a few degrees during the rest. |
| Rest it on a warm plate or board, loosely tented with foil, not wrapped tightly. | A loose tent keeps it warm without trapping steam, which would soften a crisp crust or skin. |
| Slice across the grain, cutting at right angles to the lines of muscle fibre. | Short fibres are easy to chew, while slices cut along the grain leave long, stringy fibres. |
| Pour the resting juices back over the sliced meat. | They're full of flavour and seasoning, and they keep the slices moist. |

## Context

This pattern suits steaks, chops, lamb legs and racks, roast pork and beef, whole chickens and tagliata.

It does not suit braised or stewed meat, which sits in its sauce and doesn't need resting, very thin cutlets such as scaloppine, which cool before they'd benefit, or anything you want crisp and piping hot, such as fried cutlets.

There's a trade-off between juiciness and heat. Resting keeps more juice in the meat and lets it finish cooking evenly, but the outside cools and a crisp crust softens a little. How much resting matters is debated, and some cooks rest only briefly or not at all for thin steaks. This pattern favours a short rest as the safer bet for juicy meat, and you can cut the time down for thin cuts or sear again quickly just before serving.

## Usage

**Timing the rest:** use the rest to finish the sauce, dress the salad or warm the plates. The internal temperature goes up and then slowly comes back down, so a large roast stays hot for 20 to 30 minutes under foil. Check the final temperature with a probe if you're aiming for a particular doneness (see [Cooking meat by temperature](../cooking-meat-by-temperature/)). For poultry and rolled roasts, make sure they reached 75 °C before resting, rather than relying on the rest to get there.

**Finding the grain:** look at the surface of the cooked meat for the fine parallel lines of muscle fibre. Turn the meat so those lines run left to right in front of you, then cut straight down across them. In cuts like skirt or flank the grain is obvious and it matters most. In tender cuts like fillet it matters less. Use a [sharp knife on a stable board](../sharp-knife-and-stable-board/) so you slice cleanly instead of tearing.

**Making a tagliata:** grill a thick sirloin or rump steak (see [Grilling a thick steak](../grilling-steak/)), rest it for about 5 minutes, then cut it across the grain into slices about 1 cm thick, slightly on the diagonal. Fan them over a bed of rocket, pour over the resting juices, and finish with olive oil, flaky salt and shavings of Parmigiano or a few cherry tomatoes.

**Common mistakes:** cutting into the meat straight away to "check it", wrapping it tightly in foil, resting it on a cold plate, and leaving it so long it goes cold. Don't leave cooked meat out for more than 2 hours: past that, refrigerate it.
