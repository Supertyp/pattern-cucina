## Summary

This pattern is for anyone turning fresh pasta dough into thin, even sheets. It describes rolling with a machine and with a rolling pin, how thin to go for each use and what to dust with.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Work with a piece of dough the size of an egg at a time and keep the rest wrapped. | Small pieces give manageable sheets, and the waiting dough doesn't dry out and crack. |
| In a machine, laminate first: pass the dough through the widest setting, fold it in thirds and repeat 3 to 5 times before narrowing. | Folding and re-rolling finishes the kneading, squares off the edges and gives a smooth, elastic sheet that won't tear later. |
| Then step down one setting at a time, one pass each, without skipping. | Big jumps stretch the dough too fast, so it tears or shrinks back unevenly. |
| Roll to about 1 mm for ribbons and lasagne, and thinner (about 0.5 to 0.8 mm, so you can see your hand through it) for filled pasta. | Filled pasta has a double layer at the seams, so a thin sheet keeps the edges from turning chewy. Ribbons need a little body to hold a sauce. |
| Dust with fine semolina rather than flour. | Semolina's coarse grains act like tiny ball bearings and fall off in the pot, whereas soft flour soaks into the dough and turns gummy. |

## Context

This pattern suits egg pasta for tagliatelle, pappardelle, lasagne and filled shapes, rolled with a home pasta machine or by hand on a large wooden board.

It does not suit eggless semolina shapes such as orecchiette and pici, which are rolled into ropes, not sheets (see [Shaping eggless semolina pasta](../semolina-and-water-dough/)).

There's a trade-off between the machine and the rolling pin. A hand-rolled sheet (*sfoglia*) made with a long, thin pin (*mattarello*) has a slightly rough, porous surface that grips sauce beautifully, which is why many cooks in Emilia-Romagna still roll by hand. But it takes practice and a big board. A machine gives a smoother sheet quickly and evenly. This pattern starts with the machine because it's reliable for beginners. Try the pin when you want to learn the traditional skill.

## Usage

**With a machine:** clamp it firmly to the bench. Flatten the dough with your palm so it fits the rollers, then laminate on the widest setting until the sheet is smooth and roughly rectangular. Narrow one setting at a time, supporting the sheet on your flat hand as it comes out rather than pulling it. When it gets too long to handle, cut it in half. Most machines go to 7, 8 or 9 settings; the second-thinnest is usually right for ribbons and the thinnest for filled pasta, but machines differ, so check by eye. Keep fingers clear of the rollers and never put a hand in to free stuck dough while turning the crank.

**With a rolling pin:** start from a flattened disc. Roll from the centre outwards, give the disc a quarter turn, and repeat so it stays round. Once it's bigger than a dinner plate, wrap the far edge around the pin and roll it towards you while sliding your hands outwards along the pin, which stretches the sheet. Keep going until it's thin enough to see the grain of the board through it.

**Avoiding problems:** if the sheet sticks or tears, dust it lightly with semolina and fold it back through a wider setting. If it has holes, the dough was too dry or under-kneaded; fold it up and laminate again. Lay finished sheets on a semolina-dusted tray or cloth and cover them. Cut ribbons once the surface feels leathery (see [Cutting tagliatelle and pappardelle](../cutting-pasta-ribbons/)), but fill and seal filled pasta straight away while the sheet is still soft (see [Shaping and sealing filled pasta](../shaping-filled-pasta/)).
