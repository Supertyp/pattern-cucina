## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who cooks pasta in boiling water. It describes how much water and salt to use, when to add it and how to adjust for salty sauces.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use about 1 litre of water per 100 g of pasta, with at least 3 to 4 litres for a family-sized pot. | Plenty of water returns to the boil quickly after the pasta goes in and lets the pieces move freely, so they cook evenly and don't stick. |
| Add about 10 g of salt per litre (roughly 2 level teaspoons of fine salt, or a small handful of coarse salt for a big pot). | The pasta absorbs water as it cooks, and this is your only chance to season it all the way through. About 1 % salt tastes well seasoned without being harsh. |
| Drop to about 6 to 7 g per litre when the sauce is salty, such as one with pecorino, guanciale, anchovies, capers, olives or the liquor from clams and mussels. | The pasta water and the salty ingredients both end up in the dish, so cutting back here keeps the total in balance. |
| Add the salt once the water is boiling, just before the pasta, and stir. | Salt dissolves almost instantly in boiling water. Salt grains that sit on the bottom of a cold stainless steel pot can cause small pits in the metal. |

## Context

This pattern suits dried and fresh pasta, filled pasta and gnocchi cooked in boiling water, and the same idea works for blanching vegetables.

It does not suit pasta cooked in a small amount of water or in the sauce itself (risotto-style pasta), where the salt is concentrated as the liquid reduces and you season at the end (see [Seasoning and tasting as you go](../seasoning-and-tasting/)).

There's a trade-off between plenty of water and a smaller pot. Plenty of water is forgiving and gives an even cook. Less water (some cooks use as little as 1.5 to 2 litres for 500 g of dried pasta) boils faster, uses less energy and gives starchier pasta water for the sauce (see [Saving the pasta water](../saving-pasta-water/)), but needs more stirring. This pattern takes the generous route. If you use less water, keep the salt at the same 10 g per litre, not per 100 g of pasta, or the water will be far too salty.

## Usage

**Measuring:** weigh the salt once and see what 10 g looks like in your hand or your spoon, then trust that. Italians often use coarse salt (*sale grosso*) for the pot and fine salt for seasoning dishes; either works, but coarse salt is bulkier, so a spoonful weighs less. The common advice that the water should be "salty as the sea" is an exaggeration: seawater is about 35 g per litre, more than three times too salty.

**Tasting:** once the salt has dissolved, dip a spoon in, let it cool and taste. It should taste pleasantly salty, like a well-seasoned broth, not harsh. Unsalted pasta can't be fixed by salting the sauce more, because the salt then sits on the outside instead of in the pasta.

**Thinking about the whole dish:** decide on the salt before the pasta goes in. If you'll finish the pasta in the pan with lots of pasta water, or the sauce is built on pecorino, cured pork or anchovies, go lighter. If the sauce is plain, such as tomato and basil or butter, go for the full amount. Then taste the finished dish before you add any more salt (see [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/)). Adding a splash of oil to the water is a separate and disputed habit; it does nothing for the seasoning (see [Cooking pasta al dente](../cooking-pasta-al-dente/)).
