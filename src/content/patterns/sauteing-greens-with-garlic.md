## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants tender, well-flavoured greens cooked the southern Italian way. It describes *ripassati* (literally "passed again"): tough greens are first boiled, then tossed in a pan with olive oil, garlic and chilli.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Boil tough or bitter greens (cime di rapa, broccoli, chard, chicory) in well-salted water for 2 to 5 minutes first, but put tender spinach straight into the pan. | Boiling softens tough stems and draws out some bitterness evenly, so the pan only has to add flavour. Spinach cooks in seconds on its own. |
| Drain the greens very well and squeeze out leafy ones with your hands. | Water left in the leaves stews them and dilutes the oil, so they never get glossy or a little crisp at the edges. |
| Warm 2 to 3 tablespoons of olive oil with sliced garlic and chilli over medium heat until the garlic is pale gold. | The garlic and chilli flavour the oil, which then coats every leaf. Garlic burns quickly and turns bitter if the oil is too hot. |
| Add the greens, turn up the heat and toss for 3 to 5 minutes, seasoning at the end. | Tossing in hot oil coats the greens and drives off the last water. Salting at the end lets you allow for the salt from boiling. |

## Context

This pattern suits *cime di rapa* (turnip tops, also sold as broccoli rabe or rapini), broccoli and broccolini, Swiss chard and silverbeet, chicory, cavolo nero, kale, beet greens and spinach, as a *contorno* (side dish) or as the base of a pasta sauce. Buy what is in season (see [Shopping by season](../shopping-by-season/)).

It does not suit salad leaves such as rocket and lettuce, which belong in a dressing (see [Dressing a salad the Italian way](../dressing-a-salad/)), or vegetables you want crisp and green for a cold dish (see [Blanching and shocking vegetables](../blanching-and-shocking-vegetables/)).

There's a trade-off between colour and tenderness. Boiled and re-fried greens lose some of their brightness and turn a deep, soft green, and the long-boiled versions of southern Italy are softer still. That's the traditional texture and flavour. If you prefer them bright and crunchy, cut the boiling time to a minute or two. This pattern takes the tender, traditional route.

## Usage

**Preparing the greens:** wash them well in a sink of cold water, lifting them out so the grit stays behind. Trim tough stem ends. For cime di rapa, keep the tender leaves, small stems and flower buds and discard the woody stalks. For chard, cut the stems off and cook them a few minutes longer than the leaves. Boil in plenty of salted water, then drain. Keep a cup of the water if you'll make pasta with the greens: many cooks boil the pasta in the same water.

**Cooking the garlic and chilli:** put the oil and garlic in a cold pan and warm them together, so the garlic gently flavours the oil instead of scorching (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)). Use whole crushed cloves for a mild flavour, slices for more (see [Preparing garlic](../preparing-garlic/)). When it sizzles steadily and turns pale gold, it's ready. If it turns brown, start again: burnt garlic makes the whole dish bitter. Add one or two anchovy fillets with the garlic if you like, and stir until they melt.

**Finishing:** add the squeezed greens all at once, they'll hiss. Toss with tongs to separate the clumps and coat them in oil. Cook until the water is gone and the greens look glossy, then taste for salt and add a squeeze of lemon or a drizzle of fresh olive oil. Spinach goes in raw, in handfuls, and wilts in a minute or two. Drain off any liquid it releases. Leftover greens are excellent cold the next day, or reheated with sausage or on toasted bread.
