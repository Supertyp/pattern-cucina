## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who drains pasta before saucing it. It describes how to keep some of the cooking water and use it to bind, loosen and finish the sauce, and why you shouldn't rinse the pasta.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Before you drain, scoop out about 250 ml (a mugful) of cooking water, or lift the pasta straight from the pot so all the water stays there. | Once it's down the sink it's gone, and you only need a few spoonfuls per portion, but it's better to have too much than too little. |
| Use the water a few tablespoons at a time to loosen and bind the sauce in the pan. | Starch released from the pasta thickens the water slightly and helps fat and water hold together as a smooth emulsion instead of separating into grease. |
| Don't rinse pasta that's going into a hot sauce. | Rinsing washes off the surface starch that helps the sauce cling. The exception is pasta for a cold salad, which you rinse to stop it cooking and sticking. |
| Remember the water is salted, and taste after you add it. | Water salted at about 10 g per litre adds seasoning as well as liquid, so a sauce with pecorino or guanciale can quickly become too salty. |

## Context

This pattern suits every pasta that's tossed with its sauce: oil and garlic sauces, butter and cheese sauces, egg sauces, pesto, vegetable sauces and even a tomato sauce or ragù that has thickened too much.

It does not suit pasta served in brodo (broth), pasta for a cold salad, or baked pasta, where the liquid comes from the sauce itself (see [Assembling baked pasta](../assembling-baked-pasta/)).

There's a trade-off between plenty of water and starchy water. A big pot (about 1 litre per 100 g) cooks evenly and forgivingly, but its water is fairly thin. Cooking in less water gives cloudier, starchier water that binds more strongly, at the cost of more stirring to stop sticking. This pattern keeps the usual generous pot and simply saves more water. Choose less water when you make a lot of cheese or egg sauces, such as a cacio e pepe.

## Usage

**Scooping it out:** put a heatproof jug or mug next to the colander before you start, so you can't forget. Dip it into the pot near the end of cooking, when the water is at its starchiest. Use oven gloves or a long ladle and keep your face out of the steam. Better still, move the pasta straight into the sauce pan with tongs or a spider (a wide wire scoop) and leave the pot of water on the stove, ready to ladle from.

**Using it in the pan:** add a splash, toss, look and add more only if needed. The right look is glossy and loose, with the sauce clinging to each piece and a little pooling in the pan, never soupy and never dry and sticky. The pasta keeps absorbing liquid on the plate, so stop when it looks slightly looser than you want. How much to add and when is covered in [Finishing pasta in the pan](../finishing-pasta-in-the-pan/).

**When things go wrong:** if an oil sauce looks greasy and separated, add a spoonful of water and toss hard to bring it back together. If a cheese sauce turns stringy, the pan is too hot, and more hot water won't help: let it cool slightly before adding cooler water (see [Emulsifying cheese and egg sauces](../emulsifying-cheese-and-egg-sauces/)). If the sauce tastes too salty, loosen it with plain hot water from the kettle instead and go lighter on salt next time (see [Salting the pasta water](../salting-pasta-water/)).
