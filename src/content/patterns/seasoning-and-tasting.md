## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants their food to taste fully seasoned without being salty. It describes how to add salt in stages, allow for the salt that other ingredients bring, balance with acid and fat, and taste at the right moments.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Salt in small amounts at several stages: a pinch on the soffritto, some when liquids go in, and a final adjustment at the end. | Salt added early has time to spread through the food and season it from within, while salt added only at the end sits on the surface and tastes salty rather than seasoned. |
| Hold back salt when the dish contains Pecorino, Parmigiano, anchovies, capers, olives, guanciale or salted pasta water. | These ingredients are already heavily salted and release it into the sauce, so salting as usual on top makes the dish too salty. |
| Taste before serving and fix flatness with salt, and heaviness or dullness with acid (lemon, vinegar, wine) or a little fat (good olive oil, butter). | Salt makes flavours stronger, acid cuts through richness and brightens, and fat carries aroma and rounds off sharp edges. |
| Taste at the temperature you'll serve it, and season cold dishes a little more. | Salt receptors on the tongue respond most strongly near body temperature, so very hot or chilled food tastes less salty than the same food warm. |

## Context

This pattern suits every savoury dish that cooks over time: sauces, ragù, soups, braises, risotto and legumes.

It does not suit baking and doughs, where salt is weighed as a fixed percentage of the flour and can't be tasted raw (see [Choosing dough hydration](../dough-hydration/)), or cured and preserved foods, where salt levels matter for safety.

There's a trade-off between precision and judgement. Recipes that give exact salt quantities are reliable, but ingredients vary: one anchovy is saltier than another, and a sauce reduced for longer concentrates its salt. This pattern relies on tasting, because only your tongue knows what's actually in the pan. Weigh salt when you need repeatable results, such as pasta water for a crowd, and taste for everything else.

## Usage

**Building the salt in layers:** add a pinch of salt to the soffritto to help the vegetables soften (see [Building a soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/)), season again when tomatoes or stock go in, then wait. Sauces concentrate as they reduce, so don't correct seriously until near the end. With pasta, remember the salted cooking water (see [Salting the pasta water](../salting-pasta-water/)) and the cheese still to come. Taste the finished sauce with the pasta in it, not on its own.

**Counting the hidden salt:** rendered guanciale or pancetta, a spoon of capers or a handful of grated Pecorino can carry all the salt a sauce needs. Add those first, taste, and only then decide on extra salt. Pecorino Romano is much saltier than Parmigiano Reggiano, so switching cheeses changes the seasoning.

**Tasting well and fixing mistakes:** use a clean spoon for every taste, and never put a used spoon back in the pot. Don't taste dishes containing raw meat, poultry or raw egg until they're cooked through. If the food tastes flat, try a small pinch of salt first. If it's salty but still dull, it needs acid, not more salt. If you've over-salted, add more of the unsalted base (tomato, stock, beans or pasta), since a potato in the pot removes very little. Season towards the end with care, and let people add grated cheese at the table.
