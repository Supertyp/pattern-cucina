## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making hand-shaped pasta from durum wheat and water, the everyday pasta of southern Italy. It describes the dough and the main shaping movements, including the knife-drag for orecchiette.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use finely re-milled durum wheat semolina (*semola rimacinata*) with lukewarm water at about half its weight, for example 200 g semolina and 100 ml water. | Durum wheat has strong, less stretchy gluten that holds a shape well. Lukewarm water helps the hard grains absorb it faster. |
| Add the water gradually and knead for about 10 minutes until smooth and firm, then rest it covered for 15 to 30 minutes. | Semolina absorbs water slowly. Kneading and resting turn a gritty, crumbly mass into a smooth dough that won't crack when shaped. |
| Roll small pieces into ropes about 1 cm thick and cut them into 1 to 2 cm lengths, keeping the rest of the dough covered. | Even pieces cook evenly, and eggless dough dries out and cracks quickly in open air. |
| Shape on an unfloured wooden board with a firm drag of a round-tipped knife or your fingertips. | The bare wood grips the dough so it stretches and curls rather than sliding, and the drag leaves the rough surface that holds sauce. |

## Context

This pattern suits orecchiette and cavatelli from Puglia and the south, trofie from Liguria, and hand-rolled thick strands such as pici from Tuscany (which are often made with some soft wheat flour as well).

It does not suit ribbons and filled pasta, which need a thin, stretchy egg dough (see [Mixing egg pasta dough](../egg-pasta-dough/)), or gluten-free pasta, which needs different flours and binders to hold together.

There's a trade-off between coarse and fine semolina. Coarse semolina gives a more rustic, chewy pasta but is harder to bring together. This pattern uses *semola rimacinata* because it's forgiving and easy to find (see [Choosing flour for pasta, pizza and bread](../choosing-flour/)). If you only have plain flour, mix it half and half with semolina, and expect a softer, less sturdy result.

## Usage

**Getting the dough right:** pour the water into the semolina a little at a time and work it with a fork, then your hands. It should feel firm and slightly damp, like play dough, and not stick to your fingers. If it's crumbly after a few minutes of kneading, add water a teaspoon at a time. Like egg dough, it improves a lot with resting (see [Kneading and resting pasta dough](../kneading-and-resting-dough/)).

**The knife-drag for orecchiette:** put a small piece of rope on the board, set a table knife with a rounded tip on top at a low angle and press down while dragging it towards you, so the dough curls over the blade into a rough little shell. Then turn it inside out over your thumb to make the "little ear". The first few will look odd; it takes a dozen or so to get the feel. For cavatelli, drag two or three fingertips through each piece so it rolls into a hollow shape, or press it with the flat of the knife.

**Ropes and strands:** for pici, roll small pieces of dough under your palms into long, thick strands, working from the middle out. Keep the pieces you've shaped on a tray lightly dusted with semolina, not touching. Eggless shapes cook in about 4 to 8 minutes when fresh, longer when dried, and they keep better than egg pasta (see [Drying and storing fresh pasta](../drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta/)). Their chewy texture suits robust sauces with vegetables and greens, as in orecchiette with *cime di rapa* (turnip tops) (see [Matching pasta shape to sauce](../matching-pasta-shape-to-sauce/)).
