## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making panna cotta ("cooked cream") or another cream set with gelatine. It describes how to bloom and dissolve gelatine, how much to use for a soft wobble, and how to turn the dessert out cleanly.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Soak leaves in plenty of cold water for 5 to 10 minutes and squeeze them out, or sprinkle powder over 5 times its weight of cold water and leave for 5 minutes. | Gelatine has to absorb water before it can dissolve evenly; added dry, it forms rubbery lumps. |
| Dissolve it in the warm cream, off the heat, at about 60 to 70 °C, and never boil it afterwards. | Warm liquid melts gelatine in seconds, while boiling weakens its setting power. |
| Use about 1 g of gelatine per 100 ml of liquid for a soft set in glasses, and about 1.5 g per 100 ml if you want to turn it out. | A panna cotta should barely hold its shape; more gelatine makes it stand firmly but tastes rubbery. |
| Chill at least 4 hours, ideally overnight, then dip the mould in hot water for 5 to 10 seconds to unmould. | Gelatine sets slowly and keeps firming for hours, and a brief dip melts only the outer layer so it slides out. |

## Context

This pattern suits panna cotta and other creams, milk puddings, fruit jellies and mousses set with gelatine, served in glasses or turned out.

It does not suit creams set by eggs (custards) or by fat (see [Working with mascarpone](../working-with-mascarpone/)), and it doesn't suit vegetarian guests, because gelatine is made from pork or beef. Agar-agar is the usual plant-based alternative, but it must be boiled, sets firmer and has a different, more brittle texture.

There's a trade-off between a tender set and a safe unmoulding. The silkiest panna cotta is just barely set, which works best served in a glass. Turned-out panna cotta looks impressive but needs more gelatine and is a little firmer. This pattern prefers the soft set in glasses for everyday cooking and adds gelatine only when you want to unmould.

## Usage

**Choosing and measuring gelatine:** leaves (sheets) come in grades such as bronze, silver, gold and platinum, and a sheet of one brand can weigh twice as much as another, so check the setting guide on the packet and weigh rather than count (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). As a guide, 2 teaspoons of powder is about 6 g. Leaves give a cleaner, clearer set with no taste; powder is cheaper and easier to find.

**Making the cream:** heat the cream, milk and sugar with vanilla or lemon zest until steaming and the sugar has dissolved. Take it off the heat, let it cool for a minute, then whisk in the squeezed leaves or the bloomed powder until no strands remain. Strain it into a jug, let it cool to lukewarm and stir now and then so the vanilla seeds don't sink, then pour into lightly oiled moulds or glasses. Cover and chill. When set, it wobbles as a whole when you nudge the mould and doesn't ripple like liquid on top.

**Unmoulding and fixing problems:** run a thin knife round the top edge, dip the mould in hot water for a few seconds, put a plate on top and turn it over with a firm shake. If it doesn't drop, dip again; too long and the outside melts. If it didn't set, the gelatine was too little or was boiled. Fresh pineapple, kiwi fruit, papaya and figs contain enzymes that stop gelatine setting, so cook them first or serve them on top. Panna cotta keeps covered in the fridge for 2 to 3 days, which makes it an ideal dessert to prepare ahead (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)).
