## Summary

This pattern is for anyone setting the table for an Italian meal at home. It describes where the plates, cutlery, glasses and bread go, what to leave off, and why warm bowls matter for pasta.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Put forks on the left of the plate, knife on the right with the blade facing the plate, and lay out only what the meal needs. | Cutlery is used from the outside in, and a spoon only appears for soup, so guests can read the meal from the table. |
| Place dessert cutlery horizontally above the plate. | It stays out of the way until the dolce and is easy to reach without clearing anything. |
| Set a large water glass and a wine glass above the knife, and put a carafe of water and the wine on the table. | Water and wine are both drunk through the meal, and guests can pour without waiting. |
| Put bread directly on the table or in a basket, with no butter or oil dish. | Bread goes with the meal and is used to wipe up sauce, rather than eaten as a starter (see [Bread at the Italian table](../bread-at-the-italian-table/)). |
| Warm the pasta bowls or plates before serving. | Pasta has a lot of surface and cools within minutes, and a cold plate turns a glossy sauce thick and sticky. |

## Context

This pattern suits family meals and dinners with guests where courses come one after another (see [Structuring an Italian meal](../structuring-an-italian-meal/)).

It does not suit a formal banquet with many pieces of cutlery and glasses, which follows the full rules of etiquette (*galateo*), or a buffet or casual aperitivo where people help themselves.

There's a trade-off between a fully laid table and a simple one. Setting everything at the start means you never have to bring cutlery mid-meal, but it can look crowded. A simple setting, often just a fork and knife at a family table, with fresh cutlery brought for each course, is lighter but means more getting up. This pattern lays out what's needed for the savoury courses, with dessert cutlery above the plate, and brings the rest when needed.

## Usage

**Laying the table:** start with a tablecloth, which most Italian homes still use for a proper meal, or placemats. Put the plate about two finger-widths from the edge. A flat plate stays in place for the meal, and a soup bowl or pasta bowl (*piatto fondo*) is put on top for the primo if needed. Forks go to the left (two if there are two courses that need them), knife to the right, blade inwards, then a spoon on the far right only if there's soup. The dessert spoon or fork lies above the plate. The napkin goes to the left of the forks or on the plate. Glasses sit top right, water largest, then the wine glass. A small bread plate at the top left is optional; many families put bread straight on the tablecloth.

**On the table:** bread, water, wine, a bowl of grated cheese with a spoon when the pasta needs it (see [When to add cheese](../cheese-on-pasta/)), and salt and good olive oil for the salad. Pasta is eaten with a fork alone (see [Eating pasta](../eating-pasta/)), so there's no need for a spoon beside it.

**Warming the plates:** put the pasta bowls in the oven at its lowest setting (about 60 to 70 °C) for 10 minutes, or fill them with hot water from the tap or the pasta pot and tip it out just before plating. Warm plates are hot to the touch but comfortable to hold; if you can't hold them barehanded, they're too hot to set in front of guests. Warn people when a plate is very hot.
