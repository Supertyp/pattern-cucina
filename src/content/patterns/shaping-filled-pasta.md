## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making filled pasta at home. It describes how to prepare the filling, shape and seal the pasta without trapping air, and cook it so it stays whole.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Make the filling dry, well seasoned and cold: drain ricotta, squeeze cooked greens hard and cook any meat or vegetables first. | Wet filling makes steam that pushes the seams apart and soaks the pasta. Cold filling is firmer to portion, and the pasta itself cooks for too short a time to cook raw filling safely. |
| Use a thin, fresh sheet (about 0.5 to 0.8 mm) and fill it straight away, before it dries. | A fresh sheet is still slightly tacky and seals to itself, while a dry one won't stick and cracks when folded. |
| Use a small amount of filling (about a teaspoon for a 5 to 6 cm square) and press out the air from the filling towards the edges before sealing. | Overfilled pasta can't close properly, and trapped air expands in the hot water and splits the pasta. |
| Cook in salted water at a gentle boil for 3 to 5 minutes, and lift them out with a slotted spoon rather than tipping them into a colander. | A hard, rolling boil knocks filled pasta about and tears the seams. They're done when the thickest folded edges are tender. |

## Context

This pattern suits ravioli, tortelli, tortellini, cappelletti, agnolotti and other fresh egg pasta with a filling, whether served in sauce or in broth (see [Making brodo](../making-brodo/)).

It does not suit baked filled pasta such as cannelloni, which is rolled around the filling and finished in the oven, or bought filled pasta, which only needs gentle cooking.

There's a trade-off between moulds and hand shaping. Ravioli trays and stamps make even, quick shapes and are a good start, but hand-folded shapes such as tortellini have thinner seams and that pleasing, handmade look. Hand shaping is slower, so for a crowd this pattern suggests larger shapes (ravioli or tortelli) and keeping tiny tortellini for special occasions or a group of helpers.

## Usage

**Preparing the filling:** drain fresh ricotta in a sieve for an hour or overnight in the fridge (see [Buying mozzarella, burrata and ricotta](../buying-fresh-cheeses/)), squeeze spinach or chard in a clean tea towel until no more water runs out, and bind with grated hard cheese and a little egg yolk if needed. Taste it before any raw egg goes in. Keep it in the fridge until you use it. If the filling is runny, stir in more grated cheese or a spoonful of breadcrumbs.

**Shaping:** for ravioli, lay a strip of sheet on a dusted bench, place small mounds of filling about 3 cm apart along one half, and fold the other half over. Starting from the fold, cup your fingers around each mound and press outwards to push the air out, then seal the edges firmly and cut between them with a knife or fluted wheel. For tortellini, cut 4 to 5 cm squares, put a little filling in the centre, fold into a triangle, press out the air, then wrap the triangle around your fingertip and pinch the two corners together. If the sheet has dried, dab the edges with a fingertip of water, but use as little as possible, since too much water makes the seams slide.

**Holding and cooking:** lay finished pieces in a single layer on a tray dusted with semolina, not touching, and cover with a cloth. Filled pasta goes soggy and sticks if it sits for long, so cook it within an hour or two or freeze it (see [Drying and storing fresh pasta](../drying-and-storing-fresh-pasta/)). Bring a wide pot of salted water to the boil, turn it down to a lively simmer and cook in batches. Taste one at the thickest edge to check.
