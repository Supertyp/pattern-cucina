## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who uses a kitchen knife. It describes keeping the knife sharp, the board still and your fingers out of the way, which together prevent most kitchen cuts.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep your knife sharp: hone it on a steel every few uses and sharpen it properly a few times a year. | A blunt knife needs more pressure and slips off the food instead of biting into it, which is when cuts happen. |
| Put a damp cloth or damp paper towel under the board, unless it has non-slip feet. | The cloth grips both the bench and the board, so the board can't slide or rock while you cut. |
| Hold the food with the claw grip: fingertips tucked under, knuckles against the side of the blade. | The blade can only touch your knuckles, which guide it, never your fingertips. |
| Cut on a large board, not a plate or the bench, and use a separate board for raw meat and seafood. | A big board gives you room to work safely, and a separate board stops raw meat juices reaching food that won't be cooked. |
| Never try to catch a falling knife, and never leave one in a sink of soapy water. | A falling or hidden blade is the cause of many serious cuts. Step back and let it fall, and wash knives one at a time. |

## Context

This pattern suits all everyday knife work with a chef's knife (about 20 cm), a small paring knife and a serrated bread knife, the three that cover almost every job.

It does not suit specialist tasks such as filleting fish, boning meat or using a mandoline, which need their own tools and precautions.

There's a trade-off in sharpening. A whetstone gives the best, longest-lasting edge but takes practice. A pull-through sharpener is quick and easy but removes more metal and gives a rougher edge. This pattern suggests a honing steel for regular upkeep and a whetstone or a professional sharpening service a few times a year. If you won't do either, a pull-through sharpener is far better than a blunt knife.

## Usage

**Honing and sharpening:** honing realigns the edge, while sharpening grinds a new one. To hone, hold the steel upright with its tip on a damp cloth, and draw the blade down it at about 15 to 20 degrees, alternating sides, five or six times each. If honing no longer brings the edge back, it needs sharpening. Test it on a tomato or a sheet of paper: a sharp knife slices cleanly with almost no pressure. Wash and dry knives by hand, and store them in a block or on a magnetic strip, not loose in a drawer.

**The claw grip:** curl the fingertips of your guiding hand under, rest the flat of the blade against your middle knuckles, and keep your thumb tucked behind your fingers. As you cut, walk your claw back along the food. For round things such as onions and carrots, first cut a thin slice off one side so they sit flat and can't roll (see [Dicing onion, carrot and celery](../dicing-soffritto-vegetables/)).

**Cutting motion:** for chopping, keep the tip of the chef's knife on the board and rock the heel up and down, pushing slightly forward. Use the back of the blade, not the edge, to scrape chopped food off the board, because scraping with the edge dulls it. A *mezzaluna* (a curved, two-handled rocking blade) is the traditional Italian tool for chopping herbs and soffritto, and the same rules apply.

**Hygiene:** wash the board, knife and your hands in hot soapy water after cutting raw meat, poultry or seafood, before you cut anything else. Replace boards that are deeply scored.
