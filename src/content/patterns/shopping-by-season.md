## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who wants their Italian cooking to taste the way it does in Italy, wherever they live. It describes how to let the season choose the dish, how to translate recipes between hemispheres, and what to substitute when an ingredient isn't available.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start from what's in season and plentiful, then pick the dish, not the other way around. | Produce picked ripe and sold close to home has more flavour and sugar, and costs less, than produce picked early to travel. |
| Translate recipes by season, not by month: Southern and Northern Hemisphere seasons are six months apart. | An Italian recipe for an early summer dish of broad beans and peas means late spring or early summer where you live, whatever the calendar says. |
| Shop at a market or greengrocer with a loose plan, and ask what's best this week. | Growers and grocers know what's at its peak, and a flexible list lets you buy the best rather than the listed. |
| Swap in a local ingredient of the same kind when the Italian one isn't available. | Most Italian dishes depend on a type (a bitter green, a firm white fish, a waxy potato) more than one exact variety. |
| Use the pantry and the freezer to bridge the gaps: tinned tomatoes, dried beans, frozen peas. | They're preserved at their peak and are better than fresh produce out of season. |

## Context

This pattern suits fresh vegetables, fruit, herbs and seafood, and planning everyday meals around them.

It does not suit pantry staples, which are seasonless by design (see [Stocking an Italian pantry](../italian-pantry-staples/)), or the occasional dish you cook out of season for a reason, such as a birthday request.

There's a trade-off between flexibility and planning. Shopping by season means you sometimes change the menu at the market, which suits relaxed cooking less than a fixed weekly plan. This pattern keeps a few seasonal dishes in mind for each part of the year and decides the details at the shop. If you need a set plan, build it around what's in season that month and keep a pantry fallback.

## Usage

**Knowing the seasons:** in spring, look for asparagus, artichokes, broad beans, peas and new-season greens. Summer is tomatoes, zucchini and their flowers, eggplant, capsicum, green beans, basil and stone fruit. Autumn brings mushrooms, pumpkin, grapes, figs and the first chestnuts. Winter is for cavolo nero, cime di rapa, cauliflower, broccoli, fennel, radicchio, chicory and citrus. Seafood has seasons too; ask your fishmonger (see [Buying fresh fish and shellfish](../buying-fresh-seafood/)).

**Translating recipes:** an Italian book's "autumn" risotto with pumpkin is an autumn dish in Sydney or Auckland too, just at a different time of year. Ignore month names in imported recipes and think "early spring", "high summer" and so on. In warm climates, some seasons start earlier and last longer, so trust what the market shows you over any chart.

**Local substitutes:** for cavolo nero, use kale; for cime di rapa (turnip tops), broccolini or mustard greens (see [Sautéing greens with garlic and chilli](../sauteing-greens-with-garlic/)); for radicchio, any bitter chicory or endive. Local fish and shellfish replace Mediterranean ones well by texture.

**At the market:** go early for the best choice, or late for bargains. Look for produce that's heavy, firm and fragrant, with fresh-looking stems and leaves. Buy what's abundant and cheap, because that usually means it's at its peak, and keep herbs fresh once home (see [Keeping fresh herbs](../keeping-fresh-herbs/)).
