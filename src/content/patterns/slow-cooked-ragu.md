## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making a meat ragù. It describes how to build it and simmer it low and slow until the meat is tender and the sauce is rich, using Bolognese and Neapolitan ragù as examples.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with a [soffritto](../building-a-soffritto/), then brown the meat in batches until it sizzles and has lost all its pinkness. | Browning adds savoury depth, and adding meat a little at a time keeps the pan hot so the meat fries instead of stewing in its juices. |
| [Deglaze with wine](../deglazing-with-wine/) and let it evaporate before the tomato goes in. | Wine lifts the browned bits off the pan and adds acidity, and cooking it off removes the raw alcohol taste. |
| Use tomato to colour and season the sauce, not to drown it, and simmer for 2 to 3 hours at the gentlest bubble, partly covered. | A meat ragù should taste of meat. Low, slow heat tenderises the meat and melds the flavours without drying or scorching the sauce. |
| Top up with a little stock or water whenever it starts to look dry. | The sauce should reduce slowly, not catch. Small additions keep it moist without thinning it. |
| Make it a day ahead if you can, and cool it quickly. | The flavours settle and deepen overnight, and fat that sets on top is easy to lift off. |

## Context

This pattern suits ragù alla bolognese (minced meat, a little tomato, white wine and milk), Neapolitan ragù (large pieces of meat cooked for hours in plenty of tomato), and their many regional relatives with pork, sausage, game or mixed meats.

It does not suit quick meat sauces made in 30 minutes or tomato sauces without meat (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)). Pieces of meat cooked in a little liquid as a main course are covered in [Braising meat](../braising-meat/).

There's a trade-off between tradition and time. A traditional ragù needs a few hours and an eye on the pot, and there are many family versions, with disagreement about garlic, milk, the cut of meat and the wine. A pressure cooker or slow cooker gives a good result with less attention, though it doesn't reduce and concentrate the sauce in the same way. This pattern follows the slow stovetop method. Choose the other when you can't be home to watch it.

## Usage

**Building the sauce:** for a Bolognese style, the version registered by the Accademia Italiana della Cucina uses coarsely minced beef, pancetta, soffritto, dry white wine, a little tomato and some milk, simmered for about 2 hours with stock added as needed. Some cooks add the milk before the wine, others near the end to soften the acidity. For a Neapolitan style, brown whole pieces of beef and pork, add plenty of passata and simmer very slowly. The sauce dresses the pasta and the meat is served as a main course.

**What a good simmer looks like:** a lazy "plop" every few seconds, not a lively boil. Use a heavy pot and the smallest burner on its lowest setting. Stir every 15 to 20 minutes, scraping the bottom. When it's ready the sauce is thick and glossy, the fat glistens on the surface, and the meat is soft enough to melt on the tongue.

**Safety and storing:** minced meat cooked for hours easily passes the 75 °C that FSANZ recommends. Cool leftovers fast in shallow containers and put them in the fridge within 2 hours. Use within 3 days, or freeze in meal-sized portions. Reheat until it's bubbling hot all through.
