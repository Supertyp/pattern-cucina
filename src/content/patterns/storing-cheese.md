## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who buys Italian cheeses and wants them to taste as good on the last day as on the first. It describes how to wrap, where to keep and how long to keep hard and fresh cheeses, and when to throw them out.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Wrap hard and semi-hard cheese in baking or wax paper, then put it loosely in a container or bag. | Paper lets a little moisture escape so the surface doesn't sweat and grow mould, and the outer layer stops it drying out or absorbing other smells. |
| Keep cheese in the vegetable drawer or a lower shelf at about 4 to 8 °C, away from the door. | The drawer is humid and steady, while the door warms up every time you open it. |
| Keep fresh cheeses (mozzarella, burrata) in their own liquid in a sealed container, and eat them within a day or two of opening. | They are mostly water and lose texture and flavour quickly, and as soft, moist cheeses they spoil fast. |
| Cut mould off hard cheese generously, but throw away soft and fresh cheese that has gone mouldy. | Mould can't spread far into a dense, dry cheese, but it spreads easily through soft, moist ones. |
| Take cheese out about 30 minutes before eating it on its own, but only the amount you'll serve. | Cold mutes aroma and stiffens texture. Taking out only what you need keeps the rest out of the warm, and leftovers within the 2-hour limit. |

## Context

This pattern suits the Italian cheeses most cooks keep: Parmigiano Reggiano, Grana Padano, Pecorino, Asiago, provolone, mozzarella, burrata and ricotta.

It does not suit unopened vacuum-packed cheese, which keeps until its best-before date, or ageing cheese at home.

There's a trade-off between breathability and convenience. Cheesemongers recommend paper because it lets cheese breathe, while the Parmigiano Reggiano consortium says a sealed container or food-grade plastic film also works, as long as the cheese stays away from other smells. Plastic film pressed onto the surface tends to make softer cheeses sweat and taste of plastic over time. This pattern takes the paper-and-container approach, but if you use a hard cheese within a couple of weeks, film or a sealed box is fine.

## Usage

**Hard cheese:** rewrap a wedge in fresh paper each time you use it, folding it close around the cut faces. Parmigiano Reggiano aged 12 to 18 months keeps about 15 days once cut, and cheese aged 24 months or more about a month, according to the consortium. A little white crystal on the cut face is normal: those crunchy bits are tyrosine crystals from ageing, not mould. If the surface dries or hardens, grate that part first (see [Grating hard cheese](../grating-hard-cheese/)). The consortium advises against freezing because it changes the texture and aroma.

**Fresh cheese:** keep mozzarella and burrata covered in their liquid, in the coldest part of the fridge, and eat them by the use-by date or within a day or two of opening. Ricotta goes in a covered container and should be eaten within about two or three days of opening. If the liquid turns cloudy and sour or the cheese smells off, throw it out (see [Buying mozzarella, burrata and ricotta](../buying-fresh-cheeses/)).

**Mould and safety:** on hard cheese, cut away at least 2 cm around and below any mould spot, keeping the knife out of the mould itself. Throw away soft and fresh cheeses with any mould. Soft, semi-soft and fresh cheeses such as ricotta and gorgonzola can carry listeria. Pregnant women, older people and people with weakened immunity should only eat them thoroughly cooked and steaming hot. Hard cheeses such as Parmigiano Reggiano are a much lower risk.

**Common mistakes:** a cheese board left out all afternoon (put leftovers back within 2 hours) and strong blue cheese stored unwrapped beside milder ones.
