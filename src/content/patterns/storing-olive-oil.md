## Summary

This pattern is for anyone who keeps olive oil at home and wants it to taste good to the last drop. It describes how light, heat and air spoil oil, where to keep it, how long it lasts and how to tell when it has gone off.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Keep the oil in a dark cupboard, in its tin or dark bottle, never on a sunny windowsill. | Light drives oxidation and breaks down the pigments and aromas that make fresh oil taste green. |
| Store it away from the stove, oven and dishwasher, ideally somewhere around room temperature or a little cooler. | Heat speeds up every reaction that dulls oil, so a bottle beside the cooktop ages far faster than one in a cool pantry. |
| Close the cap straight after pouring, and decant from a big tin into a small dark bottle you refill. | Oxygen is the third enemy. Every time air gets in, and the more air there is above the oil, the faster it goes stale. |
| Buy an amount you'll finish within about two to three months of opening. | Unopened oil keeps reasonably well until its best-before date, but once open it declines steadily, and bulk tins only save money if you use them quickly. |
| Smell and taste the oil if in doubt: crayon, putty, stale nuts or old butter mean it's rancid. | Rancid oil isn't dangerous, but it gives everything you cook with it a stale, greasy taste. |

## Context

This pattern suits extra virgin olive oil and other cold-pressed oils kept for everyday cooking and finishing.

It does not suit homemade oils flavoured with fresh garlic, chilli or herbs. Fresh ingredients in oil can allow *Clostridium botulinum* to grow, so make these in small amounts, keep them in the fridge and use them within a few days (see [Infusing oil with garlic and chilli](../infusing-oil-with-garlic-and-chilli/)).

There's a trade-off between convenience and freshness. A bottle by the stove with a pourer is handy, while a sealed tin in a cupboard keeps the oil better. This pattern keeps the main supply in the dark and only a small, quickly used bottle within reach, which gives you both.

## Usage

**Choosing the spot:** a pantry or low cupboard away from the oven is ideal. Avoid the shelf above the cooktop and the bench by a window, and in a hot summer kitchen choose the coolest cupboard you have. Clear glass bottles are best wrapped in foil or kept in a box. Ceramic, stainless steel or dark glass all work for decanting; avoid reactive metals and plastic you can't seal well.

**The fridge question:** you don't need to refrigerate olive oil, and Italian cooks generally don't. In the fridge it turns cloudy and partly solid, which is harmless and clears at room temperature, but repeatedly warming and chilling a bottle lets condensation in. If your kitchen is very hot, a cool cupboard or cellar is the better compromise.

**Checking an old bottle:** pour a little into a glass, warm it in your hand and smell it. Fresh oil smells grassy and fruity. Rancid oil smells of crayons, putty, varnish or old nuts and tastes greasy with no peppery finish. A musty or vinegary smell points to a fault from production rather than storage. Either way, use it up for something unimportant or throw it out, and next time buy a smaller bottle (see [Choosing extra virgin olive oil](../choosing-extra-virgin-olive-oil/)).

**Rotating stock:** write the opening date on the label with a marker, and use the oldest bottle first. If you keep a special finishing oil, open it soon after buying rather than saving it for an occasion; it won't get any better.
