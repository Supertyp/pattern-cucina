## Summary

This pattern is for anyone shaping round pizza at home. It describes how to open a dough ball by hand so the centre is thin and even and the rim, the *cornicione* (the puffy edge), stays full of air.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with a fully proofed, room-temperature ball and flour it well on both sides. | Relaxed gluten stretches without springing back, and flour stops the soft dough sticking to your hands and the bench. |
| Press from the centre outwards with your fingertips, leaving a 1 to 2 cm border untouched. | Pressing pushes the gas from the centre into the edge, where it puffs up in the oven into a light, open rim. |
| Never use a rolling pin. | A pin flattens the whole disc evenly, squeezing out the gas so the rim bakes flat, dense and crackery. |
| Stretch the centre to about 3 mm by turning the dough and draping it over your knuckles or slapping it gently from hand to hand. | Letting gravity and gentle tension do the work spreads the dough evenly without tearing it. Neapolitan rules aim for a centre no thicker than about 2.5 mm. |

## Context

This pattern suits Neapolitan-style and other round pizzas from dough of about 58 to 68 % hydration (see [Choosing dough hydration](../dough-hydration/)), and calzone.

It does not suit tray pizza and focaccia, which are pressed out in the tray, or very thin, crisp bases such as the Roman *pizza tonda*, where a rolling pin is part of the tradition.

There's a trade-off between perfect circles and an airy crust. Pressing hard and stretching evenly gives a neat round but squeezes out the gas. Working lightly keeps the rim full but leaves the shape a little irregular. This pattern chooses the airy rim. A slightly oval pizza tastes just as good, and the shape improves with practice.

## Usage

**Getting ready:** take the ball from its container with a scraper, keeping the smooth top side intact, and drop it into a bowl or pile of flour. Turn to coat both sides. Many cooks use fine semolina (*semola rimacinata*) on the bench, which feels like tiny ball bearings and stops sticking without being absorbed, but it scorches in very hot ovens if you use too much. Shake off the excess before baking either way.

**Opening the dough:** with the smooth side up, press your fingertips into the centre and work outwards in a spiral, stopping short of the edge. Flip the disc and repeat. Then place one palm flat on the dough and gently pull the opposite side away with the other hand, turning a little each time. Once it's about 20 cm across, lift it and either drape it over the backs of your hands and rotate it, letting the weight stretch it, or slap it from palm to palm, flipping it each time. It's ready when the centre is thin enough to see the shadow of your hand through it and the rim is visibly thicker.

**Fixing problems:** if the dough keeps springing back, cover it and let it rest for 10 minutes, then try again. A small hole can be pinched closed or patched with a piece of dough from the rim. A large tear means you can fold the dough into a calzone. If the rim is too thin, you pressed all the way to the edge: next time stop a finger's width short. Work quickly once it's stretched, since a thin base left on the bench starts to stick (see [Topping pizza with restraint](../topping-pizza-with-restraint/)).
