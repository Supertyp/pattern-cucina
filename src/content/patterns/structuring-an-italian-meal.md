## Summary

This pattern is for anyone planning an Italian meal with more than one course. It describes the classic order of courses, how big each one should be, how to balance them, and why most everyday Italian meals are much simpler.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Follow the classic order: *antipasto* (starter), *primo* (pasta, risotto or soup), *secondo* (meat or fish) with a *contorno* (vegetable side), *dolce* (dessert), then *caffè*. | Each course has its own job, from opening the appetite to closing the meal, and guests know what to expect. |
| Keep every course small: about 80 to 100 g of dried pasta per person for a primo, a modest piece of meat or fish for the secondo. | The meal works by tasting several things, not by filling up on one, and large portions leave no room for what follows. |
| Balance the courses so the main ingredient, richness and cooking method don't repeat. | A creamy primo followed by a creamy secondo tires the palate, while contrast keeps each course interesting. |
| For everyday meals, serve just one or two courses, or a single complete dish (*piatto unico*). | The full sequence is for Sundays, holidays and guests; at home most Italians eat a primo or a secondo with vegetables and fruit. |

## Context

This pattern suits dinner parties, Sunday lunches, holiday meals and any occasion where you want to cook Italian in courses.

It does not suit a weeknight family dinner, where a single dish is normal in Italy too, or a buffet, where everything is out at once.

There's a trade-off between tradition and practicality. The full five-course meal is a joy to eat but a lot to cook, plate and wash up. Dropping the antipasto or serving fruit instead of a dessert keeps the rhythm with half the work. This pattern recommends three courses (for instance primo, secondo with contorno and dolce) for guests, and keeping the full sequence for special occasions.

## Usage

**Building the menu:** start with the course you care about most and build around it. If the primo is rich, such as a lasagne or ragù (see [Slow-cooking a ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/)), make the antipasto light and the secondo simple, perhaps grilled fish with a salad. If the secondo is a big braise, keep the primo light, such as a soup or a simple tomato pasta. Stay in one region or one season for a natural fit, and don't repeat key ingredients across courses. For quantities, see [Portioning pasta](../portioning-pasta/).

**Serving the courses:** courses arrive one after another, each on its own plate, not all together. Pasta is a course of its own, never a side dish to meat. The contorno comes with the secondo or just after it, and a green salad often closes the savoury part of the meal (see [Dressing a salad the Italian way](../dressing-a-salad/)). Cheese or fruit may come before the dolce. Coffee follows dessert rather than accompanying it, and a *digestivo* may follow coffee (see [Drinking coffee the Italian way](../ordering-coffee-in-italy/)). Bread stays on the table throughout.

**Pacing:** Italian dinners are leisurely, with time between courses for talking. Plan the menu so that only one course needs last-minute cooking (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)), and match the wine to the weight of each course (see [Matching wine weight to food](../matching-wine-weight-to-food/)). If guests are flagging, it's perfectly fine to skip a course.
