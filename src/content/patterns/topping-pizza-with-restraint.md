## Summary

This pattern is for anyone topping pizza at home. It describes how much to put on, how to keep watery ingredients from drowning the base, and what to add only after baking.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use a thin layer of raw crushed tomato, about 70 to 80 g for a 30 cm pizza, seasoned only with salt. | The tomato cooks on the pizza in the oven. A thin layer lets steam escape so the base underneath crisps, while pre-cooked sauce turns jammy and dull. |
| Drain fresh mozzarella for at least an hour, or use low-moisture mozzarella, and use about 80 to 100 g per pizza. | Fresh mozzarella releases a lot of whey in the oven, and a home oven bakes too slowly to evaporate it before the centre goes soggy. |
| Stick to two or three toppings, spread thinly with gaps between them. | Every topping holds water and shields the dough from heat, so a crowded pizza steams instead of baking. |
| Add delicate ingredients after baking: prosciutto, rocket, burrata, fresh basil in a home oven, and a thread of extra virgin olive oil. | High heat dries out cured meat, wilts leaves to nothing and melts burrata away, while added at the end they stay fresh and fragrant. |

## Context

This pattern suits Neapolitan-style and other thin, hand-stretched pizzas baked on a stone or steel at home (see [Baking pizza in a home oven](../baking-pizza-at-home/)).

It does not suit thick tray pizza and focaccia, whose deep, bready base can carry wetter and heavier toppings, or deep-dish styles from outside Italy.

There's a trade-off between generosity and balance. A heavily loaded pizza looks abundant, but the dough, which you spent days fermenting, ends up raw underneath and tasting of nothing. This pattern puts the crust first and treats toppings as seasoning. If you want lots of toppings, bake a thicker tray pizza instead, where the base is built to hold them.

## Usage

**Preparing the tomato:** crush good tinned whole peeled tomatoes by hand or with a fork, or pass them through a food mill, then drain off excess liquid in a sieve for a few minutes (see [Choosing fresh and tinned tomatoes](../choosing-tomatoes/)). Add salt and, if you like, a little oil. Spoon it onto the centre of the base and spread it outwards in a spiral with the back of the spoon, stopping before the rim.

**Handling cheese and wet toppings:** tear or cut fior di latte into strips or small pieces and leave it in a sieve, or on paper towel in the fridge, for an hour or more. Buffalo mozzarella is even wetter, so drain it longer or add it partway through baking. Pre-cook watery vegetables such as mushrooms, zucchini and eggplant, or slice them very thinly. For a Neapolitan margherita the basil traditionally goes on before baking, but in a home oven, with a longer bake, it often scorches, so many cooks add it afterwards.

**Working fast:** top the pizza on the peel, not on the bench, and do it in under a minute. The longer the wet toppings sit, the more the base absorbs moisture and sticks. Give the peel a little shake before launching: if the pizza doesn't slide freely, lift the edge and toss in a pinch of flour or semolina underneath. Grated hard cheese, if you use it, goes on sparingly before or after baking.
