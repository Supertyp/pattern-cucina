## Summary

This pattern is for anyone with leftover bread. It describes the main ways Italian home cooking turns stale bread into something good: toasted crumbs, soaked bread salads and bread-thickened soups.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use stale, crusty bread made from flour, water, yeast and salt, and keep it in paper or a cloth bag, not plastic. | Lean bread dries out firm rather than going gummy, so it holds its shape when soaked or toasted. Plastic traps moisture, which softens the crust and encourages mould. |
| Make *pangrattato* (toasted breadcrumbs): coarse crumbs fried in olive oil over medium heat, stirring, for 3 to 5 minutes until golden, then salted. | Toasting browns the crumbs and makes them crisp, so they add crunch and flavour to pasta and vegetables. In parts of southern Italy they have long been used on pasta in place of cheese. |
| For bread salads such as panzanella, soak the bread briefly in water and a little vinegar, squeeze it out, and dress it with tomato, onion, oil and basil. | Dry bread absorbs liquid like a sponge, and squeezing it leaves tender, flavourful crumbs that soak up the tomato juices without turning to mush. |
| For soups such as ribollita and pappa al pomodoro, layer or stir in the bread and let it soften in the hot soup. | Stale bread's starch thickens the soup and gives it body, without the slimy texture fresh bread would bring. |

## Context

This pattern suits leftover country loaves, ciabatta, baguette and focaccia, a few days old and still free of mould.

It does not suit soft, sweet or enriched sandwich bread, which goes soggy rather than crisp, or any bread with visible mould: throw the whole loaf out, because mould threads spread well beyond the spots you can see.

There's a trade-off between tradition and modern habits. Traditional Tuscan panzanella soaks the bread, giving a soft, almost couscous-like salad, while many modern versions toast the bread into croutons for crunch. This pattern explains the soaked way first because it shows what stale bread can do, but toasting is fine when you want a crisp salad, especially with bread that is only slightly stale.

## Usage

**Making crumbs:** cut off very dark crusts, tear the bread into chunks and blitz it in a food processor, or grate it on a box grater if it's rock hard. Bread that is only a day old dries well in a low oven (about 120 °C, 100 °C fan-forced) for 20 to 30 minutes. Store dry crumbs in an airtight jar for a few weeks, or freeze them. For pangrattato, heat a few tablespoons of oil, add garlic or an anchovy if you like, then the crumbs, and stir constantly. They go from golden to burnt quickly, so tip them onto a plate as soon as they smell toasty. Scatter them over pasta with greens, sardines or broccoli, or over roast vegetables.

**Soaked salads and soups:** for panzanella, soak thick slices for a minute or two, until soft but not falling apart, then squeeze firmly and crumble. Mix with ripe tomatoes, red onion, cucumber and basil, dress with oil, vinegar and salt, and rest it 30 minutes so the flavours mingle. For bread soups, use toasted slices in the bowl or stir torn bread into the pot near the end. Ribollita, made with beans and cabbage, is reheated the next day and is often better for it (see [Cooking dried beans, chickpeas and lentils](../cooking-dried-legumes/)).

**Planning for leftovers:** freeze slices of good bread on the day you buy it if you won't eat it, and toast it from frozen. Keep a bag of crusts in the freezer for crumbs. Leftover soups and salads that contain bread follow the usual rules: refrigerate within 2 hours and eat within 2 to 3 days.
