## Summary

This pattern is for anyone baking yeasted pizza, focaccia or bread. It describes how to convert between yeast types, how little yeast a long fermentation needs, and how preferments such as biga and poolish add flavour.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Convert by weight: 3 g of fresh yeast equals about 1 g of instant or active dried yeast. | Fresh yeast is about 70 % water, so it takes roughly three times the weight to supply the same number of live cells. |
| Use less yeast the longer the dough will ferment: about 1 % instant yeast for a dough baked the same day, and 0.1 to 0.5 % for one that rests 24 hours or more. | Yeast multiplies as it ferments, so a small amount given time produces the same rise with more flavour and without the boozy, over-fermented taste. |
| Mix with cool or lukewarm water, never hot, and keep yeast away from long direct contact with salt. | Yeast slows down above about 40 °C and dies at around 55 to 60 °C, and concentrated salt draws water out of the cells. |
| For extra flavour, ferment part of the flour in advance as a *biga* (stiff preferment) or *poolish* (liquid preferment). | During 12 to 16 hours of slow fermentation the preferment builds acids and aromas, and it makes the final dough stronger and easier to stretch. |

## Context

This pattern suits pizza, focaccia and bread doughs leavened with baker's yeast, and doughs that use a sourdough starter (*lievito madre*, mother dough).

It does not suit cakes and biscuits raised with baking powder, or fried doughs that need a fast, sweet, enriched rise.

There's a trade-off between speed and flavour. A dough with plenty of yeast is ready in two hours but tastes mainly of yeast and plain flour. A dough with a pinch of yeast and a day in the fridge, or a preferment, tastes deeper and digests more easily, but needs planning. This pattern favours slow fermentation (see [Cold-fermenting dough](../cold-fermenting-dough/)), but a same-day dough is fine when the alternative is no homemade pizza at all.

## Usage

**Choosing and storing yeast:** instant dried yeast mixes straight into the flour. Active dried yeast is traditionally dissolved in lukewarm water first, though modern versions work like instant. Many bakers treat the two as interchangeable, while some use about 25 % more active dried. Fresh yeast is a moist, beige block that crumbles cleanly. Keep it in the fridge and use it within about two weeks, before it goes grey and sticky. Seal opened dried yeast and keep it in the fridge or freezer.

**Measuring tiny amounts:** for long fermentations you may need less than a gram. A scale that weighs to 0.1 g helps (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)). Otherwise dissolve 1 g in 100 g of the recipe's water and add the share you need. Temperature matters as much as quantity. A warm kitchen in summer ferments the same dough twice as fast as a cool one in winter, so use less yeast or cooler water when it's hot.

**Making a preferment:** a poolish is equal weights of flour and water with a tiny pinch of yeast (about 0.1 % of its flour). It's ready after 12 to 16 hours at cool room temperature, when it's domed and covered in bubbles and just starting to sink. A biga is stiffer, about 45 to 55 % water, rubbed together into shaggy lumps. It ferments for 16 to 24 hours and smells sweet and slightly alcoholic. Use the preferment for 20 to 50 % of the recipe's flour, then mix in the remaining flour, water, salt and any yeast. Sourdough starter works in the same way, with more acidity and a slower rise.
