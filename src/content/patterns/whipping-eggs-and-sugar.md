## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making Italian desserts that rely on whipped eggs: tiramisù, zabaione, *pan di Spagna* (sponge cake), semifreddo and meringue. It describes how to beat yolks and sugar to the ribbon stage, whites to soft or stiff peaks, how to fold without losing the air, and how to handle raw eggs safely.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Beat yolks or whole eggs with sugar until pale, thick and about tripled, so a trail from the beaters sits on the surface for 2 to 3 seconds (the ribbon stage). | Dissolved sugar stiffens the bubble walls, and only a thick foam holds enough air to lighten a cake or cream. |
| Whip whites in a spotless, grease-free bowl, and add the sugar gradually once they're foamy. | A trace of fat or yolk stops the proteins forming a foam, and sugar added too early slows it down. |
| Stop at soft peaks for folding into creams and at firm, glossy peaks for meringues and sponges. | Overbeaten whites go dry and grainy, then break into lumps and leak water when folded. |
| Fold with a large spatula: stir in a third first to loosen, then cut down the middle and turn the mixture over. | Loosening the heavy base first means the rest of the foam goes in with few strokes and little lost air. |
| For raw or lightly cooked egg desserts, use pasteurised eggs or cook the yolks with the sugar over simmering water, and never serve raw egg to vulnerable people. | Raw egg dishes are a leading cause of Salmonella outbreaks in Australia, and pregnant women, young children, older people and people with weakened immunity are most at risk. |

## Context

This pattern suits egg-raised sponges, mousse-like creams, semifreddo, zabaione and meringue, and the egg base of a [mascarpone cream](../working-with-mascarpone/).

It does not suit custards thickened on the stove, such as *crema pasticcera* (pastry cream), where the eggs are cooked rather than whipped, or buttery doughs like [pasta frolla](../making-pasta-frolla/).

There's a trade-off between traditional raw-egg recipes and safety. Many family tiramisù recipes use raw yolks and whites, and the texture and flavour are prized. Cooking the yolks into a sabayon or using pasteurised eggs gives slightly less volume and a faintly cooked taste, but removes most of the risk. This pattern takes the safer side whenever anyone vulnerable is eating, and treats the raw version as a choice adults make for themselves with very fresh, clean, uncracked eggs.

## Usage

**Getting the ribbon:** eggs at room temperature foam faster and higher than cold ones. With an electric mixer, yolks and sugar take 4 to 6 minutes; whole eggs for a sponge take 8 to 10. The mixture goes from deep yellow and gritty to pale, thick and smooth, and you shouldn't feel sugar grains between your fingers. For a cooked base, whisk the yolks and sugar in a bowl over barely simmering water, without the bowl touching the water, until thick, hot and at least 70 °C on a probe thermometer, then keep whisking off the heat until cool.

**Getting the peaks right:** start whites on medium speed until foamy, then add the sugar a spoonful at a time and turn up the speed. Soft peaks flop over when you lift the whisk; firm peaks stand up with a slight curl and look glossy. If they turn cottony and dull, you've gone too far: whisk in another fresh white by hand to rescue them. Weigh your sugar (see [Measuring by weight](../measuring-by-weight/)), since too little gives a fragile foam.

**Folding and handling safely:** fold the lighter mixture into the heavier one, scraping round the bowl and lifting from the bottom, and stop as soon as no streaks remain. Use the foam straight away; it starts to deflate within minutes. Separate eggs over a small bowl rather than juggling yolks between shell halves, throw away any cracked or dirty eggs, keep raw egg desserts below 5 °C and eat them within a day.
