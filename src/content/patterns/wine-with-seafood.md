## Summary

This pattern is for anyone choosing wine for an Italian fish or seafood dish. It describes why crisp coastal whites are the safe choice, when a rosato or light red works, and why big tannic reds usually clash with fish.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Start with a crisp, dry coastal white such as Vermentino, Verdicchio, Falanghina, Fiano or Grillo. | High acidity works like a squeeze of lemon, freshening the fish, and the saline, mineral character of coastal whites echoes the sea. |
| Match the wine to the sauce and cooking method, not just to "fish". | Seafood with oil, garlic and lemon is light; fried, buttery or tomato-sauced fish is richer and can take a rounder white, a rosato or a light red. |
| Avoid big, tannic reds with delicate fish and shellfish. | Red wines can leave an unpleasant fishy, metallic aftertaste with seafood. Research links this mainly to iron in the wine, and harsh tannin makes lean fish taste worse. |
| For oily fish or seafood in tomato sauce, try a dry rosato or a light, low-tannin red served cool. | Rich fish such as tuna, sardines or mackerel, and tomato-based fish stews, have enough weight and acidity to welcome a soft red or rosato. |

## Context

This pattern suits seafood pasta and risotto, fried seafood, grilled or baked whole fish (see [Cooking whole fish](../cooking-whole-fish/)), mussels and clams (see [Cooking mussels and clams](../cooking-mussels-and-clams/)) and fish stews.

It does not suit raw seafood with strong Asian-style dressings, or fish cooked with heavy red-wine sauces, where the cooking liquid decides the match. Freshwater fish and smoked fish follow the same principles but often suit slightly fuller whites.

There's a trade-off between the safe classic and personal taste. White with fish is the reliable rule and the Italian coastal habit, but many people prefer red, and light reds can work well with the right dish. This pattern starts with a crisp white because it rarely goes wrong, and moves to rosato or a light red for oily fish, grilled fish with a smoky char, or seafood in tomato.

## Usage

**Light seafood dishes:** spaghetti with clams, linguine with prawns and lemon, steamed mussels with garlic or a simple grilled white fish want a young, crisp, unoaked white. Vermentino from Liguria, Sardinia or Tuscany, Verdicchio from the Marche, Falanghina from Campania, Soave from the Veneto and dry Sicilian whites all suit. Serve them well chilled.

**Richer seafood dishes:** fried calamari and *fritto misto* love sparkling wines, whose bubbles and acidity cut through the oil. Buttery or creamy dishes, seafood risotto and baked fish take a rounder white such as Fiano or a lightly aged Verdicchio. For tuna, swordfish, sardines or fish in tomato sauce, try a dry rosato or a cool, light red such as Frappato, Bardolino or young Sicilian Nerello Mascalese.

**Testing a red:** if you want red, take a bite of the fish and a sip. A metallic or fishy aftertaste, or a harsh, drying finish, means the wine is wrong for that dish. Switch to a white or rosato, or keep the red for a heartier course. As with any course, wine is optional; chilled sparkling water with lemon is a classic alternative.

**Thinking regionally:** coastal regions grew their whites alongside their seafood, so a wine from the same stretch of coast is a good start (see [Pairing wine by region](../pairing-by-region/)).
