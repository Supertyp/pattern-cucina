## Summary

This pattern is for anyone choosing wine for a dish where tomato leads. It describes why tomato's acidity needs a wine with plenty of acidity of its own, which Italian grapes do that well, and which styles to avoid.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Choose a wine with high acidity, at least as tart as the sauce. | Tomato is naturally acidic. Next to it, a low-acid wine tastes flat and dull, while a high-acid wine stays fresh and lively. |
| Reach first for Sangiovese (Chianti and other Tuscan reds) or Barbera. | Both have bright acidity and red-fruit flavours that echo the tomato. Barbera has softer tannin, which makes it especially forgiving. |
| Avoid soft, heavily oaked or very tannic wines with fresh tomato sauces. | Oak and ripe, soft fruit taste sweetish and flabby against the acid, and tomato's acidity makes strong tannin feel harsher and more bitter. |
| Go lighter for quick, fresh sauces and fuller for long-cooked meat and tomato sauces. | A quick sauce is bright and light, while a ragù simmered for hours is rich and rounded and can take a deeper red. |

## Context

This pattern suits tomato-based pasta sauces, pizza, eggplant parmigiana, meatballs or fish stewed in tomato, and ragù where tomato plays a strong part.

It does not suit dishes where tomato is a minor ingredient, raw tomato salads with a sharp dressing (which are hard on any wine; a crisp white or rosato copes best), or seafood in tomato sauce, which has its own considerations (see [Wine with fish and seafood](../wine-with-seafood/)).

There's a trade-off between the classic red match and lighter options. Sangiovese and Barbera are the traditional choices, but on a hot day or with a light, fresh tomato sauce a crisp white such as Vermentino or Verdicchio, or a dry rosato, can be more pleasant. This pattern starts with a medium-bodied, high-acid red because it suits the widest range of tomato dishes, and swaps to a white or rosato for light, summery ones.

## Usage

**Quick tomato sauces and pizza:** for a quick sauce with garlic and basil (see [Making a quick tomato sauce](../quick-tomato-sauce/)), pizza margherita or a simple arrabbiata, choose a light to medium red served slightly cool, such as an entry-level Chianti, Barbera, Valpolicella or Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, or a crisp white or rosato. Chilli in the sauce makes alcohol feel hotter, so keep the wine moderate in alcohol.

**Rich tomato and meat sauces:** a ragù cooked for hours (see [Slow-cooking a ragù](../slow-cooked-ragu/)), lasagne or meatballs can take a fuller wine such as Chianti Classico, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo or a southern red. Fat from the meat and cheese softens tannin here, so firmer reds work better than with a plain sauce. Keep acidity as the thing to check: a jammy, low-acid red still falls flat.

**Testing your choice:** taste a spoonful of the finished sauce, then a sip of wine. If the wine suddenly tastes thin and sour, it lacks acidity or body; if it tastes bitter and drying, it's too tannic; if it tastes dull and sweet, it's too soft or oaky. A good match keeps the wine tasting fruity and the sauce tasting brighter.

**Adjusting the dish instead:** if the wine is fixed, you can nudge the sauce. A pinch less acidity (a longer simmer, a little butter or a pinch of sugar for very sharp tinned tomatoes) helps a softer wine, and a grating of hard cheese can help a tannic one. Wine is optional, and sparkling mineral water is a perfectly good partner too.
