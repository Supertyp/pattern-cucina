## Summary

This pattern is for anyone making a mascarpone cream, the base of tiramisù and many other spoon desserts and cake fillings. It describes how to handle this very rich fresh cheese so the cream stays smooth, light and firm enough to set.

| Recommendation | Why? |
| --- | --- |
| Use mascarpone straight from the fridge and loosen it first with a spatula or a few seconds of gentle whisking. | Cold mascarpone keeps its structure, and loosening it alone stops lumps when the other ingredients go in. |
| Add the sugar or egg mixture a spoonful at a time and mix only until smooth, by hand or on low speed. | Mascarpone is about 40 % fat, and like cream it splits into grainy butterfat and liquid if it's beaten too hard or too long. |
| Keep added liquids small: a few tablespoons of liqueur or coffee at most, added slowly. | Extra liquid thins the cream and can make it separate, and the soaked biscuits already bring plenty of moisture. |
| Fold in whipped cream or whites at the end, and chill the finished dessert for at least 6 hours, ideally overnight. | Folding keeps the air in, and the fat firms up as it chills, which is what makes the layers set and slice. |

## Context

This pattern suits tiramisù and its many variations, mascarpone creams for filling sponges, a spoonful of sweetened mascarpone with fruit, and creams lightened with whipped eggs or cream.

It does not suit cooking mascarpone into a hot sauce or risotto, where it simply melts and needs no special care, or desserts set with gelatine (see [Setting a cream with gelatine](../setting-panna-cotta/)).

There's a trade-off between the traditional egg-based cream and an egg-free one. The classic cream of yolks, sugar and mascarpone lightened with whites tastes rich and silky but involves raw egg. Mascarpone with whipped cream and sugar is safer and more stable, though it tastes less of egg. This pattern supports both and asks you to follow the egg safety advice in [Whipping eggs and sugar](../whipping-eggs-and-sugar/) whenever raw egg is used.

## Usage

**Mixing without splitting:** first beat the yolks and sugar to a pale ribbon. Put the cold mascarpone in a large bowl and stir it with a spatula until it's soft and smooth. Add the yolk mixture in three or four additions, mixing gently until no streaks remain. If you use an electric mixer, keep it on low and stop as soon as it's smooth. Then fold in whipped cream or whites. A good cream is glossy, thick and smooth, and holds a soft shape on a spoon.

**Fixing problems:** if it starts to look grainy or curdled, stop at once. Catch it early and you can sometimes bring it back by gently whisking in a couple of spoonfuls of cold cream by hand; once it's fully split, use it as a filling for something where texture matters less. Grain also comes from mixing very cold mascarpone into a warm cooked sabayon, so let a cooked base cool to room temperature first. If the cream is runny, the cause is usually too much liquid, under-whipped cream or whites, or a soft brand of mascarpone. Chilling overnight firms it up; next time, use less liqueur and dip the biscuits quickly rather than soaking them.

**Buying and storing:** mascarpone varies by brand, some being thicker and some looser. Keep it in the fridge, use it by the date on the tub and within a few days of opening. Cream cheese mixed with a little cream can stand in if mascarpone is hard to find, but it's tangier and denser. A tiramisù is ideal for a dinner party because it needs making the day before (see [Timing a multi-course meal](../timing-a-multi-course-meal/)), and it keeps 2 days in the fridge.
